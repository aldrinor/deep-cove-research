# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: How Often It Happens and Why It Matters

**Horizontal gene transfer is real.** Plants and animals sometimes acquire heritable genetic material from other species without mating with them. These acquisitions are less routine than bacterial gene exchange, but their distribution is highly uneven: parasitic plants, some insects, bdelloid rotifers, plant mitochondria, and transposable elements are prominent hotspots. Published estimates range from **0.1–0.2% of genes** in surveyed obligate parasitic plants to **9.5–14.1% of homology-screened transcripts** in four *Rotaria* species—different measurements, not endpoints of one universal prevalence range. [1][2]

**Some transfers demonstrably improve performance.** Examples include bacterial-origin genes supporting wheat drought tolerance and whitefly nitrogen recycling, fungal-origin genes enabling aphid carotenoid synthesis, and microbial enzymes contributing to insect digestion. Other acquisitions become weakly expressed genomic remnants, while some reshape reproduction through genetic conflict rather than straightforward host benefit. Rarity therefore does not mean every transfer must be adaptive: acquisition supplies variation; selection, drift, and transmission determine its fate. [3][4][5]

**The review follows four questions.** What qualifies as HGT? Which plant and animal examples withstand scrutiny? What do prevalence estimates actually measure? And when does transferred DNA become an adaptation? The discussion separates transfer history, molecular function, and organismal benefit, using literature through 2026.

## What the clearest examples establish

The following cases show why the evolutionary importance of HGT cannot be judged from gene counts alone.

| Case | Donor → recipient | Transferred material | Observed endpoint | Evidence-based judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wheat CSPs [3] | Bacteria → Triticeae | Three cold-shock-protein genes | Drought response, photosynthesis, grain yield | Recipient performance tested |
| Whitefly nitrogen recycling [4] | Bacteria → *Bemisia tabaci* | *BtUCA*, *BtAtzF* | Amino-acid supply and fecundity | Environment-dependent benefit demonstrated |
| Spodoptera gasmin [6] | Viral ancestry → *S. littoralis* | *Sl gasmin* | Antibacterial phagocytosis | Cellular function supported by rescue |
| Pill-bug f element [5] | *Wolbachia* → *Armadillidium vulgare* | Large bacterial-genome insertion | Female sex determination | Major developmental consequence |
| Stick-insect pectinases [7] | Bacteria → Euphasmatodea | GH28 pectinases | Pectin degradation | Biochemical capability demonstrated |
| Dodder acquisitions [8] | Host plants → *Cuscuta* | Coding and noncoding sequences | Haustorial expression and small RNAs | Adaptive role inferred |
| Bdelloid foreign genes [2] | Diverse donors → rotifers | Numerous metabolic genes | Distinct enzyme complements | Extensive HGT; benefits vary by gene |

**Transfer and benefit are separate findings.** A gene can have a strongly supported foreign origin while its ecological role remains inferential. Conversely, an experimentally important protein can have an incompletely reconstructed donor or transfer route. Keeping those questions separate prevents both exaggerated adaptation claims and excessive dismissal of genuine transfers.

## What counts as HGT in a plant or animal?

### Nonsexual acquisition across species boundaries

**HGT changes a gene’s ancestry.** Horizontal gene transfer, also called lateral gene transfer, is the nonsexual movement of genetic information between organisms or genomes outside a parent–offspring relationship. In this review, the central events are cross-species acquisitions that become integrated and heritable in plant or animal lineages. Incoming material can add a gene, replace an existing version, or introduce regulatory and other noncoding sequences. [9]

**The recipients define the scope.** A bacterial-to-wheat transfer belongs in a review of non-microbial systems because the recipient is a multicellular plant. However, it is not an example of exchange *between two eukaryotes*. Plant-to-plant transfers, fungal-to-animal transfers, and plant-to-insect transfers address that narrower question directly. Microbial eukaryotes provide useful context, but plants and animals carry the analysis here.

| Process | How genetic material moves | Classification here |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal gene transfer | Nonsexual acquisition from another species | Central subject |
| Vertical inheritance | Parent to offspring through reproduction | Subsequent inheritance, not a new HGT |
| Introgression | Hybridization followed by ancestry exchange | Separate reproductive route |
| Endosymbiotic gene transfer | Endosymbiont or organelle to host nucleus | Related, separately identified |
| Intracellular gene transfer | Between compartments within one cell | Not itself interspecific HGT |
| Contamination | Another organism’s sequence enters a sample or assembly | Technical or sampling explanation |

**Introgression requires reproductive contact.** Introgression occurs when hybrids bridge genetic material between lineages, usually through subsequent reproduction and backcrossing. It requires sufficient overlap and compatibility for hybrids to form, survive, and reproduce. HGT does not require hybrid offspring, although distinguishing the two becomes difficult between closely related species. [10]

**Organelle ancestry needs separate treatment.** Endosymbiotic gene transfer, or EGT, describes movement from an endosymbiont or organelle into its host’s nuclear genome. Its evolutionary importance is enormous: a 2011 synthesis estimated that nearly 20% of *Arabidopsis* nuclear genes derive from the cyanobacterial ancestor of chloroplasts. That ancient contribution should not be counted as a contemporary rate of unrelated bacteria donating genes to plants. [11]

**Intracellular movement can precede HGT.** A plastid sequence entering the mitochondrion of the same plant is intracellular gene transfer, or IGT. If that mitochondrial sequence subsequently crosses into another plant species, the second movement is HGT. The Pytheas example below illustrates why a sequence’s journey may contain both processes. [12]

**Physical presence is not inheritance.** Foreign DNA in a gut, intracellular bacterium, infected tissue, or sequencing library does not automatically belong to the animal’s or plant’s chromosomes. Likewise, a host transcript moving into a parasite is molecular exchange, but becomes evolutionary HGT only if an integrated copy enters a transmissible lineage. This distinction is particularly important in the intimate associations most likely to facilitate genuine transfer. [13]

### A developing field, not a brand-new phenomenon

**The discoveries span several decades.** Stable bacterial T-DNA incorporation into plant cells was established in the 1970s, and Agrobacterium-like sequences were reported in uninfected *Nicotiana* in 1983. A *Wolbachia*-derived explanation for the pill-bug f element was proposed in 1984, decades before its genomic characterization. Thus, plant and animal HGT is relatively young as a comparative-genomics field, not something first discovered in the last few years. [1][5]

**Genome sequencing changed the scale.** Early examples were exceptional observations. Expanded genome and transcriptome sampling then revealed repeated acquisitions, shared ancestral transfers, and lineage-specific gene families. Studies of bdelloids, aphids, parasitic plants, and grasses shifted the question from whether eukaryotic HGT occurs to how extensively it contributes to particular genomes and traits. [9][14]

**The microbial comparison remains instructive.** Bacterial HGT has a much longer mechanistic and experimental tradition, including well-characterized transformation, conjugation, and virus-mediated transfer. Its role in spreading antibiotic resistance is established. Plant and animal studies more often reconstruct past events from genomes, then test selected genes for function. [15]

By 2026, prokaryotic HGT had been recognized for decades, whereas documented eukaryotic cases began more than 30 years before 2021 and became increasingly evident only with expanded genome sampling; hundreds of later reports span protists, plants, animals, and fungi. [1][16]

**The central distinction is heritability.** A convincing evolutionary case requires more than foreign material entering a multicellular organism: it requires incorporation into a lineage that can pass that material onward.

## Plant cases: contact, natural transformation, and metabolic innovation

### A comparative catalog of nuclear acquisitions

**Plant nuclear HGT has several routes.** Parasitic plants offer prolonged host contact, *Agrobacterium* supplies a dedicated transfer apparatus, and grasses contain foreign genomic blocks whose historical movement is clearer than their delivery mechanism. The table uses evolutionary branches where the evidence supports relative timing rather than a numerical age.

| Donor → recipient | Gene or cargo | Route or mechanism | Evolutionary timing | Function | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hosts, especially Fabales/Caryophyllales → [8] | Defense-associated genes; LRR kinase | Haustorial association; genomic DNA | 16–20 events ancestral within genus | Parasite–host interaction | Genomic and phylogenetic support |
| Brassicaceae → [17] | Strictosidine synthase-like genes | DNA transfer; retained introns | Independent parasite-lineage acquisitions | Developmental and wound responses | Strong origin evidence; inferred role |
| Grasses ↔ [1] | Gene encoding a 448-aa protein | Haustorial contact; RNA route proposed | Following host–parasite lineage separation | Function unassigned | Direction-sensitive interpretation |
| *Agrobacterium* → [15] | T-DNA, including *rol* genes | Secretion-mediated transfer and integration | Dated dicot panel: 0.62–24 Ma | Transformation and developmental effects | Mechanism and inheritance established |
| Bacteria → [3] | Three CSP genes | Nuclear acquisition; vector unassigned | Triticeae history; millennia of selection | Drought tolerance and photosynthesis | Functional and field experiments |
| Other grasses → [1] | *PPC* and genomic blocks | Genomic-DNA transfer | Repeated acquisitions along grass lineages | C4 metabolism and other functions | Phylogenies and genomic context |

### Cuscuta: repeated acquisition at the feeding interface

**Dodder provides unusually extensive evidence.** Yang and colleagues identified 108 transcribed, probably functional HGT events in *Cuscuta campestris* and related species, plus 42 additional host-derived regions containing transposons, pseudogenes, or noncoding sequences. Those are different categories of acquisition, not 150 demonstrated adaptive genes. Genome structure supported movement of genomic DNA rather than a universal mechanism based on reverse-transcribed messenger RNA. [8]

**The donors match ecological contact.** The earlier *C. campestris* genome study identified 64 novel high-confidence candidates associated with at least 32 donor sequences, with a majority tracing to Fabales and Caryophyllales. These orders contain plants that dodders parasitize. The 2018 and 2019 inventories overlap in subject and should not be added as independent totals. [18]

**The haustorium supplies an opportunity.** This specialized feeding organ joins parasite and host tissues, allowing exchange of water, nutrients, proteins, and nucleic acids. It therefore creates repeated exposure to host-derived material. However, the movement of RNA across the junction and the eventual integration of host DNA are distinct steps; the genomic evidence addresses the latter. [13]

**Convergence makes adaptation plausible.** Eighteen *Cuscuta* acquisitions came from gene families independently acquired by Orobanchaceae parasites, and most were highly expressed in haustorial feeding structures in both groups. Independent retention of similar cargo in similar ecological settings is more suggestive of functional filtering than a random assortment of foreign genes would be. The inference concerns parasite biology, rather than a measured increase in lifetime reproductive success for every locus. [8]

**Some cargo may affect regulation.** Many acquired *C. campestris* genes generate 24-nucleotide small RNAs associated with RNA-directed DNA methylation. One acquired leucine-rich-repeat protein kinase overlaps a microRNA implicated in host-gene regulation. These observations connect foreign sequences with regulatory systems operating at the parasite–host interface, extending their potential significance beyond imported proteins alone. [8]

**The history includes ancestral events.** Between 16 and 20 transcribed transfers were inferred to predate diversification across sampled *Cuscuta* lineages. Their distribution establishes long-term inheritance after acquisition. It does not make all present-day copies independent transfers, nor does it demonstrate that HGT caused the genus’s radiation. The strongest judgment is that repeated acquisition supplied material subsequently retained and deployed in parasite biology.

### Orobanche and Cuscuta SSL genes: strong origins, less resolved functions

**The sequence contrast is striking.** A 691-base-pair *Orobanche aegyptiaca* transcript fragment showed 88% nucleotide identity and 89% amino-acid identity to the *Arabidopsis thaliana* strictosidine synthase-like gene AT2G41300. Its amino-acid identity to the closer plant relative *Mimulus guttatus* was only 49%. The corresponding *Cuscuta australis* sequence also resembled Brassicaceae homologs much more strongly than homologs from its own closer relatives. [17]

**Independent transfers fit the phylogeny.** Both parasite sequences grouped with three *Arabidopsis* SSL genes with 100% bootstrap support in the study’s analysis. The supported donor assignment is Brassicaceae; *Arabidopsis* supplies a close sampled homolog rather than an automatically identified historical donor species. Introns in the acquired copies support DNA-mediated integration rather than transfer of an already spliced transcript. [17]

**Expression links the genes to stress.** *Orobanche* SSL expression increased approximately three- to fivefold during haustorial formation and vegetative growth. In *Cuscuta*, expression increased after wounding, reaching about 3.2-fold above the comparison after 24 hours. Two *Orobanche* codons showed probable positive selection, whereas the study detected no comparable signal for the *Cuscuta* copy. [17]

**A family name is not an assay.** “Strictosidine synthase-like” identifies sequence relatedness, not a demonstrated biochemical reaction in these parasites. Developmental expression and selected sites support functional hypotheses, but do not establish that the transferred genes perform a particular alkaloid-biosynthesis role or improve parasitic success. These are persuasive transfer cases with a more tentative adaptive interpretation.

### Striga and sorghum: separating an event from conflicting descriptions

**The named case involves two lineages.** The reported transfer connects *Striga hermonthica* and sorghum or related grasses and concerns a gene encoding a 448-amino-acid protein whose function was unassigned. A 2014 account describes a monocot host, probably *Sorghum*, donating the gene to *Striga*. [17]

**Later descriptions reverse the direction.** The 2021 plant review describes *Striga*-to-*Sorghum* movement. A 2025 review repeats that direction in its mechanistic discussion while listing sorghum or related grasses as donors to *Striga* in its case table. Accordingly, the appropriate catalog entry preserves the donor–recipient pair and the directional disagreement rather than presenting both descriptions as separate established transfers. [1][13]

**The RNA explanation is event-specific.** Differences in intron structure motivated a proposed messenger-RNA intermediate followed by reverse transcription. That interpretation should not be generalized to parasitic plants as a whole: retained introns and large host-derived blocks in other systems instead support genomic-DNA movement. The case illustrates an important distinction between evidence of interspecific acquisition and a fully resolved historical route.

### Agrobacterium: a clear mechanism without automatic host benefit

**Natural transformation crosses kingdoms directly.** *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* and *A. rhizogenes* transfer plasmid-borne T-DNA into plant cells. Physical attachment and a type IV secretion system facilitate delivery, followed by integration into plant DNA. Natural insertions in *Nicotiana* and *Linaria* demonstrate that bacterial transformation can become inherited plant-genome history rather than remaining confined to an infected tissue. [15][19]

**The cargo includes recognizable bacterial genes.** In *Nicotiana glauca*, cellular T-DNA includes *NgrolB*, *NgrolC*, *NgORF13*, *NgORF14*, and *Ngmis*. A mikimopine-producing *A. rhizogenes* lineage is the proposed donor for the natural examples reviewed by Matveeva and Otten. Transcriptionally active T-DNA also occurs in all tested cultivated sweet potatoes and sampled wild relatives. [15][13]

**The history extends over millions of years.** Shaposhnikov and Matveeva’s 2026 analysis identified 93 additional naturally transgenic dicot species. Comparisons of cellular-T-DNA inverted repeats produced approximate transfer ages of **0.62–24 million years** for 28 species. This is a dated panel-wide range, not an age assigned indiscriminately to every tobacco, toadflax, or sweet-potato insertion. [20]

**Pathogenic origin complicates adaptive stories.** The same transfer machinery underlies crown-gall or root-inducing biology. A sequence that benefits bacterial colonization need not initially benefit the plant. Long-term retention and expression show evolutionary persistence, but the consequences of particular natural insertions must be judged from their plant phenotypes rather than from their bacterial origin alone.

### Triticeae CSPs: functional evidence reaches crop performance

**Three bacterial genes entered Triticeae.** Wang and colleagues reported bacterial-origin cold-shock-protein genes in the grass tribe containing wheat, barley, and rye. Their wheat experiments connected the acquired *TaCSP* genes with drought response and photosynthetic efficiency under water deficiency. [3]

**The mechanism connects gene to physiology.** The acquired proteins regulate a type-1 metallothionein gene and enhance scavenging of reactive oxygen species, molecules that can damage cells during stress. An elite CSP haplotype from *Aegilops tauschii* increased wheat drought tolerance, photosynthetic efficiency, and grain yield when introduced into wheat. This provides a stronger adaptive argument than expression or sequence retention alone. [3]

**Modern improvement differs from ancient acquisition.** The initial bacteria-to-plant event is HGT; subsequent movement of a useful haplotype among compatible crop relatives is a separate breeding history. Likewise, reported positive selection over thousands of years describes later evolution, not the original transfer date. [21]

**The judgment is comparatively strong.** Foreign origin, a physiological mechanism, and recipient performance converge in this case. Its practical significance lies in showing that an acquired gene can become an integrated component of a crop’s stress-response network, while the magnitude of benefit remains conditional on genotype and growing conditions.

### Alloteropsis: importing useful components of a complex pathway

**Grasses exchange functional genomic blocks.** Comparative work summarized in the 2021 review identified 59 HGTs in an *Alloteropsis semialata* analysis incorporating 146 other grass species, with at least nine donor species represented. Acquisitions occurred in blocks as large as 170 kilobases. This pattern points to movement of genomic material rather than exclusively single, reverse-transcribed transcripts. [1]

**PPC connects transfer to carbon fixation.** Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase, abbreviated PPC or PEPC, contributes to the initial carbon-fixing reactions of C4 photosynthesis. Recurrent acquisition of C4-associated enzymes in *Alloteropsis* illustrates how a lineage can recruit biochemical variants that evolved elsewhere. Other acquired blocks contain genes annotated for stress responses, structural functions, disease resistance, and senescence. [13]

**A transferred enzyme is not an entire trait.** C4 photosynthesis also depends on coordinated anatomy, expression, and metabolism. HGT can supply a useful component without independently creating that complete system. Similarly, annotations across a large transferred block do not establish that every accompanying gene contributed to adaptation.

**The delivery history remains distinct.** Illegitimate pollination has been proposed for grass transfers, but genomic blocks establish the cargo more securely than the particular vector. The resulting judgment is that HGT contributes to grass metabolic evolution, not that all grass acquisitions share one demonstrated mechanism.

### Organellar transfers and a dated photoreceptor acquisition

**Plant mitochondria are a separate hotspot.** Some of the most extensive eukaryote-to-eukaryote DNA exchange occurs between plant mitochondrial genomes. These cases must be evaluated separately from nuclear protein-coding acquisitions because the recipient compartment, transferred material, and prevalence denominator differ. [9]

| Donor → recipient | Cargo | Proposed route | Timing | Function or consequence | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Algal and moss lineages → [1] | Extensive mitochondrial genomes | Whole-mitochondrion fusion | Acquisitions on recipient lineage | Expanded mitochondrial genome | Comparative organellar genomics |
| *Tetrastigma* hosts → [22] | Mitochondrial sequences, including *nad1* region | Intimate parasitic association | Before sampled descendant copies | Extensive host-derived mtDNA | Multigene phylogenetic conflict |
| *Cuscuta* → Genisteae → [12] | 5,076-bp Pytheas sequence | One IGT, then two HGTs | Ordered multistep reconstruction | Organelle sequence mosaic | Most-parsimonious historical route |
| Hornwort lineage → [13] | *neochrome* photoreceptor | Interspecific nuclear acquisition | Approximately 179 Ma | Low-light responses | Comparative phylogenomics |

**Amborella demonstrates bulk acquisition.** Its approximately 3.9-megabase mitochondrial genome contains extensive acquisitions from several algal lineages and a moss. Whole-mitochondrion fusion offers an explanation for movement on this scale. The conspicuous outcome is genome enlargement and foreign-sequence incorporation; size alone does not demonstrate an adaptive advantage. [1]

**Rafflesiaceae show substantial mitochondrial replacement.** Phylogenies of the mitochondrial *nad1B–C* region place these parasites near their *Tetrastigma* hosts, whereas other markers recover their expected evolutionary placement. Work summarized in a 2025 review estimated that host-derived sequence accounts for up to **40% of Rafflesiaceae mitochondrial DNA**. That figure concerns mtDNA, not 40% of all plant genes. [22]

**Pytheas reconstructs a longer journey.** The proposed sequence is plastid-to-mitochondrion movement within *Cuscuta*, followed by transfer to a Genisteae host and then to *Orobanche rigens*. The recovered *Orobanche* fragment is 5,076 base pairs long and contains plastid-derived sequence signatures. Its strongest sampled match was *Laburnum anagyroides*, with 100% query coverage and 98.52% identity, but that match does not itself identify the actual historical donor. [12]

**The reconstruction has a defined status.** The authors favor one IGT followed by two HGTs as the most parsimonious explanation. Sequence loss, incomplete lineage sorting, and incomplete organellar sampling affect the finer historical interpretation. The value of this case is its demonstration that one foreign fragment can pass through several genomic and ecological contexts, not evidence of a particular adaptive phenotype.

**Neochrome supplies an absolute-age example.** A comparative study of 434 transcriptomes and 40 genomes supported transfer of this chimeric photoreceptor from a bryophyte lineage to ferns approximately **179 million years ago**, substantially later than the more than 400-million-year separation of the lineages. Its association with fern low-light adaptation makes it an informative contrast to the large but less functionally resolved mitochondrial acquisitions. [1]

**Plant HGT has multiple consequences.** Intimate contact explains many opportunities, but the outcomes range from organellar mosaics to demonstrable stress-response and metabolic functions.

## Animal cases: biochemical capabilities, symbiosis, and development

### A comparative catalog of animal acquisitions

**Animal HGT is taxonomically uneven.** The strongest examples are concentrated in particular invertebrate groups and particular kinds of cargo. They include both microbial-to-animal transfers and strictly inter-eukaryotic transfers, such as fungal genes entering aphids and plant genes entering whiteflies.

| Donor → recipient | Gene or cargo | Route or mechanism | Evolutionary timing | Function | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fungi → [23] | Carotenoid synthase/cyclase and desaturase | Endosymbiotic association proposed | Before aphid–adelgid–phylloxerid divergence | Carotenoid synthesis and pigmentation | Genomic structure and phylogeny |
| Gammaproteobacteria → [7] | GH28 pectinases | Gut-microbe-associated acquisition | Approximately 60–100 Ma | Pectin digestion | Phylogeny and enzyme assays |
| Amoebozoan-related donor → [24] | GH5 cellulases | Nuclear acquisition and assimilation | Before sampled genus diversification | Cellulose degradation | Activity and lineage inheritance |
| Plants, bacteria, fungi → [16] | Detoxification, nutrient, CAZyme genes | Feeding and symbiosis-associated contexts | GH17 history predates 5–40 Ma split | Feeding and nutrient metabolism | Several direct functional tests |

**Genome-scale acquisitions differ from enzyme imports.** Rotifers, pill bugs, and moths illustrate additional outcomes and routes.

| Donor → recipient | Gene or cargo | Route or mechanism | Evolutionary timing | Function | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diverse non-animal donors → [2] | Numerous enzymes; TPS and trehalases | DNA uptake during repair proposed | Ancient shared and later acquisitions | Metabolism and stress responses | Extensive genomic support |
| *Wolbachia* → [5] | f element; bacterial-genome insertion | Germline exposure; microhomology-mediated integration | Recent relative to sequence divergence | Female sex determination | Junction, inheritance, and crosses |

### Aphids: fungal genes became animal pigment machinery

**Fungal genes enable carotenoid synthesis.** Aphids possess acquired carotenoid cyclase/synthase and desaturase genes, including the functional classes commonly called *CrtYB* and *CrtI*. Their foreign origin explains an otherwise unusual animal capability: making carotenoids rather than relying entirely on dietary or symbiont provisioning. In pea aphids, one desaturase contributes to red–green color polymorphism and susceptibility to natural enemies. [23]

**The acquisition predates several insect families.** Zhao and Nabity examined nine phylloxerid species and found carotenoid genes sharing ancestry with those of aphids and adelgids. Both protein phylogenies supported the relevant insect grouping with 100% bootstrap support. Conserved gene organization and genomic neighborhood evidence reinforced the inference of an ancestral fungal acquisition followed by vertical inheritance. [23]

**Later duplication diversified the toolkit.** The study identified nine cyclase/synthase and five desaturase genes in *Myzus persicae*, compared with three and four in *Acyrthosiphon pisum*. Phylloxerids generally retained single copies. Those differences represent post-transfer gene-family evolution, not repeated fungal donation for every present-day gene copy. [23]

**Color benefits depend on ecological context.** A pigment that changes exposure to predators or parasitoids need not have the same selective value in every habitat or season. The important evolutionary result is a new axis of phenotype variation on which natural selection can act. The proposed association between expanded carotenoid copy number and generalist feeding is more inferential than the established connection to pigmentation.

**A newer study examined energy physiology.** In green-morph pea aphids at 12 °C, a 2025 experiment reported ATP concentrations of **12.165 ± 0.42 versus 3.885 ± 0.21 micromoles per gram fresh weight** under 5,000 versus 200 lux. Calculated directly from those means, the difference is **3.13-fold**—12.165 ÷ 3.885—or approximately a **213% increase**. The paper’s accompanying “2.4-fold” and “240% increase” descriptions do not match those numerical means. [25]

**The physiological effect was temperature-dependent.** The same study reported a 46% shorter prereproductive period and a 62% longer reproductive duration under its cold, high-light comparison, with the light effects disappearing at 22 °C. These endpoints make the acquired pigment machinery physiologically interesting, but the experiment manipulated light and temperature rather than the ancestral acquisition itself. It therefore supports context-dependent performance more directly than it isolates a gene-specific HGT effect. [25]

**The strongest conclusion concerns capability.** Fungal HGT supplied carotenoid-biosynthesis machinery; subsequent duplication, expression, and ecology determined how different insect lineages used it.

### Stick and leaf insects: bacterial enzymes entered digestion

**Pectinases provide a clear biochemical example.** Shelomi and colleagues recovered 93 full-length pectinase open reading frames from six phasmatodean species. Gut extracts from all six degraded citrus pectin and polygalacturonic acid, and characterization of individual proteins demonstrated active forms. These enzymes belong to glycoside hydrolase family 28, or GH28. [7]

**The closest sampled relatives are bacterial.** Phasmatodean pectinases formed a distinct group near gammaproteobacterial homologs, including those from *Pantoea*, *Klebsiella*, and *Enterobacter*. The supported donor resolution is a bacterial lineage associated with the gut environment; the analysis does not equate a particular modern bacterial species with the historical donor. [7]

**Distribution supplies a historical bracket.** Pectinase genes occurred across sampled Euphasmatodea but were absent from the examined *Timema cristinae* transcriptome and draft genome. Mapping that pattern onto dated insect relationships placed the favored acquisition approximately **60–100 million years ago**, before the major diversification of Euphasmatodea. An older acquisition followed by loss in the *Timema* lineage remains an alternative history. [7]

**Digestion is supported more directly than diversification.** Endogenous pectinase production could reduce dependence on microbial digestion and improve access to plant cell-wall carbohydrates. However, a transfer preceding a species radiation does not establish that it caused the radiation. Moreover, some expressed copies lacked detectable activity on the tested substrates, illustrating why expression alone is a weak substitute for biochemical characterization.

### Nematodes: functional assimilation followed by ordinary evolution

**Acquired cellulases became animal functions.** Mayer and colleagues compared seven *Pristionchus* species and three related diplogastrid nematodes. All eight species with cellulase transcripts showed detectable cellulase activity; the other two showed neither transcripts nor activity. This correspondence links foreign-gene inventories with a recipient biochemical phenotype. [24]

**Different lineages recruited different donors.** *Pristionchus* GH5 cellulases were closest to sampled amoebozoan homologs, including those of *Dictyostelium discoideum* and *Polysphondylium pallidum*. *Koerneria sudhausi* possessed a distinct cellulase lineage. Separately, the pine-wood nematode *Bursaphelenchus xylophilus* acquired fungal-related GH45 cellulases. These are multiple histories, not one universal nematode transfer. [24]

**After acquisition, inheritance became vertical.** Within *Pristionchus*, the cellulase gene tree broadly tracked the species tree, supporting an ancestral acquisition followed by maintenance through several speciation events. The genes also duplicated, disappeared from some lineages, and acquired nematode-like intron structures. HGT introduced material that subsequently participated in familiar genomic processes. [24]

**Selection acted on particular copies.** Among 24 worldwide natural isolates of *P. pacificus*, *Ppa-cel-1* and *Ppa-cel-2* occurred in all isolates, whereas *Ppa-cel-3* occurred in 16. A codon-level test supported positive selection on *Ppa-cel-2* at *p* = 0.001, but not on the other two genes. This is evidence for gene-specific evolution, not universal positive selection across acquired cellulases. [24]

**The ecological implication is plausible but bounded.** Cell-wall degradation can support access to resources or parasitic interactions, depending on the nematode. Demonstrated enzyme activity establishes functional assimilation; assigning a particular feeding advantage requires connecting that activity to performance in the relevant ecological setting.

### Whiteflies: several independent acquisitions support plant feeding

**Whiteflies offer unusually diverse functional evidence.** *Bemisia tabaci* contains acquired genes associated with detoxification, nitrogen recycling, vitamin production, and carbohydrate processing. Their donor lineages include plants, bacteria, and fungi. These histories should be treated as separate acquisitions rather than evidence for a single transfer route.

| Acquired genes | Donor lineage | Biochemical role | Recipient endpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| *BtPMaT1*[13] | Plants | Malonylation of phenolic glucosides | Resistance to plant defense compounds |
| *BtUCA*, *BtAtzF*[4] | Bacteria | Urea-derived nitrogen recycling | Amino-acid supply and fecundity |
| *bioA*, *bioD*, *bioB*[26] | Bacteria | Biotin biosynthesis | Adult survival and fecundity |
| GH17 and GH152 genes [16] | Plants | β-glucan degradation | Enzyme activity on plant-associated substrates |

**Plant cargo can counter plant defenses.** The plant-derived phenolic glucoside malonyltransferase *BtPMaT1* allows whiteflies to neutralize compounds used in plant defense. This is a particularly direct answer to whether genes move between eukaryotes: a plant-origin enzyme became part of an animal’s interaction with plants. Its function is clearer than the identity of the original donor plant or the delivery vector. [13]

**Nitrogen recycling has a causal chain.** In *B. tabaci* MED, *BtUCA* and *BtAtzF* convert urea generated from uric acid into ammonia that can support amino-acid production. Silencing both genes during a switch to nitrogen-poor tobacco increased uric acid, reduced free amino acids, and lowered fecundity. Additional nitrogen improved amino-acid content and fecundity. [4]

**A related species strengthens interpretation.** *Trialeurodes vaporariorum*, which lacks homologs of these genes, showed no corresponding performance effects on the same gene-silencing plants. Together with the metabolite changes, that comparison supports a gene-specific nutritional mechanism rather than merely a nonspecific effect of the experimental plants. The advantage is especially meaningful under nitrogen limitation. [4]

**Biotin genes integrate host and symbiont metabolism.** In *B. tabaci* MEAM1, acquired *bioA*, *bioD*, and *bioB* are expressed in bacteriocytes, specialized cells housing nutritional symbionts. Their proteins complemented corresponding bacterial biotin deficiencies. Silencing the whitefly genes reduced biotin and hindered adult survival and fecundity, with partial rescue by biotin supplementation. [26]

**These are genes of the host.** The biotin example does not simply show that symbionts feed an insect. It shows acquired bacterial genes operating from the insect genome within a metabolic partnership. That distinction is essential: an adaptive change in a microbiome is not automatically HGT into its multicellular host.

**Carbohydrate enzymes reveal further diversity.** Colinet’s 2025 analysis identified 25 HGT-acquired carbohydrate-active enzymes, or CAZymes, arising from 14 inferred events: three bacterial, twelve fungal, nine plant-derived, and one with bacterial-versus-fungal ancestry bracketed. Those four gene categories sum to 25; post-transfer duplication helps explain why the gene count exceeds the event count. [16]

**One duplication has a temporal anchor.** Plant-derived *Bta06115* and *Bta06118* arose from an inferred acquisition followed by tandem duplication. Their distribution places the duplication before divergence of three *B. tabaci* cryptic species, whose split was estimated at approximately **5–40 million years ago**. That is a minimum relative-age constraint on the preceding acquisition, not evidence that transfer necessarily occurred within that interval. [16]

**Biochemical tests distinguish the copies.** *Bta06115* showed β-1,3-glucanase activity on β-1,3 and certain mixed-linkage glucans; the corresponding role of *Bta06118* rests more heavily on conserved structure and catalytic residues. Such enzymes could act on plant substrates or defenses, but substrate cleavage and increased lifetime fitness remain different endpoints. [16]

**The resulting judgment is unusually strong.** Selected whitefly acquisitions have experimental links extending from biochemical reaction through nutrient availability to survival or reproduction. Their broader inventory still contains genes with less resolved ecological roles.

### Armadillidium: bacterial DNA became a sex-determining region

**The f element changed development.** The terrestrial isopod *Armadillidium vulgare* carries a nuclear insertion derived from a feminizing *Wolbachia* lineage closely related to strain wVulC. The insertion is associated with female sex determination in a genetic background that would otherwise be male. Sequencing, a host–bacterial junction, copy-depth evidence, and genetic crosses establish a much stronger case than bacterial-looking sequence alone. [5]

**The two size estimates describe change.** The detailed reconstruction supports an initial insertion of approximately **1.5 megabases**, subsequently expanded by internal duplications and rearrangements into a region of approximately **3 megabases** containing more than 3,000 predicted genes. Thus, the commonly quoted 1.5- and 3-megabase figures can represent different stages of the insertion’s history rather than mutually exclusive measurements. [5]

**Symbiosis explains germline opportunity.** Maternally transmitted *Wolbachia* inhabits host oocytes during its life cycle. That residence places bacterial DNA near the inheritance machinery that an external gut microbe would have to reach by additional steps. Junction structure implicated microhomology-mediated recombination in nuclear integration. [5]

**A developmental effect is not simple benefit.** The f element functions as a new female sex-determining region, effectively contributing to a new W chromosome. Yet sex-ratio distortion can favor the transmission of a genetic element while generating conflict with other host genes. Its evolutionary significance is therefore clear even without treating feminization as a general improvement in host fitness.

**The locus exceeds its causal gene.** The region’s phenotypic role is well supported, while the particular feminizing gene or regulatory effect remains less resolved. The case demonstrates that extensive acquired DNA can reorganize an animal’s reproductive system, not that every retained bacterial gene within the region is functional.

### Spodoptera gasmin: strong functional evidence, reconstructed viral ancestry

**Gasmin contributes to cellular immunity.** In *Spodoptera littoralis*, *Sl gasmin* is an intron-containing genomic sequence of approximately 1,126 base pairs encoding a predicted secreted protein of 346 amino acids. Similarity to bracovirus-associated sequences supports viral ancestry, with homologs also present in *S. exigua* and *S. litura*. [6]

**Loss and rescue establish function.** Reducing gasmin expression nearly abolished haemocyte uptake of the two bacterial species tested. Supplying control plasma restored phagocytosis, supporting a secreted opsonin-like role: the protein helps mark targets for engulfment by immune cells. This is a direct functional result rather than an annotation-based claim. [6]

**The historical route is a reconstruction.** Parasitic-wasp-associated polydnaviruses deliver DNA circles that can integrate into lepidopteran tissues. A surviving recipient with germline integration provides a plausible route to inherited domestication. That mechanism supports interpretation of the gasmin history, while the original viral donor and exact event sequence require more resolution than the protein’s present cellular function.

### Bdelloid rotifers: extensive HGT without a universal adaptive explanation

**Bdelloids remain an exceptional animal group.** Their genomes contain substantial numbers of non-animal genes from bacteria, fungi, plants, and protists. Keeling’s 2024 review emphasizes that this extensive-HGT interpretation survived comprehensive reanalysis, unlike some earlier claims of high foreign-gene content in animals. Bdelloids should therefore be treated as a genuine hotspot, not a typical metazoan baseline. [27]

**Their acquisitions span deep time.** Eyres and colleagues found an ancient shared component predating the divergence of bdelloid families more than 60 million years ago, alongside numerous lineage-specific genes. Foreign genes subsequently follow vertical inheritance, duplication, and loss, just as the nematode and aphid examples do. [2]

**Trehalose-related genes illustrate functional caution.** *Adineta vaga* contains four putative trehalose-6-phosphate synthase genes and seven putative trehalase genes. The synthases affiliate with plant, fungal, and some protist proteins, whereas the trehalases fall within bacterial clades. Strong induction of synthase transcripts during desiccation supports a stress-linked hypothesis, but the demonstrated endpoint in that study was expression rather than enzyme-mediated protection or reproductive benefit. [28]

**Desiccation has two possible roles.** It could increase transfer opportunities through membrane damage and DNA repair, and it could favor retention of useful stress-response functions after acquisition. Those mechanisms are not equivalent. More foreign genes in desiccation-exposed species could reflect increased acquisition, different retention, or correlated aspects of ecology.

**The animal record is heterogeneous.** Some cases establish new biochemical capabilities, some reach performance endpoints, and others demonstrate major developmental change without straightforward host adaptation.

## How commonly do genes move between eukaryotic species?

### Broad distribution is not the same as a high transfer rate

**The overall answer is repeated but uneven.** Reviews report HGT across major eukaryotic groups that have been carefully examined, while multicellular lineages differ substantially in their retained foreign-gene content. For ordinary nuclear genes, the supported picture is a comparatively small contribution in many lineages and much larger inventories in ecological or biological hotspots. [14]

**A 2026 survey supplies a broad proxy.** Li and colleagues identified **10–237 nonredundant cross-kingdom HGT regions per species** in ten selected reference organisms. Their HGT-appearance metric associated **1,348 of 1,496 reference eukaryotes, or 90.1%**, with the inferred transfer histories. [29]

**The proxy measures candidate-associated distribution.** The 90.1% figure comes from organisms appearing in trees linked to the selected genomes’ HGT candidates; it is not 1,496 independently validated recipient histories. Moreover, the reference collection includes fungi and protozoa, the approach targets cross-kingdom rather than within-kingdom exchange, and directionality is sometimes deliberately described neutrally as HGT “involving” particular organisms. Consequently, it does not mean 90.1% of plant and animal species independently received a confirmed foreign gene.

**The units also include noncoding regions.** The survey reported 735 regions across its ten focal genomes, not 735 experimentally functional imported genes. Their copies overlapped 8,891 annotated gene-associated regions under the study’s broad overlap definition, but only 485—5.4%—were affected in coding sequence. Overlap with an intron or nearby regulatory region can be important, but it is not equivalent to acquiring an entire protein-coding gene. [29]

**Strictly inter-eukaryotic counts have different anchors.** A 2025 plant review compiled more than 600 reported plant-to-plant cases. More than 42% involved parasitic plants and hosts; over 95% of reported nonparasitic plant-to-plant events occurred within Poaceae. Those percentages describe different subsets and should not be added. They support concentration in particular settings, while also reflecting concentrated study effort. [13]

### Nuclear gene inventories vary with taxon and denominator

**Several useful estimates have explicit scope.**

| Recipient group | Reported quantity | Denominator or panel | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Obligate parasitic plants [1] | 0.1–0.2% host-derived genes | Five surveyed parasite–host systems | Taxon-specific nuclear fraction |
| *B. tabaci* MEAM1 [16] | 255 stringent candidates; ≥136 events | 15,662 predicted proteins | Estimated candidate fraction: 1.63% |
| Insects [29] | 1,410 genes; 741 transfers | 218 insect genomes | Comparative inventory |
| Plant interkingdom literature [13] | >800 bacterial-acquired genes; ~20 fungal events | Published plant studies | Reported inventory, not prevalence |
| Natural *Nicotiana* T-DNA [15] | 6/17 species; 15/42 species | Two different surveys | Assay-specific detection |

**The whitefly percentage is calculated.** The estimate is 255 ÷ 15,662 × 100 = **1.63% of predicted proteins** classified as stringent HGT candidates. The 255 genes correspond to at least 136 inferred events because one acquisition can yield multiple descendants through duplication. Independent homologs across other *B. tabaci* cryptic species strengthen genomic interpretation. [16]

**The insect inventory predates its summary.** The 1,410-gene, 741-transfer result comes from the 2022 insect comparative study discussed in the 2026 synthesis. It concerns non-metazoan donors, including microbial lineages, and should not be presented as a frequency specifically of animal-to-animal exchange. [29]

**Similar percentages can conceal disagreement.** The *Nicotiana* detections calculate to 35.3% and 35.7% of species in their respective surveys. Nevertheless, the surveys differed in coverage and target genes and gave contradictory calls for *N. debneyi* and *N. cordifolia*. Numerical agreement near 35–36% therefore does not imply an agreed species-by-species distribution. [15]

**Thresholds materially change animal counts.** Crisp and colleagues’ comparative panel used nested HGT-confidence classes. The resulting means were:

| Taxon | Class C genes/species | Class B genes/species | Class A genes/species |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| *Caenorhabditis* | 173 | 127 | 68 |
| *Drosophila* | 40 | 25 | 4 |
| Primates | 109 | 79 | 32 |

These are study-specific classifications, with class A applying the strictest filters; the columns are nested rather than additive. [30]

**Historical claims require contextual interpretation.** The same broad literature produced a proposed human inventory of 145 foreign genes, subsequently challenged by an assessment emphasizing mitochondrial and retroviral contributions instead. That disagreement prevents treating older primate candidate counts as an uncontested census of functional bacterial acquisitions. [19]

### Rotaria shows why transcript denominators matter

**The published fractions concern screened transcripts.** Eyres and colleagues classified transcripts using an HGT index that compared their best non-animal and animal protein matches. The commonly quoted 9.5–14.1% values concern transcripts meeting the homology-screening criteria, not all sequence in each genome.

| Species | Habitat | Homology-screened transcripts | Foreign fraction | All-transcript conditional floor |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| *R. magnacalcarata* | Permanent freshwater | 20,852 | 10.4% | 5.7% |
| *R. socialis* | Permanent freshwater | 22,130 | 9.5% | 5.2% |
| *R. sordida* | Desiccating moss | 29,770 | 12.8% | 7.0% |
| *R. tardigrada* | Temporary pools | 37,071 | 14.1% | 7.6% |

The conditional floors treat all retained transcripts without informative database matches as nonforeign; the study reported these alternative denominator-based values. [2]

**The ecological ordering survives that change.** The two desiccation-exposed species retain higher foreign-transcript fractions under either denominator. That consistency strengthens the association with habitat, even though the absolute percentages depend on expression, database matching, and screening thresholds. Permanently aquatic species also contain substantial foreign inventories, so desiccation is not a necessary explanation for all bdelloid HGT.

**A dated reconstruction estimates turnover.** The study inferred **12.8 gains per lineage per million years**, with a 95% highest-posterior-density interval of 9.1–16.9, versus **2.0 losses**, with an interval of 0.9–4.1. These are reconstructed foreign-gene gains and losses, not directly observed uptake events. Their time calibration used approximate substitution-rate anchors. [2]

**Large inventories can accumulate slowly.** A lineage can possess many ancient foreign genes while adding new ones relatively infrequently over millions of years. Present-day foreign-gene abundance therefore does not reveal the probability of transfer during one generation or one ecological encounter.

### Organelles and transposable elements need separate measures

**Different genomic components have different histories.**

| Component | Study scope | Quantitative result | Measurement basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plant mitochondrial DNA [22] | Rafflesiaceae examples | Up to 40% host-derived mtDNA | Fraction of mitochondrial sequence |
| Drosophila autonomous TEs [31] | Three species; 141 families | 0.035 ± 0.012 transfers/family/Myr | Mean rate ± standard error |
| Angiosperm LTR elements [1] | 40 flowering-plant species | 32 detected transfers | Comparative event inventory |
| BovB and L1 elements [32] | 759 eukaryotic genomes | BovB in 72 species; six cross-phylum L1 transfers | Distribution and extreme-event counts |

**Drosophila provides a time-normalized estimate.** Bartolomé and colleagues examined 1,436 potentially active autonomous TE insertions grouped into 141 families in *D. melanogaster*, *D. simulans*, and *D. yakuba*. Nearly one-third of autonomous families showed evidence consistent with recent horizontal transfer, and LTR retrotransposons accounted for 89% of putative cases. The estimated rate applies to those elements and species, not to ordinary nuclear genes. [31]

**Plant extrapolations are broader than observations.** The flowering-plant LTR survey detected 32 transfers among 40 angiosperm species and extrapolated hundreds of thousands of transfers across flowering plants during the last two million years. That extrapolation is a model-based expansion of the sampled pattern, not a direct count of all those events. [1]

**Element presence is not an event count.** BovB occurring in 72 of 759 genomes does not mean 72 transfers occurred. Vertical inheritance, repeated transfer, duplication, and loss all affect that distribution. Likewise, six cross-phylum L1 cases capture unusually distant exchange rather than the full within-lineage transfer history. [32]

**The prevalence judgment is compartment-specific.** HGT is repeatedly detected between eukaryotes, with strong concentration in particular lineages and mobile genomic components; a pooled percentage would obscure the biological question rather than answer it.

## How confidently can HGT be distinguished from other explanations?

### Published candidates often change category

**A large reanalysis provides a reality check.** Aguirre-Carvajal and Armijos-Jaramillo reassessed 1,170 published plant interkingdom-HGT candidates. Updated analyses retained **343, or 29.3%**, as phylogenetically consistent with HGT. Manual curation separated the candidates as follows. [33]

| Classification | Candidates | Share of 1,170 |
|---|---:|---:|
| HGT, original recipient interpretation | 230 | 19.7% |
| HGT, recipient placement shifted | 113 | 9.7% |
| Inconclusive | 358 | 30.6% |
| Putative endosymbiotic gene transfer | 13 | 1.1% |
| NoHGT | 431 | 36.8% |
| Potential contamination | 25 | 2.1% |

**These percentages answer a validation question.** The 230 strict original-recipient calls and 113 shifted-recipient calls sum to 343. Neither 19.7% nor 29.3% measures the fraction of plant genes acquired horizontally. They measure the interpretation of a preselected published candidate set.

**The 90.1% result is not contradictory.** A broad candidate-associated footprint across reference organisms and a strict retention fraction among previously proposed genes have different numerators, denominators, and thresholds. The former asks how widely particular candidate histories appear distributed; the latter asks how many historical claims survive an expanded analysis.

**Database composition changes the history.** The 2026 perspective reports that newly recovered homologs altered topologies for 188 demoted candidates. In one examined dataset, querying NCBI NR alone retained 32.2% as potential transfers, compared with 25.5% using a more comprehensive database. Additional eukaryotic relatives can turn an apparently isolated bacterial affinity into an older, broadly distributed gene family. [34]

### Integration, ancestry, and function require different evidence

**Three questions should be distinguished.**

| Claim being evaluated | Strong supporting evidence | What that evidence establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Sequence belongs to recipient genome | Host–foreign junctions, spanning reads, localization | Physical integration |
| Acquisition was horizontal | Taxon-rich phylogenies and alternative-history tests | Evolutionary origin |
| Sequence is heritable | Pedigrees or consistent lineage transmission | Access to inheritance |
| Acquisition benefits the recipient | Functional perturbation, rescue, performance endpoints | Present biological consequence |

**Contamination can mimic the whole pattern.** A fungus living inside a plant or a bacterium inside an insect naturally contributes DNA to a mixed sample. Strong integration evidence includes foreign sequence flanked by authentic recipient DNA, raw reads spanning those junctions, independent individuals, and chromosome-level localization. PCR of the foreign gene alone confirms presence in a preparation, not necessarily its chromosomal location. [13]

**Biological transport can also mislead.** Parasitic plants transport large numbers of host messenger RNAs. The *Orobanche* SSL study therefore used unattached developmental samples to help distinguish incorporated parasite genes from host transcripts present during feeding. This is a particularly instructive false-positive mechanism because the molecular movement is real, but the inferred heritable acquisition may not be. [17]

**Long reads strengthen physical interpretation.** The 2026 cross-kingdom survey reported supportive depth profiles for 60.7–100% of candidates, averaging 90.3%, across the eight focal species with the relevant third-generation data; lizard and frog were excluded from that check. Targeted *Arabidopsis* assays supported the presence of eleven regions involving twelve transporter genes. Such results reinforce assembly context, but presence and depth alone do not choose HGT over every alternative ancestry model. [29]

### Several biological processes create discordant gene trees

**Incomplete lineage sorting preserves ancestral variation.** A gene tree can differ from a species tree when gene variants present in an ancestral population survive through rapid speciation and sort differently among descendants. This process, abbreviated ILS, does not require a later transfer. It matters particularly when species split close together in time and when a reconstruction relies on one locus. [34]

**Differential loss creates apparent foreignness.** An ancient gene can remain in one plant lineage while disappearing from many others. If its surviving homologs are mainly bacterial or fungal, a sparse database can make the plant copy look newly acquired. Gene duplication followed by lineage-specific loss can similarly lead analysts to compare different ancestral copies as though they were direct counterparts. [33]

**Introgression carries linked ancestry.** Hybridization can produce an unexpectedly similar gene in another species without nonsexual HGT. Conserved ancestry across the gene and surrounding flanks can favor introgression, whereas an isolated foreign element in a different genomic context supports another route. This is a useful diagnostic tendency, not an absolute rule after millions of years of rearrangement and recombination. [35]

**EGT can resemble a new bacterial transfer.** Thirteen candidates in the plant reassessment were classified as putative EGT because their cyanobacterial affinities and nuclear locations were more parsimoniously explained through plastid ancestry. Correctly identifying an old endosymbiotic contribution preserves its evolutionary importance while changing the process being counted. [33]

**Phylogenetic artifacts add technical alternatives.** Long-branch attraction can incorrectly group rapidly evolving sequences; compositional bias can group sequences with similar nucleotide or amino-acid preferences. Alignment errors, incomplete annotations, short fragments, and chimeric assemblies can create additional conflicts. Bootstrap support measures consistency under an analysis, not immunity to an inappropriate model or missing taxa. [11]

**Small error rates create large inventories.** A 2011 methodological discussion used a hypothetical 15,000-gene genome: an artificial clustering rate of one in 1,000 yields 15 apparent foreign genes, while one in 100 yields 150. These are illustrative calculations, not measured error rates, but they explain why “dozens of candidates” alone is weak evidence in a genome-wide search. [11]

### Confidence should attach to individual claims

**One disputed claim need not invalidate another.** A raw-data reanalysis challenged an earlier claim of DNA exchange among bdelloid rotifers through likely contamination. That targets inter-rotifer exchange, not the entire body of evidence for ancient non-animal genes integrated into bdelloid genomes. [36]

**Inventories are useful but selected.** The euHGT inventory contains 9,511 metazoan candidates from literature spanning 2000–2025; a 2026 compendium reports 9,495 candidates. The compendium’s source-paper selection retained 36 of 151 identified papers. These resources organize reported claims rather than provide a random sample of animal genomes. [37][38]

**Combined evidence deserves greatest weight.** The metazoan compendium uses five confidence levels, with the strongest categories combining phylogenetic evidence with localization or functional tests. A robust interpretation likewise combines independent evidence rather than assuming one method settles integration, history, inheritance, and adaptation simultaneously. [38]

**Confidence is strongest when evidence converges.** Gene-tree conflict starts an HGT hypothesis; genomic integration, inheritance, and explicit comparison with competing histories make it convincing.

## Why stable HGT is restricted—and why hotspots exist

### The bottleneck is a sequence of requirements

**Foreign DNA must complete several transitions.** Huang’s weak-link model identifies three essentials: entry into recipient cells, integration into the recipient genome, and transmission to offspring. Long-term evolutionary persistence adds another filter: the inserted material must survive loss, silencing, and selection. Failure at any stage leaves no stable lineage-level acquisition. [39]

**Exposure is much commoner than inheritance.** Plants and animals encounter foreign DNA through food, parasites, symbionts, damaged tissues, and infection. Most such encounters remain extracellular, digestive, transient, or somatic. The rarity of successful heritable transfer therefore need not imply that contact with foreign material is rare.

**Animal germlines are often protected.** DNA entering an adult gut cell ordinarily has no route into eggs or sperm. Early developmental stages and maternally inherited intracellular symbionts offer different opportunities because they can expose cells contributing to descendants. The *Wolbachia*–pill-bug example makes this route especially concrete. [37][5]

**Plants differ developmentally from animals.** Plant germ cells generally arise later from meristematic lineages, and vegetative propagation can regenerate descendants from nonreproductive tissues. These traits provide potential routes from a somatic insertion into future generations. Nevertheless, an insertion in a mature leaf or another terminal tissue need not reach a seed or vegetative offspring. [13]

**Reproductive barriers require careful wording.** HGT bypasses the need for compatible mating and fertile hybrids; reproductive isolation does not itself prohibit nonsexual DNA entry. However, crossing that boundary does not remove physical barriers, molecular incompatibility, developmental restrictions, or the need for transmission. This is why “across reproductive barriers” defines the phenomenon without explaining away its difficulty.

### Genome defense and functional compatibility filter acquisitions

**Integration need not produce a useful gene.** Foreign sequence may lack suitable regulatory signals, be expressed in the wrong tissue, produce an incompatible protein, or interfere with existing pathways. A protein may also require partners absent from the recipient. Successful acquisition of a modular enzyme can therefore be more readily integrated than a deeply interdependent molecular system, although individual histories require direct evaluation. [40]

**Silencing changes the fate of cargo.** Plants and animals possess mechanisms that suppress mobile and foreign sequences. Plant TE silencing includes DNA methylation and small-RNA-associated pathways; host defenses and deletion processes can limit subsequent proliferation. Natural selection then acts on the organismal consequences of whatever expression or activity remains. [1]

**Mobile elements can escape those controls.** Horizontal transfer may place a transposable element into a recipient genome that does not yet suppress it effectively, allowing renewed proliferation. This can benefit the element’s transmission even when its spread imposes costs on the host. It is one reason TE transfer should not be equated with the adaptive import of an enzyme. [31]

### Ecological associations ease particular bottlenecks

**Hotspots share opportunities, not one mechanism.**

| Context | Barrier potentially eased | Named example | Mechanistic standing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haustorial contact | Repeated intercellular exposure | *Cuscuta* and hosts | Molecular exchange established |
| Intracellular symbiosis | Access near inherited cell lineages | *Wolbachia* and pill bugs | Integration and inheritance supported |
| Gut association | Repeated microbial contact | Euphasmatodean pectinases | Donor context reconstructed |
| Viral association | Delivery and genomic integration | *Spodoptera* gasmin | Historical route proposed |
| Mobile elements | Integration and renewed proliferation | BovB, L1, plant LTRs | Repeated transfer histories inferred |
| Organelle exchange | Movement of large DNA complements | *Amborella* mitochondria | Fusion model fits genomic evidence |
| Grafting or regeneration | Contact plus potential transmission | Experimental *Nicotiana* grafts | Experimental exchange demonstrated |

**Haustoria combine opportunity with sampling difficulty.** Their close host–parasite interface provides a plausible explanation for repeated plant HGT. It also makes host contamination and transported RNA particularly easy to confuse with genomic incorporation. The same ecological intimacy can therefore increase both true transfer opportunity and false-positive risk.

**Symbioses supply more than current donors.** Mealybug work showed that genes acquired from diverse bacteria can help sustain a nested nutritional symbiosis. The bacterium most important to a host today need not have supplied every acquired gene. HGT can assemble host-controlled metabolic contributions from several historical sources. [41]

**Viruses are plausible genetic carriers.** The presence of host sequences in viral material shows capture, while integration of viral DNA into host tissues shows another part of the route. Neither observation alone establishes delivery of a particular host gene into another species’ germline. The plant review describes host-RNA encapsidation by cucumber necrosis virus and host sequences in moth baculovirus populations as suggestive evidence, not complete transgenerational demonstrations. [1]

**Blood feeding can connect distant hosts.** BovB similarities involving bed bugs and snakes, and associations with intracellular parasites, support hypotheses in which ecological intermediaries bridge otherwise disconnected animals. These histories make biological sense as vector opportunities, but sequence association does not document every delivery step. [32]

**Desiccation may open a repair window.** In bdelloids, drying can cause membrane leakage and chromosome damage; repair after rehydration offers a proposed opportunity for foreign-DNA incorporation. The comparative habitat pattern is consistent with that model, while continued acquisition in permanently aquatic species shows that it is not a complete explanation for the group. [2]

**Grafting demonstrates possibility under contact.** Experiments with *Nicotiana* showed exchange at graft junctions, including movement of entire chloroplast genomes between species. Regeneration can turn an affected cell lineage into a whole plant, providing an inheritance route. These experiments establish biological capacity; they do not by themselves measure the natural contribution of graft-mediated HGT across all plant communities. [1]

**Organelle acquisition is not always hereditary.** The sea slug *Elysia chlorotica* can retain functional chloroplasts after feeding on *Vaucheria litorea*. The chloroplasts are not transmitted to its eggs in the reviewed evidence, so this physiological acquisition is not counted here as stable transgenerational HGT. It is a useful counterexample to the idea that foreign function automatically implies inherited gene transfer. [1]

**Rarity reflects compounded filters.** Successful events occur where ecological exposure, molecular entry, integration, and an inheritance route happen to align; those conditions vary greatly among lineages.

## Do transferred genes confer evolutionary benefits?

### The answer depends on which outcome is measured

**Benefits are established for selected genes.** Wheat CSPs and whitefly nutrient pathways provide particularly strong examples because functional manipulation reaches physiological or performance endpoints. Phasmatodean and nematode enzymes establish acquired biochemical capabilities, while gasmin connects a reconstructed viral-origin gene with an immune-cell function. These are different levels of evidence, not interchangeable demonstrations of increased fitness.

| Outcome | Representative examples | Strongest endpoint | Evolutionary interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beneficial recipient performance | Wheat CSPs; whitefly nutrient genes | Yield, survival, fecundity | Adaptation under tested conditions |
| Functional biochemical novelty | Pectinases; cellulases; gasmin | Enzyme or cellular activity | Capability established |
| Context-dependent ecological effect | Aphid carotenoid genes | Pigmentation and natural-enemy susceptibility | Selection can vary environmentally |
| Neutral or weakly selected retention | Many *Wolbachia* inserts | Pseudogenization and low expression | Genomic persistence without clear benefit |
| Genetic conflict and development | Pill-bug f element | Sex determination and transmission | Major change without simple host benefit |
| Deleterious potential | Disruptive insertions; selfish elements | Mechanistic and comparative evidence | Acquisition can impose costs |

### An acquired capability can be useful immediately—or later

**HGT imports pre-existing biochemical variation.** An animal need not evolve a pectin-degrading enzyme entirely from its native proteins if a microbial enzyme becomes incorporated and expressed. Similarly, a plant-associated insect can recruit a plant-origin detoxification function. This is the distinctive evolutionary opportunity: access to variants shaped in another lineage. [40]

**The recipient still has to integrate it.** An imported function becomes useful only if it is expressed appropriately, encounters the relevant substrate, and fits the recipient’s physiology. The whitefly nitrogen pathway illustrates this integration especially well because the ecological problem—nitrogen-poor feeding—connects directly to biochemical changes and fecundity.

**Later evolution can change the function.** Acquired genes can duplicate, diversify, gain regulatory elements, or participate in new pathways. Aphid carotenoid expansions and nematode cellulase turnover show that the long-term consequence of HGT includes subsequent evolution, not merely preservation of the donor’s original state. [23][24]

**Adaptation can arise after neutral establishment.** Reviews emphasize that some transfers may initially be neutral, nearly neutral, or slightly detrimental and later acquire adaptive relevance. A retained sequence can become useful when ecology changes or when recipient regulatory evolution makes its activity beneficial. This differs from assuming that the original acquisition occurred because the organism needed that function. [40]

### Neutral retention is a substantive evolutionary outcome

**Many insertions resemble genomic fossils.** Most *Wolbachia*-derived animal inserts examined in the 2017 review showed pseudogenization or very low transcription, consistent with neutral evolution or weak selective constraint. Their presence records real DNA movement even when it does not explain a conspicuous adaptation. [5]

**Expression does not settle biological relevance.** Transcription can occur without producing a useful protein, and a protein can retain biochemical activity without affecting survival or reproduction in its new host. The f-element review explicitly emphasizes these distinctions. A weakly transcribed insertion and a gene whose loss reduces fecundity should therefore receive different functional interpretations.

**Long retention is suggestive, not decisive.** Conservation of an intact, expressed gene over substantial evolutionary time strengthens a functional argument. Nevertheless, linkage, low deletion rates, and weak selection can preserve DNA without a large benefit. Retention supports a hypothesis that becomes stronger when linked to activity, phenotype, and recipient performance.

### Deleterious effects and genetic conflict matter

**Foreign functions can disrupt existing networks.** Insertion can damage a native locus, inappropriate expression can alter physiology, and an imported enzyme can create harmful imbalances between metabolites. The whitefly nitrogen-recycling study discusses the potential costs of newly acquired genes and the importance of their coordinated integration into host metabolism. [4]

**Selfish success is not host success.** A TE can spread because it copies efficiently; a sex-ratio distorter can spread through transmission advantages. Neither process requires improved organismal performance. The f element’s importance lies partly in showing that HGT can restructure sex determination through conflict among genetic interests. [5]

**Disease provides an important boundary.** *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation can harm plants through altered growth, while long-term cellular T-DNA insertions represent a selected subset of the broader transformation process. Somatic pathogenic transformation and the fitness effects of a stable inherited insertion should not be treated as the same measurement.

**Surviving genes create ascertainment bias.** Genomic studies preferentially recover acquisitions that survived long enough to be sequenced. Highly deleterious insertions may disappear rapidly; neutral insertions may degenerate beyond recognition. Consequently, the functional genes that attract detailed study are not an unbiased sample of all original DNA-entry events.

### Positive selection supports a narrower conclusion than “adaptive transfer”

**Selected sites concern subsequent evolution.** The *Ppa-cel-2* result and the two probable selected *Orobanche* SSL sites suggest that particular acquired proteins experienced adaptive sequence change. They do not independently prove that the initial insertion was advantageous, nor identify the precise ecological pressure responsible. [24][17]

**Comparative associations also have alternatives.** High foreign-gene content in desiccating rotifers could reflect acquisition opportunity as well as adaptive retention. Concentration in grasses could reflect biology as well as genomic study effort. Repeated acquisition from the same gene families in parasites is compelling convergence, but direct functional effects remain gene-specific.

**The adaptive judgment should be conditional.** HGT clearly contributes to adaptation in some plant and animal systems. The strongest cases connect foreign origin with function and recipient performance under a named ecological condition; broader claims about diversification or major transitions require more than temporal coincidence.

## Plants and animals compared with microbial HGT

**The underlying principle is shared.** In every system, DNA movement supplies variation whose eventual fate depends on compatibility, ecology, selection, and transmission. The differences concern how often material reaches a transmissible genome and how directly the full process can be studied.

| Comparison axis | Bacteria and archaea | Multicellular plants | Multicellular animals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inheritance access | Acquired DNA can enter the reproducing cell | Developmental lineage or vegetative transmission | Germline access usually decisive |
| Established routes | Transformation, conjugation, viral transfer | T-DNA; contact and organellar routes | Endosymbiont, viral, and TE-associated histories |
| Principal barriers | Entry, integration, compatibility, defense | Cell walls, compartments, lineage transmission | Tissue separation, germline access, compatibility |
| Common evidence | Mechanistic experiments and comparative genomes | Comparative genomes plus selected functional tests | Comparative genomes plus selected functional tests |
| Frequency pattern | Broad and often substantial | Concentrated in parasites, grasses, organelles, TEs | Highly uneven; bdelloids and some insects stand out |
| Evolutionary consequences | Resistance and metabolic diversification | Stress responses, metabolism, genomic mosaics | Nutrition, digestion, pigmentation, development |

**Microbial experiments resolve costs more directly.** Acar Kirit, Lagator, and Bollback’s 2020 study tested 44 successfully cloned genes with 32 replicate competitions per gene across four days. Such experiments can characterize distributions of fitness effects under controlled conditions. The comparable challenge in a plant or animal often includes development, tissue-specific expression, symbionts, and environmental variation across a much longer life cycle. [42]

**The contrast is not absolute.** Wheat field performance, whitefly survival and fecundity, and gasmin rescue experiments show that eukaryotic studies can establish causal effects. Conversely, microbial comparative-genomic claims also depend on models and sampling. The difference is the maturity and breadth of mechanistic evidence, not a division between experimentally proven microbes and purely speculative eukaryotes.

**Sampling alters apparent rarity in both directions.** A 2026 perspective contrasted 12,253 listed Bryophyta species with only 155 NCBI genomes as of 25 November 2025. Sparse relatives can generate false foreign assignments, while unsampled donors and ancient sequence divergence can conceal real transfers. Closely related transfers may be especially difficult to separate from vertical inheritance or introgression. [34][14]

**Frequency and consequence remain different questions.** A mechanism need not account for a large fraction of genes to change a lineage’s ecology. One successfully integrated function can alter nutrient use, a defensive interaction, or reproductive development. Equally, a large acquired DNA segment can consist mostly of passengers or degenerating remnants.

**The numbers describe different quantities.** Nuclear gene fractions, screened-transcript fractions, mitochondrial-DNA fractions, TE-family rates, and candidate-classification shares are kept separate here. Dates follow published clocks or branch-relative reconstructions; the whitefly 1.63% fraction and aphid 3.13-fold ATP contrast are explicit calculations. Species-level donors and absolute ages depend on densely sampled, dated phylogenies with genomic context. Comparable global nuclear, organellar, and TE rates would each require confirmed independent events divided by representative lineage-time; net deleterious effects would be decided by matched recipient fitness comparisons with appropriate functional controls.

**The evolutionary conclusion is clear.** HGT in plants and animals is neither a negligible curiosity nor a universally adaptive shortcut. It is a heterogeneous source of variation, usually filtered by formidable inheritance and compatibility barriers, but occasionally supplying a function—or a genetic conflict—with consequences far larger than the number of transferred genes would suggest.
