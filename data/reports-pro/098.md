# Crafting Techniques for Non-Alcoholic Cocktails: A 2026 Evidence Brief

Through 13 September 2026, the strongest evidence supports a function-led approach to non-alcoholic cocktails: rebuild aroma, acidity, bitterness, saline or heat, body, and carbonation separately instead of treating a non-alcoholic spirit as a one-for-one alcohol substitute. For made-to-order drinks, cold botanical extraction or hydrosols combined with tea, acid, clarification, texture, and controlled CO₂ offer the most practical sensory leverage. For ready-to-serve products, validated acidification, preservation, final-ABV testing, and pressure-compatible packaging matter more than novelty. The category is growing quickly, but evidence for finished cocktails remains less developed than evidence from beer, wine, kombucha, and fruit beverages.

## 1. Scope, Definitions, and Current Landscape

### What counts as a current non-alcoholic cocktail?

**The scope is deliberately broad.** This brief covers current through 13 September 2026, with global coverage and emphasis on both made-to-order drinks and ready-to-serve or pre-mixed products. The latest dated market example used here is Greene King’s 3 September 2026 report on 0% cocktails and low-and-no sales growth [1].

The catalog includes five overlapping product forms:

- made-to-order zero-proof cocktails built at a bar;
- pre-batched or refrigerated cocktails served within a short service window;
- ready-to-drink cocktails in cans, bottles, kegs, or other packages;
- non-alcoholic spirit serves using botanical, tea, fruit, or distilled bases;
- fermented mixers such as kombucha and jun, plus dealcoholized wine or beer bases.

This distinction matters because the same formulation behaves differently at the bar and in a package. A fresh citrus highball can depend on immediate aroma release and ice dilution, whereas an RTD version must survive oxygen, time, temperature changes, microbial exposure, and pressure loss.

### Non-alcoholic, zero-proof, 0.0%, and dealcoholized

**ABV is a measured quantity.** Alcohol by volume means millilitres of pure ethanol per 100 millilitres of beverage, measured at 20°C [2]. For a cocktail, the relevant value is the finished drink after dilution, bitters, tinctures, fermented mixers, and carbonation—not merely the declared strength of one base ingredient.

**“Non-alcoholic” is jurisdiction-specific.** The U.S. Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau uses “non-alcoholic” for finished beverages below 0.5% ABV and permits “alcohol free (0.0% ABV)” for products represented as 0.0%, while noting that its specific definitions apply to malt beverages [3]. In marketplace language, “non-alcoholic” often includes drinks made without alcohol and drinks containing trace alcohol below a local threshold.

**“Zero-proof” is a market label.** Current bars and brands use zero-proof to signal an alcohol-free style or menu category, but TTB lists “mocktail” among marketplace terms without a regulatory definition [3]. A zero-proof menu description therefore does not, by itself, establish 0.0% ABV or even a globally consistent under-0.5% threshold.

**0.0% is narrower than under 0.5%.** A review of NoLo regulation describes an analytical “zero” or “0.0% vol.” subcategory at 0.04% vol.; 0.05% vol. would round to 0.1% vol. [4]. England’s guidance uses no more than 0.05% ABV for “alcohol free,” while the United States commonly uses below 0.5% for “non-alcoholic.” A drink labeled 0.0% in one market should therefore not automatically be described as 0.0% in another without checking the measurement and legal basis.

**Dealcoholized describes a process.** Dealcoholization begins with a fermented or alcoholic beverage and removes ethanol after fermentation through thermal, membrane, vacuum, or related processing. The Kombucha Brewers International code defines dealcoholized kombucha as post-fermentation removal of naturally occurring ethanol and requires that the process be stated on the label [5]. England applies “de-alcoholised” to a product from which alcohol has been extracted when the final strength is no more than 0.5% ABV [6]. In the United States, FDA guidance requires “dealcoholized” or “alcohol-removed” identity wording for covered products and a statement that the product contains less than 0.5% ABV [7].

### The regulatory patchwork

**There is no global definition.** A 2026 comparative analysis of Australia, Finland, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, Thailand, the United Kingdom, and the United States found that NoLo thresholds vary across and within countries and that formal definitions are uncommon [8].

| Jurisdiction or framework | Relevant threshold or rule | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| United States | Non-alcoholic below 0.5% ABV; alcohol-free 0.0% ABV in TTB terminology | Finished ABV and product category must be distinguished |
| England | Alcohol free no more than 0.05%; de-alcoholised no more than 0.5%; low alcohol no more than 1.2% | Descriptors are voluntary but should not mislead |
| Canada | Non-alcoholic or alcohol-free below 0.05%; low alcohol or dealcoholized below 1.1% | “Non-alcoholic” is narrower than the U.S. threshold |
| Australia and New Zealand | Alcohol-content information required at 0.5% ABV or more; additional wording applies at 1.15% or less | Labeling begins at a different boundary from England |
| Cross-country examples | The cited review reports alcohol-free thresholds from 0.05% in the UK to 1.2% in France and Japan at 1.0% | A global product requires jurisdiction-specific review |

The European Union has an alcoholic-spirit definition rather than a harmonized zero-proof cocktail definition. Regulation (EU) 2019/787 defines a spirit drink as an alcoholic beverage generally requiring at least 15% ABV, with specified exceptions; that rule governs alcoholic legal names and does not define zero-proof cocktails [9].

**Ingredients can change the finished category.** Glass & Note identifies Seedlip Grove 42 at 1.2% ABV as widely used but not truly non-alcoholic, and contrasts Angostura bitters at 1.7% ABV with Fee Brothers Black Strap Bitters and All The Bitter’s Grapefruit Bitters at 0.0% ABV [10]. A cocktail containing a 0.0% spirit and alcoholic bitters may not have the same final status as a drink made entirely from 0.0% components. The correct operational practice is to record the ABV of every alcoholic component, calculate or assay the finished beverage, and apply the local rule.

### Current market and geographic landscape

**Demand is broadening beyond abstinence.** NIQ reports that 92% of non-alcohol buyers also purchase alcohol-containing products, indicating that moderation and occasion choice are more important than a simple quit-or-drink distinction [11]. Glass & Note cites a 43% increase in global non-alcoholic beverage sales from 2020 through 2023, while NIQ projects the alcohol-free category to exceed $1 billion by the end of 2025. These figures describe different populations and measures: one is a global sales-growth statistic, the other a category forecast.

**RTD and menu demand are converging.** Mintel reports estimated U.S. non-alcoholic beverage growth of 10% in 2024, a 98% increase in tropical beverage introductions over three years, and more than 40% growth in global sober-curious products described as “tart” [12]. The same source says 55% of people moderating alcohol seek more pre-mixed sober options. Greene King reported a 19% increase in non-alcoholic cocktail demand versus its 2024 research, with cocktails becoming the second-largest low-and-no category inside its managed-pub business [1].

## 3. Innovative Production Methods

### Bar-scale extraction, fermentation, clarification, carbonation, and texture

**No single technique replaces alcohol.** The strongest methods each solve a different problem: hydrosols and cold infusion protect aroma, clarification removes particles, fermentation supplies acids and complexity, carbonation changes lift and mouthfeel, and texture agents add weight or foam.

### Hydrosols and botanical distillation

**Hydrosols are dilute aroma waters, not essential oils.** The hydrosol review defines them as the aqueous phase produced alongside essential-oil distillation, containing usually less than 1 g/L of essential-oil traces plus water-soluble plant compounds, with a typical pH range of 3.5–6.5 [30]. Steam distillation keeps plant material in contact with steam; hydrodistillation places it directly in water. Condensation separates the oil and aqueous fractions by density.

At laboratory scale, a Clevenger or modified apparatus is common. Industrial operations favor steam systems because they process larger quantities, reduce hydrolytic degradation, and lower investment relative to prolonged boiling. A St. Louis bar report describes the first pint or so of a distillation as the most aromatic and longer collection as softer in flavor; it also gives a simple serve of approximately 1 tablespoon rose hydrosol with 6–8 ounces chilled orange juice [31].

**The sensory mechanism is selectivity.** Oxygenated and hydrophilic compounds partition into the water phase more readily than many hydrocarbon-rich oil compounds. This gives a hydrosol a softer and more integrated aroma than an essential oil, but also makes its composition variable. Distillation time, plant-to-water ratio, plant maturity, collection fraction, temperature, and storage all influence the result.

Emerging extraction is more novel but less cocktail-specific. A review reports simultaneous ultrasound-and-microwave-assisted extraction producing 172% more total phenolic compounds than traditional hydrodistillation, while also emphasizing the need for chemical characterization, safety assessment, and standardization. That result supports hydrosol process innovation, not a direct claim that a cocktail will taste better.

### Cold infusion

**Cold infusion protects volatile character.** Glass & Note reports that rosemary steeped in room-temperature water for 72 hours produced 37% more terpenes than boiling, based on GC–MS analysis in a Beverage Lab NYC R&D trial [10]. The causal explanation is straightforward: boiling accelerates extraction but also increases volatilization, thermal transformation, and loss of delicate compounds.

The method has two practical forms. A long infusion uses a covered vessel, controlled temperature, timed steep, and fine filtration. A made-to-order infusion uses a short contact time, as in Djin’s 10-minute infusion of one Green Me cocoon in 5 cL Djin Immunity. The first is more suitable for batch preparation; the second is more theatrical and flexible but less uniform.

Cold infusion does not have a documented cocktail-scale yield, throughput, or cost comparison against boiling in the supplied evidence. Its operational gate is therefore scheduling and hygiene: a 72-hour aqueous infusion must be controlled as a fresh food component, with temperature, container sanitation, filtration, and discard rules defined by the operator.

### Shrubs, kombucha, and jun

**Shrubs give the most controllable fermentation-like complexity.** Drink Balo specifies a 1:1:1 by-weight ratio of fruit, sugar, and vinegar, refrigerated for 5–7 days with daily stirring and then strained. Its 1 lb fruit, 1 lb sugar, and 1 lb vinegar batch is reported to last six months refrigerated and yield approximately 16–18 drinks at a 1 oz shrub to 5 oz sparkling-water ratio [16]. The source describes the process as cold fermentation, but the presence of vinegar and refrigeration makes it more controllable than a live kombucha culture.

**Kombucha is a living process.** BC Centre for Disease Control guidance specifies 4–5 g/L tea, 50–150 g/L sugar, and 100–200 mL/L previous SCOBY culture. The tea is steeped for at least five minutes, cooled from 60°C to 20°C within two hours and no later than six hours before inoculation, and fermented aerobically for 7–21 days, typically 10 days, with recommended pH below 4.2 but not below 2.5 [32].

The metabolic sequence explains both its appeal and its risk. Yeast converts sugars to ethanol and CO₂; acetic-acid bacteria then convert much of the ethanol into acetic, gluconic, glucuronic, and other organic acids. The resulting acid, aroma, and carbonation provide complexity that soda cannot reproduce, but ethanol production varies with the SCOBY, sugar, tea, fruit, temperature, time, vessel geometry, oxygen, and backslopping.

**Jun is faster and more volatile.** BC guidance describes jun as green tea fermented with honey: 2 teaspoons of tea in 2 L water, 250 mL honey in 2 L, fermentation for 4–7 days at 21–26°C, and an optional 1–3-day sealed second fermentation. Jun is generally more carbonated and higher in alcohol than kombucha, making it attractive for sparkling drinks but more demanding for final-ABV control.

The Food Science and Technology factorial study reports kombucha yields of 87.3–96.4%, SCOBY growth of 41.11–62.08%, turbidity of 108–421.5 NTU, glycerol of 0.25–0.52 mg/mL, and ethanol of 1.90–3.21 mg/mL across evaluated formulations [33]. A separate fermentation study found that increasing the vessel surface-area-to-volume ratio from 0.09 to 0.16 changed glucose and fructose concentrations by 5–7 times without a significant difference in acetic acid or ethanol [34]. Vessel geometry is therefore a scale-up variable, not merely a packaging detail.

### Clarification and centrifugation

**Clarification separates visual lightness from flavor intensity.** Fruit juices and purées are cloudy mainly because of suspended pectin, cellulose, and plant particles; milk washing is better suited to binding polyphenols and tannins. These are different mechanisms and should not be treated as interchangeable.

The agar method reported by Deadshot uses 2 g agar in 250 mL cool water, boils the mixture for several minutes, whisks in 750 mL fresh lime juice, sets it for 10 minutes over an ice bath, and drains it through a cheese bag. The source reports nearly 100% yield [35]. Agar is attractive for bars because the equipment is simple and the output is visually striking, but the trade article reports that agar strips more flavor than centrifugation.

Milk washing uses gradual acidification. In a documented 10 L alcoholic batch, the process combines 20 parts Angostura with 1 part lemon juice, adds an equal volume of cold milk, stirs for one minute, rests overnight, and strains through cheesecloth. Casein forms the curds and binds bitter or astringent polyphenols; whey proteins remain and add silkiness and foam. In a zero-proof system, the same mechanism can be used with tea, fruit, or botanical bases, but the dairy matrix changes the food-safety and shelf-life requirements.

**Centrifugation favors flavor retention and throughput.** A controlled pineapple, turmeric, and ginger beverage study used 15,344×g, reported as 10,000 rpm, for 15 minutes to produce a low-pulp base, followed by pasteurization at 71°C for 30 seconds [36]. The trained panel found low-pulp beverages more palatable for mouthfeel, sweetness, bitterness, pepperiness, pulpiness, and spiciness, although high-pulp samples retained more measured polyphenols.

At bar scale, the trade source describes enzyme-treated juices spun at thousands of revolutions per minute for minutes per batch. It gives an $800 Spinzall reference and reports half a dozen machines in daily use at Existing Conditions. That is strong evidence of operational adoption, but the flavor comparison remains a bartender report rather than an independent sensory panel.

### CO₂ carbonation

**Carbonation changes more than texture.** Dissolved CO₂ produces carbonic acid, increases perceived freshness and bite, and helps release volatile aromas when pressure is released. The 2019 carbonation review links dissolved CO₂, proteins, sugars, bubbles, foam stability, aroma release, mouthfeel, taste, and consumer acceptance [37].

STILLROOM specifies 0–4°C, 2–4 bar, and at least 1–2 hours under pressure for whole-batch integration [15]. Its process is:

1. Complete and filter the cocktail.
2. Chill the liquid thoroughly.
3. Transfer it to a pressure-rated vessel.
4. Apply CO₂ at 2–4 bar.
5. Hold under pressure for at least 1–2 hours.
6. Release pressure and serve with minimal agitation.

Pulp, spices, and sediment act as nucleation sites and accelerate gas loss. This is why clarified or finely filtered liquids carbonate more reliably than fruit-heavy or particulate drinks.

An ISI siphon of 0.5 or 1 L with two CO₂ chargers produces a fine, immediate bubble for short runs. A reinforced PET pressure bottle with an injection cap produces more intense, adjustable carbonation and several hours of cold storage, but requires oxygen purging and manual pressure control. The UTFPR yerba-mate–jaboticaba study applied 1.5 atm to maintain at least 1.2 atm carbonation, stored samples at 4°C for seven days, and used pressure-rated PET bottles [38].

### Thermal preservation, aseptic filling, and membrane processing

**Thermal processing trades freshness for stability.** Industry process guidance reports hot fill at 185–203°F (85–95°C), pasteurization at 140–212°F (60–100°C), and aseptic processing at 275–302°F (135–150°C) for a few seconds, followed by filling into pre-sterilized containers [39]. Reported shelf-life ranges are 7–14 days for cold fill, 2–4 months refrigerated for pasteurization, and 6–12 months ambient for hot fill or aseptic processing.

For acidified kombucha, Pennsylvania guidance recommends 180°F treatment, immediate bottling, a 30-second inversion, and another 30-second hold. At pH no higher than 4.2, the guidance describes pasteurized bottled kombucha as shelf-stable for up to two years for quality [40]. This is a process-specific guidance value, not a universal cocktail shelf life.

**Dealcoholization is an industrial analogy.** Vacuum distillation, falling-film evaporation, reverse osmosis, nanofiltration, pervaporation, osmotic distillation, and spinning-cone columns are documented for beer and wine. Representative parameters include:

- vacuum rectification at 42–48°C with reported throughput of 4–200 hL/h below 0.05% ABV for beer;
- Centritherm or spinning-cone evaporation at 35–60°C and 60–200 mbar, with 0.5–100 hL/h capacity when coupled to rectification;
- reverse osmosis at 60–80 bar;
- nanofiltration at approximately 75 bar with 1–10 nm pores;
- pervaporation reducing white wine from 13.11% to 0.5% ABV while losing approximately 70% of aroma compounds;
- spinning-cone processing near 38°C with short residence time but possible heat effects.

These data establish process families, not cocktail performance. A mixed drink contains citrus acids, sugars, salts, proteins, botanicals, and suspended solids that behave differently from beer or wine. Dealcoholization should therefore be treated as an R&D route for a defined base rather than a validated solution for finished cocktails.

## 4. Formulation Architecture and Ingredient Functions

### Alcohol is a bundle of functions

**Ethanol contributes several sensory and technical effects at once.** Glass & Note describes ethanol as a solvent for volatile aromatics, a contributor to warmth and body, and a surface-tension modifier that can aid foam integration [10]. Beer research shows that removing ethanol changes retronasal flavor, taste, mouthfeel, and aftertaste even when orthonasal aroma alone does not significantly distinguish 0% from 5% beer [41].

The formulation problem is therefore modular. Aroma, body, bitterness, astringency, trigeminal heat, dilution, and preservation need separate control.

### Base, acid, and sweetness

**The base supplies identity, not all structure.** Seedlip, Ritual, Lyre’s, Ceder’s, Tanqueray 0.0, Lumette, and Djin provide botanical or spirit-style cues, but tea, coffee, fruit, vegetables, kombucha, hydrosols, and soda can also serve as structural bases. The best choice depends on whether the target is a Martini-like drink, a tropical sour, a bitter aperitif, or a long carbonated serve.

**Acid must do more than taste sour.** Lime and lemon provide immediate citric brightness, while vinegar and shrubs add acetic depth. Verjus provides grape-derived acidity with a softer profile. Glass & Note reports a fruit-acid analysis in which pineapple was described as containing 42% citric, 38% malic, and 20% ascorbic acid, compared with lemon at 72% citric and 28% ascorbic acid. That source uses a house-pineapple shrub at 0.6 oz per 5 oz drink and reports that a 0.1 g acid addition to a 0.75 oz acid component shifted pH by 0.15 units [10].

The practical implication is that acid identity changes the time profile of the drink. Citric acid arrives quickly; malic acid can feel rounder and longer; acetic acid contributes volatile sharpness; lactic notes from milk washing or fermentation soften the impression.

**Sweetness must be managed as both flavor and substrate.** Sugar and syrups balance acid and bitterness and add viscosity. The 3:2:1 margarita provides a deliberately sweet ratio: three parts base, two parts orange syrup, and one part lime. Kombucha guidance begins with 50–150 g/L sugar, but fermentation consumes part of that sugar and changes the resulting acid, ethanol, and carbonation. A sweetener that works in a made-to-order drink may become a fermentation or spoilage driver in an RTD product.

### Bitterness, saline, minerals, botanicals, and heat

**Bitterness replaces structural counterweight.** Without ethanol’s softening and carrying effects, sweetness and fruit can become flat or cloying. Tea tinctures, grapefruit, gentian, non-alcoholic bitters, zero-proof amaro, and botanical spirits provide the bitter axis. Sexy Fish’s Sugar Snap is a direct example: jasmine tea contributes herbal, bitter, and astringent finish.

**Salt is an amplifier, not merely a garnish.** A pinch of salt in the STILLROOM highball, celery salt in Lumette Caesar, Clamato, and Worcestershire all create savory lift. Salt can suppress perceived bitterness and make fruit and acid feel fuller, but a salted rim and liquid saline produce different temporal effects. The former is concentrated at the lips; the latter is integrated throughout the drink.

**Mineral water changes mouthfeel.** Glass & Note reports calcium and magnesium differences between mineral waters and describes San Pellegrino as adding a chalkier mouthfeel than Topo Chico. This is a formulation consideration rather than a universal sensory rule: mineral salts can help thin drinks feel more substantial, but high mineral load can interfere with clarity, acid perception, or carbonation.

**Botanicals restore aroma selectively.** Hydrosols supply a dilute aqueous botanical fraction; cold infusion preserves more fresh terpene character; tinctures provide concentrated control; distillation produces repeatable aromatic water. Raffles’ Singapore Sling Zero illustrates a hybrid design using Singapore Sling Tea, a 48-hour spice tincture, orange peel, and cherry around a 0.0% spirit base.

**Heat and smoke provide trigeminal depth.** Jalapeño, ginger, Tabasco, peppercorn, burnt citrus, curry, and smoked fruit stimulate oral and nasal sensations associated with intensity. Casa Cavia’s Burnt Margarita combines burnt lemon, spicy orange marmalade, and coconut-and-curry milk. Sonesta’s Young Duchess Margarita uses spicy simple syrup with lime. These designs do not imitate ethanol chemically; they replace some of its perceptual “bite” with heat, smoke, and aromatic persistence.

### Body, foam, dilution, and carbonation

**Body needs deliberate engineering.** Glass & Note reports glycerol trials across 37 non-alcoholic bases, with 0.5–1.2% v/v most effective for mouth coating without sweetness interference. Class Barmag maps sugar or gomme syrup to body, glycerin to smooth thickness, and aquafaba to foam [28]. These are functional strategies, not evidence that one concentration works across all matrices.

Milk washing can add whey-protein silkiness and lasting foam. Casa Cavia uses flax mucilage in Fig Martini, and its White Scotch uses airy toasted-rice-and-vanilla foam. These formulations show that texture can be supplied by hydrocolloids, protein, mucilage, or a designed foam layer rather than by ethanol itself.

**Dilution is part of the recipe.** The measured 80 mL water in the STILLROOM highball is different from unmeasured ice melt in a shaken or stirred drink. Lumette, Djin, Balo, and the menu examples rely on ice, but most do not quantify final dilution. A professional program should record ice mass, contact time, drink temperature, and final volume when consistency matters.

**Carbonation supplies lift without soda dilution.** Soda and tonic are convenient, but direct CO₂ allows the formulator to carbonate the complete recipe. This is especially useful when added soda would weaken acid, sweetness, or aroma. Filtered liquids carbonate more reliably because suspended solids otherwise accelerate gas loss.

## 5. Sensory and Technical Comparison

### Aroma, flavor, mouthfeel, balance, and finish

**Aroma favors cold and selective processes.** The reported 37% terpene advantage for 72-hour cold-infused rosemary supports cold extraction for fresh botanical highballs. Hydrosols provide a second route: the aqueous fraction carries oxygenated and water-compatible aroma compounds without the intensity of neat essential oil. By contrast, dealcoholization literature reports aroma loss. In one white-wine pervaporation example, reducing 13.11% ABV wine to 0.5% was associated with approximately 70% aroma-compound loss [42].

**Flavor depends on matrix and balance.** The 2024 Beverages study compared alcoholic and NoLo gintonic, mojito, and mule samples in Spain. Its abstract reports about 600 participants; its methods report more than 650 recruited and 610 valid responses. The valid groups contained 105 traditional gintonic, 101 NoLo gintonic, 106 mojito, 99 NoLo mojito, 100 mule, and 99 NoLo mule responses. On a nine-point hedonic scale, no significant preference difference appeared between each alcoholic drink and its NoLo counterpart [43].

**Mouthfeel exposes ethanol loss.** Ramsey et al. found that orthonasal aroma alone did not significantly distinguish 0% from 5% lager, but retronasal testing distinguished flavor, taste, mouthfeel, and aftertaste in 101 beer consumers [41]. This supports measuring the drink in the mouth, not only smelling it in the glass. Glycerol, syrups, whey, aquafaba, flax, and carbonation address different parts of that sensory gap.

**Balance is usually acid-sweet-bitter-carbonated.** The direct NoLo cocktail study repeatedly associated the tested drinks with refreshing, acidic, sweet, and carbonated descriptors. In a small Indonesian Virgin Mojito study, refinement and gram-based standardization increased the overall hedonic mean from 2.83 to 3.66, with aroma reaching 4.00; the panel contained three expert assessors [44]. The result supports standardization, but the small single-venue panel does not establish a universal recipe.

**Finish requires tannin, bitterness, or spice.** Jasmine tea, grapefruit hydrosol, Seedlip Spice 94, gentian, spice tinctures, tea tannin, and saline all extend the finish. The mechanism is persistence: a drink that finishes immediately after the fruit or soda disappears can feel thin even when its initial flavor is strong.

### pH, Brix, and acidity must be read together

**pH is not a complete safety specification.** Acidified-food guidance uses equilibrium pH of 4.6 or below as a broad boundary, but acid identity, water activity, sugar, salt, temperature, oxygen, package, and handling also affect pathogen survival. A challenge-study review reports that acidulants differ substantially at the same pH and that benzoate can reduce the time needed for a five-log reduction in some acetic-acid systems [45].

**Brix is not sweetness alone.** Soluble solids include sugars, acids, minerals, and other dissolved material. A fermented beverage may lose Brix while becoming more acidic and more aromatic. A fruit cocktail at 10°Brix and a tea cocktail at 10°Brix do not have the same sensory profile.

**Titratable acidity captures buffering.** Two drinks with similar pH can require different quantities of base to neutralize because their acid systems and buffers differ. Kombucha’s acetic-acid range, a citrus drink’s citric and malic acids, and a shrub’s vinegar all produce different sensory trajectories even when the measured pH is similar.

### Direct cocktail evidence versus transferable evidence

**The evidence hierarchy is functional.** The 2024 paired NoLo cocktail study is the strongest direct evidence for consumer preference among the sources used here. The Indonesian Virgin Mojito study supplies a small but direct standardization example. The UTFPR drink, pineapple beverage, kombucha, beer, wine, hydrosol, and packaging studies provide transferable evidence for particular mechanisms, not proof of finished-cocktail equivalence.

**Beer findings explain mechanisms, not recipes.** Beer studies establish that ethanol affects retronasal aroma, mouthfeel, and aftertaste and that dealcoholization can reduce aroma and body. They do not establish that 0.7% glycerol, dextrin, or a particular hydrocolloid will reproduce the experience of a Martini, sour, or highball.

**Fermentation data explain process behavior.** The 2026 kombucha study found green-tea kombucha pH falling from 7.02 to 3.45 and titratable acidity rising from 0.04% to 0.21% over seven days, while pineapple kombucha began at pH 3.83 and ended at 3.68 with acidity rising from 0.41% to 1.21% [46]. These are useful production anchors, but they do not predict the final pH, Brix, carbonation, or acceptance of a cocktail containing the fermented liquid.

## 6. Safety, Stability, Labeling, and Service Feasibility

### Food safety and fermentation control

**Low ABV removes a preservation advantage.** Campden BRI notes that mixing liquids changes chemistry and that lower ABV reduces stability and shelf life. Non-alcoholic cocktails face additional microbiological challenges because alcohol’s antimicrobial properties are absent [47].

For acidified products, pH 4.6 is a broad U.S. process boundary for foods with water activity above 0.85 [48]. Product release requires more than that threshold. Challenge-study guidance recommends demonstrating a five-log pathogen reduction across at least four sampling times in at least two independent replicate trials, with pH measured at each time point [45].

**Fermentation requires batch-level control.** KBI recommends ethanol testing of non-alcoholic kombucha every batch, third-party laboratory confirmation at least annually, and retention-sample testing at least twice yearly [5]. BC guidance lists pH, temperature, starter volume, sugar loading, fermentation duration, refrigeration, pasteurization, preservatives, and cold shock as control points.

The Brazilian market illustrates the practical problem. A headspace-GC-FID study of twelve São Paulo brands found 11 of 12 above 0.50% ABV before legislation and 8 of 12 above 0.50% after implementation, with reported ranges of 0.40–3.56% and 0.11–2.40% ABV in the detailed results [49]. The paper reports different rounded percentages and ranges in its abstract, results, and discussion; the count comparison is the most stable result. The study attributes variation to inoculum composition, sugar and fruit, tea concentration, time, temperature, vessel geometry, and backslopping.

**Shelf life belongs to the finished matrix.** Food Standards Scotland’s 2025 guidance states that there is no universal method for setting shelf life. **Preservation changes flavor.** ABV Technology contrasts pasteurization, which offers broad microbial reduction but requires capital and can change flavor, with preservatives, whose efficacy depends on pH, composition, legal limits, and dose [51]. Campden BRI identifies preservatives, pasteurization, and freezing as the principal stability routes and notes that sorbic acid can influence flavor.

Preservative examples in the supplied guidance include 0.1% sodium benzoate plus 0.1% potassium sorbate for fermented beverages. Their use must be evaluated against pH, local law, sensory effects, and the complete formulation. Sterile filtration at or below 0.45 microns is a labeled process for kombucha under the KBI code, but it does not by itself solve every package, oxygen, or post-fill issue.

### Packaging and pressure

**The package must match the process.** Carbonated cocktails require pressure-rated PET, reinforced bottles, cans, kegs, or other closures designed for the applied pressure. UTFPR used high-pressure PET bottles for a beverage held at 4°C. Kombucha guidance identifies glass, aluminum cans, and kegs as package formats, with natural carbonation continuing after bottling until fermentation is halted.

Aseptic systems require pre-sterilized containers and a sterile filling environment. Hot fill requires packaging that withstands thermal load and vacuum formation during cooling. Cold-fill products require refrigeration, preservative protection, or both. Vacuum or modified-atmosphere packaging reduces oxygen but can create additional control requirements for anaerobic pathogens, including *Clostridium botulinum*, so oxygen reduction is not a stand-alone safety strategy.

### Labeling and final ABV

**Label the finished beverage by jurisdiction.** The following rules are the relevant working boundaries, not a single global standard.

| Market | Claim or trigger | Application to cocktails |
|---|---|---|
| United States | Below 0.5% ABV may be non-alcoholic; 0.0% may be alcohol-free in TTB terminology | Test bitters, tinctures, fermented mixers, and the diluted final drink |
| England | Alcohol free no more than 0.05%; de-alcoholised no more than 0.5%; low alcohol no more than 1.2% | State the process and maximum ABV where the descriptor requires it |

**Base ABV is not final ABV.** A Raffles cocktail made with a 0.0% spirit still contains fruit juice, tea, spice tincture, bitters, and dilution whose strengths must be assessed. Conversely, a cocktail made with a 1.2% base could finish below 0.5% after dilution, but the exact result depends on volume and any other alcoholic components. The calculation must use the actual volumes and stated ABVs; an assay is preferable when the claim is consequential.

### Workflow, equipment, scale, and cost

**Made-to-order work favors flexibility.** A bar can use a small still, infusion vessels, precision scales, pH meter, refractometer, cheese bags, siphon, centrifuge, CO₂ cylinder, and chilled glassware. The trade-off is labor: Glass & Note reports that its non-alcoholic drinks took 22% longer to prepare than spirit-based drinks because of extra filtration and chilling steps.

A short pre-batch can reduce that burden. Glass & Note describes 375 mL glass bottles at 2.2°C held for a 90-minute service window. This is a useful model for events or tasting menus where the batch size and service period are known.

**RTD work favors validation and repeatability.** Manufacturers need pH meters, refractometers, titration equipment, final-ABV testing, pasteurizers or hot-fill lines, pressure systems, aseptic filling, sterile filtration, or membrane and vacuum systems. GEA describes beverage test centers capable of comparative lab-to-pilot-scale studies, including filtration, separation, thermal treatment, extraction, filling, and packaging [52].

**Capital and ingredient prices are visible even when full COGS are not.** Seedlip lists 700 mL bottles at $31.99; that is approximately $45.70 per litre as a worked estimate: $31.99 ÷ 0.7 L. The Spinzall centrifuge reference is $800. Current menu anchors include $14++ at voco Orchard Singapore, 14 at Sonesta Kauai, $14,000 for Casa Cavia’s zero cocktails, and $18,000 for the alcoholic comparators. Zepeim recommends 85–100% of the equivalent sparkling-wine pour, 75–90% of the equivalent alcoholic wine pour, and identical pricing for a serious technique-intensive non-alcoholic cocktail.

These are price and equipment anchors, not complete cost models. A full comparison would require ingredient costs, labor minutes, yield, waste, packaging, cold-chain energy, quality testing, rent, and service volume.

| 3 | Shrub or controlled kombucha/jun + carbonation | Sparkling long drinks and culinary pairings | Moderate to strong for fermentation control; indirect for cocktails | Shrubs are the most controllable; live fermentation needs final-ABV monitoring |
| 4 | Tea, bitters, saline, spice, and texture agents | Spirit-style drinks, sours, and zero-proof classics | Moderate: direct menus and trade formulations, with some R&D data | Most transferable formulation architecture because each component has a defined function |
| 5 | Membrane, vacuum, or thermal dealcoholization | Industrial dealcoholized wine or beer bases | Strong for adjacent beverage processing, weak for cocktails | Suitable for manufacturing-scale bases, but aroma and mouthfeel restoration remain separate tasks |
| 6 | Ultrasound-microwave extraction, dextrin or glycerol aroma mimics, reconstructed microbial cultures | Experimental beverage R&D | Early or indirect | Worth testing only with matrix-specific sensory, safety, and cost measurements |

### Technique-formulation pairings

**Pair the process with the deficit it solves.** Cold infusion and hydrosols are best when the missing quality is fresh aroma. Clarification is best when suspended solids interfere with appearance, carbonation, or perceived lightness. Tea, bitters, salt, and heat are most useful when the drink lacks bitterness, finish, or trigeminal intensity.

| Formulation need | Recommended pairing | Current example or anchor | Why it works | Validation gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh botanical aroma | Cold infusion or hydrosol + citrus or tea + CO₂ | Djin’s 10-minute infusion; Raffles’ tea and spice tincture; Casa Cavia grapefruit hydrosol | Protects and layers volatile aroma while carbonation releases it | Compare aroma retention and final ABV over service time |
| Transparent fruit or citrus | Agar or centrifugation + filtered acid + CO₂ | Deadshot agar lime; 15,344×g pineapple beverage treatment | Removes particles that cloud the drink and destabilize bubbles | Measure yield, flavor loss, turbidity, and carbonation persistence |
| Fermented acid complexity | Shrub or controlled kombucha + fresh juice + sparkling water | Balo 2 oz kombucha + 1 oz juice + 0.5 oz shrub | Combines organic acids, tea or fruit aroma, and effervescence | Monitor pH, titratable acidity, ethanol, sugar, and storage |
| Bitter spirit-style finish | Tea tincture + zero-proof bitters or amaro + saline | Sexy Fish Sugar Snap; Lumette Caesar; Harry’s Phony Negroni | Bitterness and salt counter sweetness and add length | Calibrate drops or millilitres and test finished ABV |
| Weight and foam | Glycerol or syrup + aquafaba, whey, coconut, or flax | Glycerol 0.5–1.2% v/v; Casa Cavia Fig Martini and White Scotch | Viscosity and foam replace some ethanol body and persistence | Test viscosity, foam half-life, sweetness, and stability |
| RTD consistency | Acidified base + pasteurization or aseptic fill + suitable package | Reported hot-fill and aseptic process ranges; pressure-rated PET for carbonated beverages | Controls microbial risk and protects the commercial format | Complete challenge, durability, packaging, and label validation |

### What should be prototyped now?

**Bars should start with measurable, low-capital systems.** A practical first program would include one cold botanical or hydrosol highball, one clarified citrus drink, one shrub-based sparkling drink, and one texture-led spirit-style serve. Each should use a documented ratio, a measured acid component, a defined dilution method, and a recorded final temperature.

Aromatic highballs should use cold infusion or food-grade hydrosol, fine filtration, and either tonic, soda, or direct CO₂. The Djin and STILLROOM formulas provide practical anchors. Clarified drinks should use agar when simplicity and yield dominate, and centrifugation when flavor retention, throughput, and capital justify the equipment.

**Culinary menus should exploit regional ingredients.** Burma Burma’s laphet, pandan, sesame, jaggery, sugarcane, coconut, and jackfruit demonstrate that non-alcoholic drinks can express a cuisine rather than imitate a Western spirit. Four Seasons Abu Dhabi’s tea, vinegar, citrus-peel upcycling, distillation, fermentation, and clarification show how a premium hotel program can connect technique with sustainability and food pairing.

**Manufacturers should separate base development from cocktail assembly.** A dealcoholized wine or beer base can be engineered for aroma and body before it enters a cocktail, but the finished drink still needs its own acid, sweetness, dilution, carbonation, and shelf-life specification. A package designed for a still base may fail when the cocktail is carbonated or contains pulp, protein, dairy, or live culture.

### What evidence would change the recommendations?

**Head-to-head cocktail trials are the decisive gap.** The most useful study would prepare the same finished cocktail through cold infusion, hydrosol, agar clarification, centrifugation, controlled fermentation, direct carbonation, and a dealcoholized-base route. Each version would be tested for aroma, flavor, mouthfeel, balance, finish, pH, Brix, titratable acidity, carbonation, final ABV, shelf life, labor, yield, equipment burden, and cost.

A second priority is fermentation control. Kombucha and jun systems need repeated batch testing across SCOBY, sugar, tea, vessel geometry, storage, and flavoring conditions. The deciding evidence would be a validated relationship between process variables and final ethanol, acid, sensory acceptance, and package pressure.

A third priority is formulation-specific stability. The deciding evidence for a refrigerated or ambient cocktail would be a product-specific durability or challenge study using the intended package, closure, process, storage temperature, opening practice, and finished matrix. A change in any of those inputs would require a new release decision.

The evidence base is strongest for regulatory thresholds, food-safety principles, fermentation measurements, and adjacent beer or wine technology; it is weaker for direct cocktail method comparisons, cocktail-scale yield and cost, finished-cocktail ABV, and long-term stability. Many current menus provide ingredients without ratios, measured technical values, or final ABV, while the geographic record is stronger in Asia, North America, Europe, the Gulf, Latin America, and the Pacific than in Africa or explicitly Australia and New Zealand.

**The practical conclusion is clear.** Non-alcoholic cocktail craft is not a subtraction exercise. The most reliable drinks distribute alcohol’s functions across acid, sweetness, bitterness, botanicals, salt, heat, body, dilution, and carbonation. Cold extraction, clarification, controlled fermentation, and direct carbonation are the most useful current innovations for bar development; validated preservation, packaging, and final-ABV control determine whether those innovations can become reliable RTD products.
