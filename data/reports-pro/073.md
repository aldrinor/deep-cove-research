# New Paradigm of Holistic Empowerment in Elementary English Education and Teaching

## Abstract

**Holistic empowerment combines competence and influence.** Elementary English education should develop children’s language proficiency while also strengthening their capacity to think, collaborate, make supported decisions, understand others, and participate meaningfully in classroom and community life. This paper argues for a context-responsive paradigm that combines explicit language instruction with learner agency, whole-child development, multilingual inclusion, formative assessment, and mutually supportive relationships among teachers, learners, families, and communities. Its conceptual starting point is important: a language-education scoping review found that 24 of 50 studies, or 48%, did not explicitly define empowerment. The term therefore requires an operational meaning rather than use as a general expression of educational improvement. [1]

**Practical change begins with classroom design.** Research supports specific components of this approach: dialogic questioning, scaffolded joint construction, progressively independent language use, purposeful feedback, and sustained professional learning. The proposed contribution is their alignment around children’s capabilities, participation rights, and increasing responsibility. For novice teachers, the paper provides documented classroom cases, a progressive entry route, a sample instructional sequence, questioning tools, differentiation strategies, and a manageable assessment approach. Empowerment is understood as supported participation—not unrestricted choice, reduced academic expectations, or replacement of teachers by technology.

**Keywords:** holistic empowerment; elementary English education; primary English; elementary ELT; primary EFL; learner agency; student agency; co-agency; learner autonomy; whole-child education; critical pedagogy; multilingual pedagogies; translanguaging; dialogic teaching; formative assessment; social-emotional learning; project-based learning; digital literacy; AI-assisted language learning; family–school partnership; novice-teacher mentoring.

## Introduction: What needs to change

**The central shift concerns educational relationships.** The question is not whether elementary English classrooms should teach vocabulary, phonics, grammar, reading, writing, listening, and speaking. They should. The question is whether children merely reproduce teacher-selected language or increasingly use that language to interpret experience, express ideas, influence decisions, and act responsibly with others.

**The argument follows four practical questions.** The paper first defines empowerment and examines its theoretical foundations, then explains its classroom mechanisms. It subsequently provides a novice-teacher pathway and shows how assessment, ethical safeguards, and school support determine whether the approach works equitably.

**The comparison identifies what changes.** OECD’s Learning Compass contrasts knowledge delivery and evaluation with a model that also includes co-construction: teachers and students contribute to the development of learning. Explicit instruction remains part of this relationship. [2] The following comparison describes orientations, not fixed categories into which every teacher or school must fit.

| Dimension | Transmission-oriented emphasis | Holistic empowerment emphasis | What remains essential |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Coverage and correct reproduction | Language learning and whole-child development | Clear language outcomes |
| Authority | Teacher controls most decisions | Supported learner influence and shared responsibility | Adult safeguarding responsibility |
| Interaction | Questions mainly check answers | Dialogue develops and tests ideas | Accurate models and useful feedback |
| Tasks | Rehearsal and textbook completion | Rehearsal connected to purposeful communication | Adequate practice |
| Language resources | English treated as the sole resource | Multilingual repertoires support English development | Observable English learning |
| Assessment | Predominantly end-point judgments | Embedded checks, performance, and reflection | Fair, interpretable evidence |
| Support and tools | Individual delivery and resource use | Collaborative teaching and bounded tool use | Feasibility, access, and privacy |

### Classroom and school conditions

**Elementary English includes different learning environments.** English may be a foreign-language subject with limited weekly exposure, an additional language needed to access the wider curriculum, or the language of literacy instruction in an English-dominant setting. These differences affect how much language input, independent reading, family assistance, and spontaneous interaction teachers can reasonably expect. ACTFL’s emphasis on communicative purpose helps maintain common goals without assuming identical learning conditions. [3]

**Developmental support must accompany academic challenge.** Whole-child education recognizes connections among social, emotional, cognitive, and academic development and children’s physical and mental health. Its practical implications include welcoming relationships, rich learning experiences, integrated support, teacher preparation, and supportive policy—not simply adding enjoyable activities to existing lessons. [4] English teachers contribute through accessible instruction and relationships while working with appropriate colleagues when children require specialist support.

**Opportunity is unevenly distributed before instruction.** Chang and Wang’s study involved 42 teachers and 404 pupils from 90 urban and rural primary schools across 13 regions of Hunan, China. It described improvements in provision alongside differences in resources, teacher conditions, learning experiences, and curriculum implementation. [5] Consequently, apparent learner independence may partly reflect prior tutoring, home resources, or familiarity with classroom expectations. Empowerment must expand opportunities for children who begin with fewer advantages.

### Purpose and research questions

**The thesis connects instruction with participation.** Holistic empowerment is best understood as an organizing orientation for elementary English education: rigorous language development should increase children’s capabilities and their legitimate influence over learning, rather than operate separately from their identities, relationships, and circumstances.

The paper addresses four research questions:

- **Conceptual definition:** How should holistic empowerment be defined for elementary English, and how does it differ from proficiency, agency, autonomy, and self-regulation?
- **Classroom enactment:** Which planning routines, interactions, scaffolds, and professional supports make empowerment observable?
- **Assessment and ethics:** How should student learning, teacher practice, implementation, workload, and ethical participation be evaluated?
- **Contextual adaptation:** What should change across linguistic, rural–urban, resource, and school-policy settings?

**The paper offers a conceptual synthesis.** Its evidentiary emphasis is peer-reviewed elementary English research and relevant reviews published from 2015 through 13 September 2026, complemented by authoritative frameworks and older foundational assessment guidance. Direct primary-English studies inform classroom judgments; broader K–12, general-primary, preschool, and teacher-learning evidence informs appropriately identified adaptations. This is not a report of a newly conducted intervention: the lesson designs and teacher-language examples below are proposals.

## Literature review and conceptual foundations

### What the research contributes

**Different evidence strands answer different questions.** Frameworks clarify educational purposes, reviews identify recurring patterns, comparative studies strengthen outcome judgments, and qualitative classroom studies explain participation and implementation. These contributions should be connected without treating them as interchangeable evidence.

| Evidence strand | Corpus and period | Main contribution | Relevance to this paper |
|---|---|---|---|

| EFL formative assessment | 40 studies; 2019–October 2023 | Practices, beliefs, and barriers | Informs feedback and implementation |

| School-based SEL | 12 meta-analyses; PreK–12 | Developmental and academic outcomes | Supports relationship-centered teaching |

The respective sources are the empowerment scoping review [1], Liu, Abdullah, and Dong’s autonomy review [6], the formative-assessment review [7], Li and Wilson’s AI-writing review [8], and Greenberg’s SEL synthesis [9].

**The specific gap concerns integration.** Existing scholarship provides substantial work on language learning, autonomy, dialogue, inclusion, and development, but these strands do not automatically establish what an integrated elementary-English empowerment approach means or how its outcomes should be judged. Liu, Abdullah, and Dong show that autonomy assessment remains concentrated in university populations, formal settings, and established questionnaire frameworks, with comparatively little attention to digital competence and critical thinking. [6] The contribution here is therefore conceptual and practical alignment, not the announcement of a newly validated educational package.

### Defining empowerment and neighboring concepts

**Empowerment includes access and legitimate influence.** In this paper, holistic empowerment means a relational educational process through which children develop English-language capabilities, gain equitable access to participation, and exercise meaningful, age-appropriate influence over goals, methods, reflection, and collective action. This working definition joins whole-child development with OECD agency and the empowerment review’s emphasis on identity and power. [4][2][1]

**Related concepts illuminate different educational problems.** A child might regulate effort efficiently while having little influence over the task, or express an opinion that adults never consider. Neither circumstance should automatically be described as empowerment.

| Concept | Working meaning | Elementary English example |
|---|---|---|
| Empowerment | Capability, opportunity, and legitimate influence | Children help shape a task and access its language demands |
| Agency | Purposeful action that influences learning | A pupil requests rehearsal before presenting |
| Autonomy | Increasing control over learning | A pupil selects and evaluates a useful strategy |
| Voice | Expression of perspectives and preferences | Children explain which classroom texts represent them |
| Choice | Selection among alternatives | Pupils choose an audience or presentation format |
| Self-regulation | Planning, monitoring, and adjusting task performance | A pupil checks a draft against a language goal |
| Self-directed learning | Broader direction of a learning pathway | A pupil initiates a continuing reading interest |

**Agency is relational rather than solitary.** OECD defines co-agency through mutually supportive relationships among learners, teachers, parents, and communities. It also distinguishes agency from voice, choice, and autonomy and treats agency as learnable rather than a fixed personality trait. [2] Thus, accepting help, negotiating a role, or asking for clarification can demonstrate agency; independence is not synonymous with refusing assistance.

### How the theoretical perspectives connect

**Whole-child education establishes the educational purpose.** It asks teachers to recognize that academic participation depends on relationships, well-being, opportunity, and support. In English teaching, this means maintaining language goals while examining whether fear, inaccessible materials, exclusion, or unmet needs prevent children from engaging with them. The approach changes the conditions of learning as well as its intended outcomes. [4]

**Sociocultural theory explains supported participation.** Learning develops through interaction with people and cultural resources. Scaffolding means providing support that makes a demanding task accessible and then adjusting that support as learners become more capable. De Oliveira, Jones, and Smith’s first-grade classroom study examined interactional scaffolding during detailed reading, text deconstruction, and joint construction with emergent-to-advanced bilingual learners. [10] The important mechanism is not assistance alone, but assistance that enables children to undertake increasingly consequential parts of the task.

**Self-regulation makes responsibility teachable.** Goal setting becomes educationally useful when children also identify actions, monitor progress, and revise strategies. In Ponomarioviene and colleagues’ 2025 Lithuanian action research, 108 fourth graders used journals containing one monthly goal and four weekly goals. This was general-primary rather than English-specific evidence, but it demonstrates a concrete structure for making agency visible. [11] A novice English teacher can adapt the principle more modestly: “Today I will ask for clarification when I do not understand.”

**Critical pedagogy asks whose knowledge counts.** Empowerment also concerns the distribution of authority: whose experiences enter the curriculum, whose accent is respected, who receives demanding questions, and whose suggestions affect classroom decisions. Marmeleira and Cruz’s Portuguese first-cycle English action research connects critical pedagogy with autonomy, collaboration, motivation, and active learning. [12] This perspective retains language accuracy while resisting the assumption that children’s existing languages or identities are obstacles to overcome.

**Social-emotional learning supports participation and judgment.** CASEL organizes SEL into self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. Greenberg’s synthesis of 12 meta-analyses describes consistent benefits of tested school-based SEL programs across social, emotional, behavioral, and academic outcomes, with stronger implementation associated with stronger effects in two meta-analyses. [9] For English teachers, the mechanism is practical: children need ways to manage frustration, listen, disagree, repair misunderstandings, and persist during demanding communication.

**Structure should enable meaningful influence.** Taken together, these perspectives support a teacher who explicitly teaches language, organizes accessible participation, and gradually transfers consequential responsibility to learners.

## What makes the paradigm holistic and new

### Breadth without loss of language focus

**English remains the organizing academic discipline.** ACTFL identifies four language skills and three communicative modes: interpretive communication involves understanding messages; interpersonal communication involves exchanging meaning; presentational communication involves creating messages for an audience. [3] A holistic lesson should therefore identify what English children will understand or produce, not merely its theme or activity.

**The dimensions should remain distinguishable.** The following mapping is a proposed planning aid. It connects developmental purposes with classroom evidence without assuming that one activity develops every dimension equally.

| Dimension | English-learning connection | Practical enactment | Observable evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Language competence | Understand and produce purposeful messages | Read, discuss, draft, and revise | Comprehension and language performance |
| Cognitive development | Compare, infer, justify, and solve problems | Evaluate alternative solutions | Reasons and use of evidence |
| Communication and collaboration | Negotiate meaning with others | Information gaps and shared tasks | Clarification and reciprocal contributions |
| Social-emotional learning | Manage communication and relationships | Rehearse disagreement and repair | Listening, help-seeking, and repair |
| Agency and autonomy | Influence and regulate learning | Set goals and evaluate strategies | Decisions followed by reflection |
| Values and intercultural understanding | Interpret perspectives and consequences | Compare stories and cultural practices | Respectful, evidence-based interpretation |
| Inclusion and equity | Access demanding language learning | Flexible scaffolds and participation routes | Participation across learner groups |
| Digital and media literacy | Evaluate and create mediated messages | Compare sources and check claims | Explanation of credibility and privacy |
| Family and community connection | Use language across meaningful settings | Shared reading and local inquiry | Reciprocal contributions and authentic audiences |

### Novelty lies in alignment and power

**The components are not individually new.** Dialogue, cooperative learning, explicit teaching, and formative assessment have established histories. What is new in the proposed orientation is their deliberate alignment: language goals, learner influence, inclusion, assessment, and institutional support are designed together rather than added as separate initiatives.

**Teacher authority becomes more transparent.** Teachers retain responsibility for curriculum, safety, accurate models, equitable participation, and appropriate challenge. Learners gain influence over selected questions, examples, strategies, audiences, and revisions. Importantly, they should also learn why some decisions are shared while others remain adult responsibilities.

**Choice requires an equity test.** OECD’s discussion of PISA 2012 mathematics data shows that allowing 15-year-olds to select procedures or encounter problems in varied contexts could benefit socioeconomically advantaged learners more than disadvantaged learners. This is an adjacent-population warning, not an elementary-English treatment effect. [2] Its implication is nonetheless clear: a choice is empowering only when children possess the knowledge, language, resources, and support required to use it.

**Participation must affect something consequential.** Selecting a worksheet color offers a different degree of influence from choosing a project question or explaining why a draft needs revision. Hayashi and colleagues’ Japanese elementary activity analysis illustrates deliberation through children proposing festival activities, inviting alternative opinions, and reaching decisions. These were general classroom activities, not English lessons, but they show how influence can become observable. [13]

**Language remains the organizing spine.** If increased choice reduces meaningful English practice or concentrates influence among confident pupils, teachers should narrow the alternatives, strengthen modeling, and redistribute opportunities before expanding independence.

## Frontline classroom enactment

### Plan purposeful tasks and scaffold participation

**Plan backward from language and influence.** Begin with a communicative purpose, identify the language needed, and decide which meaningful decision learners can make. For example, a recommendation task may require “We can … because …” while allowing pupils to choose a proposal and justify it. The cognitive demand is evaluating alternatives; the language demand is expressing a supported recommendation.

**Use a recurring construction cycle.** OELA’s 2025 practice brief presents four stages: build topic knowledge, model and deconstruct the genre, jointly construct texts, and write independently. It also emphasizes drafting, conferencing, revision, and feedback moving from purpose and organization toward language precision. [14] This sequence gives novices a practical alternative to asking for independent performance before children understand the task.

**Documented vignette: the owl unit.** OELA highlights a fifth-grade unit at Rowell Elementary School in Fresno, California. Pupils read informational texts, investigated owl pellets, kept science-journal notes, reconstructed explanation texts, identified cohesive language, and wrote about threats to owls. The school served grades K–6, and 39% of pupils were identified as English learners in the enrollment reference accompanying the example. [14] Its transferable feature is the connection among experience, subject knowledge, discussion, text analysis, and writing—not a requirement to reproduce the same materials.

**Responsibility should shift through supported stages.** A 24-week Indonesian intervention with 102 third-grade EFL learners combined phonics with guided, collaborative, and independent reading. It reported engagement ratings increasing from 2 to 4 on a four-point scale across stages and pronunciation and comprehension improvements at *p* < .001. [15] The instructional implication is that explicit foundational teaching and increasing independence can coexist; statistical significance itself does not state the size of a learning gain.

**Creative activities need communicative consequences.** Ratnadimarta’s six-week Indonesian practitioner initiative used storytelling, role-play, interactive games, and multimedia, documenting engagement, confidence, participation, and self-expression through observations, feedback, and reflective journals. [16] A useful adaptation is to ask what children must communicate to complete the activity: solve a misunderstanding, obtain information, persuade an audience, or explain a choice. Enjoyment is valuable, but it is not a substitute for a language-learning purpose.

### Use teacher talk to distribute thinking

**Questions shape access to intellectual work.** Hardman’s study analyzed 42 video-recorded primary English lessons in England, including detailed analysis of 18 transcripts. Intervention teachers used more open or authentic initiation questions and follow-up moves than control-school teachers, accompanied by greater pupil participation and elaboration of thinking. [17] Although this evidence concerns primary English teaching in England, its interactional mechanisms offer useful adaptations for additional-language classrooms.

**Question quality includes the response pathway.** A demanding question can exclude learners if they cannot process its language or rehearse an answer. Conversely, a short display question can support learning when it checks an essential word and leads into explanation. Novices should plan both foundational checks and opportunities for original interpretation.

The following stems are illustrative adaptations, not quotations from research transcripts.

| Purpose | Teacher stem | Learner support |
|---|---|---|
| Establish meaning | “What do you notice?” | Picture, object, or short text |
| Seek evidence | “What makes you think that?” | “I think … because …” |
| Extend thinking | “Can you tell us more?” | Partner rehearsal |
| Connect contributions | “How does that connect with …?” | Repeat the earlier idea |
| Invite alternatives | “Does anyone have a different idea?” | Respectful disagreement model |
| Clarify | “Which part is unclear?” | Pointing or home-language explanation |
| Support choice | “Which option helps your goal?” | Two accessible alternatives |
| Encourage reflection | “What helped, and what will you change?” | Work sample or visual prompt |

**Processing support can change participation.** Wandersee’s 2022 capstone observed one fourth-grade classroom. During one repeated choral-question sequence, responses among five English learners present increased from none initially to three by the final prompt. [18] The useful lesson is to examine processing time, repetition, gestures, and sentence supports; increased responding should then be checked against comprehension.

**Illustrative exchange: extend rather than replace.** In a teacher-designed recommendation discussion, the interaction might proceed as follows:

> Teacher: “Which change would help our classroom?”  
> Pupil: “Reading corner.”  
> Teacher: “A reading corner. Why would it help? You can begin, ‘We can … because …’”  
> Pupil: “We can make a reading corner because we need a quiet place.”  
> Teacher: “Who has another idea, or a question about that reason?”

**The mechanism is distributed responsibility.** The teacher supplies language and maintains the discussion, but the pupil retains authorship of the recommendation. Follow-up talk asks classmates to interpret and respond rather than simply await the teacher’s verdict.

### Make collaboration and social-emotional learning explicit

**Group work needs an interactional structure.** Choirunnisa and Rodliyah followed 25 sixth-grade EFL learners in Bandung, Indonesia, through six Index Card Match sessions in five groups. Their qualitative analysis identified peer mediation, role negotiation, self- and peer regulation, clarification, positive emotional climate, and responses to difficulties. [19] These findings help explain how structured cooperation can generate opportunities to use English and co-construct understanding.

**Roles should create reciprocal contributions.** An illustrative adaptation assigns pupils responsibility for explaining a match, requesting a reason, checking understanding, or reporting a group decision. Roles should rotate, and every learner should contribute language rather than allowing the strongest speaker to become the permanent spokesperson. When necessary, teachers model one complete exchange before releasing groups.

**SEL should be taught through communication.** In a Chinese primary-school case involving interviews with three English teachers and systematic observations, Zhang and Meng reported improved engagement, collaboration, and language participation after integrating CASEL-informed activities into English instruction. [20] Practical targets include asking for help, disagreeing respectfully, explaining frustration, and repairing a broken turn-taking agreement.

**Development requires repeated opportunities, not themes alone.** Greenberg reports that effective SEL activities are sequenced, active, focused, and explicit; 73% of studies in the cited synthesis assessed outcomes only at post-test within six months of intervention completion. [9] A separate elementary-AI synthesis cites a single-session fifth-grade intervention that found no significant empathy change. [21] Reading a story about kindness therefore provides an opportunity for instruction, not proof of enduring empathy.

### Connect languages, cultures, families, and communities

**Multilingual resources can support demanding English work.** Translanguaging means drawing purposefully on more than one language to make meaning. Copland and colleagues’ work in Bangladesh, Malawi, Mexico, and Uzbekistan documents teachers using home languages, everyday materials, storytelling, and locally adapted interaction to reduce barriers to primary English participation. [22] The practical sequence may involve discussing a concept in a familiar language, examining its English expression, and then using English for the agreed communicative task.

**Keep challenge while adjusting the route.** Children can compare alternatives or explain causes even when their English is emerging. Visuals, objects, oral rehearsal, bilingual notes, simplified directions, and sentence frames can make the same intellectual task accessible. Support should be adjusted from evidence of understanding, not removed simply because the lesson timetable has reached “independent work.”

**Documented activity: Language Portraits.** Lytra and colleagues’ 2025 toolkit for primary schools in England invites children to represent meaningful languages through colors on a silhouette and discuss their portraits. It allocates 20 minutes plus presentation and discussion time, suggesting that presentations in a class of 30 can extend across several days. [23] An English-learning adaptation might teach “I use … with …” while allowing children to decide which aspects of their language lives to share.

**Intercultural learning requires interpretation rather than display.** Comparing greetings, stories, family practices, or environmental concerns becomes educationally significant when pupils examine perspectives and avoid treating one child as a representative of an entire culture. ACTFL connects literacy with active comparison of linguistic and cultural systems. [3] Teachers can ask whose viewpoint appears in a text, what another person might think, and which evidence supports an interpretation.

**Values become meaningful through real decisions.** The Portuguese PEPPA pilot, implemented with first- and second-year pupils during 2022–2023, connected project-based English with sustainability, cultural understanding, creativity, and environmental awareness. [24] An adaptation should preserve genuine discussion: children might compare possible school improvements rather than repeat a predetermined “responsible” answer.

**Families contribute knowledge, not unpaid English instruction.** Lytra and colleagues treat school–home–community connections as fundamental to multilingual pedagogy. [23] Teachers can invite an oral story, local expression, observation, or reading conversation in any familiar language, then help children bring that knowledge into English classroom work.

**Documented vignette: supported home reading.** Malaysia’s six-month SPIRE project involved 25 five-year-old children, 25 parents, and one teacher. Interviews, journals, and home visits associated closer teacher–parent collaboration with better reading progress; the teacher supported parents, who supported children, with wider family participation also documented. This is preschool partnership evidence rather than an elementary-English causal estimate. [25]

**Reciprocity is the deciding condition.** REL West’s family-engagement brief emphasizes listening, welcoming relationships, shared decisions, and two-way communication; it also summarizes four school-initiated programs with statistically significant positive achievement effects. [26] Classroom extensions should therefore offer an in-school alternative and never make achievement depend on family English proficiency, literacy, internet access, or attendance at school events.

### Develop digital judgment before digital independence

**Technology use is not digital literacy.** Digital and media literacy involves accessing information, evaluating messages, creating communication, and recognizing how a source seeks to influence its audience. ACTFL places these activities within critical literacy. [3] Children can begin with printed materials: compare an advertisement and an informational text, identify their purposes, and ask what evidence supports each claim.

**Documented practice includes both engagement and workload.** A 2026 Wonosobo study followed three elementary English teachers in three Indonesian schools. Teachers used tools including PowerPoint, YouTube, Quizizz, Google Forms, Canva, and digital storytelling applications with scaffolding; observations and interviews described engagement and vocabulary-retention benefits alongside unstable internet, limited devices, distraction, uneven digital literacy, and increased workload. [27] The practical judgment is to select tools for a specific instructional function and prepare an equivalent low-technology route.

**Voice assistants illustrate a productive tension.** Underwood’s nine-month teacher-led study with 11 primary-age EFL learners documented enthusiasm, more English speaking in group activities, reformulation, and self-correction when pupils used voice assistants. It also reported answers that were poorly understood or mismatched to children’s language needs, requiring safe search, turn-taking, and stopping routines. [28] The tool created reasons to communicate, but the teacher remained responsible for interpretation and safety.

**Design guidance is distinct from learning evidence.** Han and Lee developed 10 principles and 24 guidelines for elementary English-speaking classes using AI chatbots. Their usability work involved three South Korean teachers designing imagined lessons, while implementation guidance explicitly links grouping to device availability and identifies connectivity and preparation requirements. [29] Such guidance can inform lesson organization without being treated as a measured pupil-learning effect.

**AI should preserve learner authorship.** Li and Wilson’s review distinguishes cognitive, creative, and language-enhancement supports and highlights the importance of students accepting, revising, or rejecting suggestions. Its concluding discussion centers on secondary writing, making elementary adaptation a teacher-mediated design decision. [8] A 2026 synthesis of 23 qualitative studies of elementary pupils’ AI perspectives likewise emphasizes privacy, comprehension, access, and continued teacher guidance. [21]

**Participation must become increasingly independent.** Across digital and nondigital activities, the test is whether support enables children to understand, communicate, judge, and act with growing responsibility. When a tool obscures understanding, replaces authorship, or excludes learners, redesign or suspend its use.

## A progressive guide for novice teachers

### A seven-step entry route

**Begin with one manageable instructional change.** A novice does not need to implement every dimension in every lesson. The following proposed sequence combines agency-supporting conditions, scaffolded language instruction, formative evidence, and professional reflection.

| Stage | Teacher action | Learner agency and evidence | Feasibility check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Establish goals | Select one language and one agency goal | Pupils explain or indicate the goals | Goals fit current proficiency |
| 2. Teach routines | Model entry, help-seeking, and stopping | Pupils practice safe participation | Directions are accessible |
| 3. Model the task | Demonstrate language and decision-making | Pupils identify useful strategies | Model is short and clear |
| 4. Construct jointly | Rehearse through pairs or groups | Pupils contribute and negotiate | Each learner has a role |
| 5. Transfer responsibility | Invite individual production and revision | Work shows personal decisions | Support remains available |
| 6. Extend and reflect | Add a family, community, or bounded digital link | Pupils explain learning and relevance | Equivalent access is protected |
| 7. Review together | Examine evidence with a mentor or peer | Teaching changes follow pupil evidence | Workload remains sustainable |

**Progress should follow readiness rather than novelty.** Add another choice, scaffold, or partnership only when the existing routine is understandable and usable. If behavior, language demands, or workload overwhelm implementation, return to one stable routine and one additional developmental goal.

### A five-session upper-primary sequence

**The sample unit is an illustration.** “A Welcoming, Greener School” is designed for upper-primary learners who can already understand simple English sentences. Five proposed 40-minute sessions provide 200 minutes of instruction; they can occupy one week where English is taught daily or extend across a longer timetable.

**The outcomes combine language and influence.** Pupils interpret short proposals, exchange preferences, and present a recommendation using “We can … because ….” They also practice inviting a partner’s contribution and explaining how feedback changes their work.

| Session | Main task | Teacher support | Learner decision | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Build knowledge | Examine familiar school-space pictures | Objects, vocabulary, home-language discussion | Identify an improvement | Initial ideas and comprehension |
| 2. Examine a model | Read a short recommendation | Think-aloud and language highlighting | Select a convincing reason | Explanation of text choices |
| 3. Construct jointly | Compare proposals and draft together | Sentence frames and role rehearsal | Negotiate a recommendation | Group talk and shared draft |
| 4. Produce independently | Create an individual recommendation | Brief conferences and accessible formats | Choose wording and supporting detail | Individual work |
| 5. Revise and communicate | Respond to peers and share | Criteria, rehearsal, and feedback | Explain a revision | Final message and reflection |

**The audience makes the task consequential.** The recommendation could be shared with another class or an appropriate school decision-making group. A family contribution might be an optional observation about a welcoming or environmentally responsible shared space, with an equivalent classroom contribution available.

### A complete 40-minute lesson

**The lesson concentrates on one recommendation.** The following proposed allocation illustrates Session 3. Timings are planning choices rather than observed classroom measurements.

1. **Entry and goal: 4 minutes.** Show two accessible proposal pictures. Pupils identify the language goal and select a participation goal, such as asking a partner for an idea.
2. **Explicit language teaching: 6 minutes.** Model “We can … because …,” relevant vocabulary, and a short clarification request. Check meaning through pointing and brief responses.
3. **Teacher modeling: 8 minutes.** Compare two short recommendations aloud. Demonstrate how a reason supports a proposal and how to invite disagreement.
4. **Partner construction: 10 minutes.** Partners hold different information cards and explain them to each other. They select a proposal and jointly construct a recommendation.
5. **Individual application: 8 minutes.** Each pupil writes, dictates, or records a personal recommendation using the agreed language target. The teacher checks whether the child can explain the message.
6. **Exit reflection: 4 minutes.** Pupils identify a useful language feature, describe a contribution, and name one next step. The teacher uses these responses to plan subsequent support.

**Management is taught before release.** Demonstrate how to obtain materials, whose turn comes first, what to do when a word is unfamiliar, and how to stop. Use a visible model and predictable attention signal. The first successful collaborative task may be short; complexity can increase after the interaction routine becomes dependable.

### A reusable planning checklist

**A compact template keeps planning coherent.** Complete the following prompts before teaching; the wording can be copied directly into a lesson plan.

- **Language and modes:** “Pupils will understand/use … through interpretive, interpersonal, or presentational communication.”
- **Agency and responsibility:** “Pupils will influence … and explain their decision by …”
- **Scaffolding and release:** “I will model …; joint practice will include …; support can reduce when …”
- **Participation and roles:** “Every pupil will contribute through …; roles will rotate by …”
- **Multilingual and inclusive access:** “Home languages, visuals, accessible materials, or alternative response routes will support …”
- **Relationships and values:** “The interaction skill or perspective-taking question is …”
- **Evidence and feedback:** “I will check … and use the result to decide …”
- **Family, community, and digital links:** “The extension is …; the equivalent alternative and privacy safeguard are …”
- **Teacher reflection:** “Afterward, I will examine whose learning and influence increased, using …”

### Troubleshooting predictable classroom problems

**Adaptation should preserve the learning purpose.** Novices should first identify whether a difficulty concerns task understanding, English expression, participation, resources, or classroom organization. Different problems require different responses.

| Classroom problem | Likely instructional issue | Immediate adaptation | Evidence to revisit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Only fluent pupils speak | Unequal processing and language access | Rehearsal, frames, and structured turns | Contributions across pupils |
| Groups are busy but superficial | Weak communicative necessity | Require different information and a justified decision | Quality of explanations |
| Directions cause confusion | Excessive linguistic complexity | Demonstrate, simplify, and bridge languages | Pupils explain the task |
| Proficiency varies widely | One access route dominates | Offer visuals, oral rehearsal, and extensions | Challenge at different levels |
| Devices or internet fail | Tool dependence | Use prepared cards or offline materials | Equivalent learning opportunities |
| Assessment consumes the lesson | Excessive recording demands | Sample one language and one participation indicator | Quality of instructional decisions |
| Home tasks increase inequality | Assumed family resources | Provide in-school completion and oral options | Participation without penalties |

**Mixed proficiency does not require separate curricula.** Keep a shared question while varying language supports, response length, rehearsal, and expected elaboration. An emerging learner might produce a supported recommendation; a more proficient learner might compare alternatives and respond to a counterargument. Collaborate with specialist colleagues to adapt access for disability-related needs without confusing those needs with English proficiency.

### Mentoring and reflective practice

**Novice difficulties are instructional and institutional.** A 2024 Korean study surveyed 50 elementary English teachers and involved 10 in focus-group research. Participants described insufficient English provision in novice training, difficulty teaching through English, and challenges addressing different proficiency levels, while identifying mentoring, learning communities, and further training as helpful. [30]

**Documented reflection shows professional decision-making.** During April 2015–March 2016, Nemoto and Takagi’s Tokyo practice study followed a novice external English instructor teaching two sixth-grade classes weekly. The practitioner completed 25 post-lesson mentor dialogues averaging approximately 25 minutes. A co-designed three-hint quiz was reported to increase pupil English utterances, while the practitioner selected suggestions according to teaching beliefs, pupil conditions, and feasibility. [31] The transferable insight is disciplined consideration of alternatives, not imitation of an intensive mentoring schedule.

**Professional learning needs sustained classroom connections.** Darling-Hammond, Hyler, and Gardner’s review of 35 rigorous studies identifies seven recurring features: content focus, active learning, collaboration, models, coaching, feedback and reflection, and sustained duration. The report also discusses six strong-methodology studies finding no student-learning impact. [32] Teacher learning must therefore be judged through classroom practice and pupil evidence, not attendance alone.

**Experimental evidence favors purposeful collaboration.** A yearlong professional-learning trial randomized 30 elementary schools and analyzed 29 ESL teachers from 29 schools. Four full-day workshops, weekly team meetings, and monthly coaching improved observed instructional strategies and collaboration processes, but did not significantly change collaboration frequency or incorporation of cultural wealth into the classroom environment. All analyzed teachers had more than five years’ experience, making novice application a professional-learning adaptation. [33]

**Mentoring also supports identity and resilience.** A 2025 Pakistan English-teacher study used quantitative data from 120 teachers and qualitative evidence from 50 participants. It identified instructional reconstruction, reflective empowerment, and socio-emotional scaffolding as interconnected mentoring pathways shaped by leadership, institutional culture, and resources. [34]

**Start small and review together.** A practical mentor conversation can examine one work sample and one interaction: What did the pupil understand? What support mattered? Whose contribution changed the task? What will be retained, removed, or adjusted? Teacher journals and peer observation become useful when they lead to such specific decisions.

## Assessment, evaluation, and ethical participation

### Keep outcomes distinct and instructionally useful

**Assessment should improve the next decision.** Formative assessment means gathering evidence during learning and using it to adapt teaching or learner action. The 40-study K–12 EFL review identifies promising practices alongside the influence of teacher and student beliefs and educational context. [7] An exit response is formative only when it informs what happens next.

**Language evidence needs complementary methods.** The National Academies’ assessment guidance recommends combining discrete vocabulary or grammar evidence with authentic language use, including retelling, interviews, directed dialogue, observation, self-evaluation, and portfolios. [35] A lively discussion may show interaction without establishing individual comprehension; a vocabulary check may show recall without establishing communicative use.

**The proposed indicators form separate modules.** Teachers can select a small number for each unit rather than score every dimension every day.

| Domain | Student outcome to examine | Practical evidence source | Instructional response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Language competence | Comprehension and purposeful production | Short checks and authentic performance | Adjust language instruction |
| Cognitive development | Supported inference or justification | Explanation linked to text or experience | Model reasoning |
| Communication and collaboration | Reciprocal meaning-making | Sampled talk and peer response | Reteach interaction routines |

### Select instruments for their actual purposes

**Validated modules can strengthen particular judgments.** The English Reading Literacy Questionnaire was developed using 784 Grade 3–6 pupils from six schools in six Chinese provinces, with data collected in February–March 2022. Its final 14 items covered habit and attitude, knowledge and skills, and culture and strategy; reported internal consistency ranged from 0.729 to 0.823, with a criterion correlation of *r* = .871 against the Chinese Students’ English Rating Scale. [36] These findings concern a reading-literacy questionnaire for its intended population, not an overall empowerment score.

**Curriculum alignment matters when selecting tools.** A separate English-education competency self-assessment was reduced from a 59-item pilot administered to 260 pupils to a 33-item version administered to 324 upper-elementary and middle-school learners. [37] Teachers should examine whether its curriculum assumptions, wording, age expectations, and purposes match their setting before adapting it.

**Frameworks organize evidence differently from instruments.** Mitra and Wadegaonkar’s Holistic Assessment Framework case involved 17 Grade I teachers in India receiving training and yearlong implementation support. It demonstrates participatory, multidimensional assessment practice rather than serving as a validated elementary-English empowerment scale. [38] Likewise, CIPP—context, input, process, and product—helps evaluate school provision and implementation conditions, as illustrated by Chang and Wang’s Hunan study. [5]

**SEL assessment should support development.** CASEL explicitly does not recommend SEL measures for accountability purposes. [39] Teachers should therefore describe teachable interaction behaviors and support needs rather than grade children’s personalities, emotional disclosures, or conformity.

### Evaluate implementation, student learning, and teacher outcomes

**Implementation evidence explains what pupils experienced.** Record whether planned modeling occurred, language supports were accessible, pupils exercised the intended influence, and feedback led to revision. Note adaptations and interruptions. A completed project does not by itself establish that the proposed learning opportunities occurred.

**Student outcomes require individual evidence.** Compare an initial and later performance on comparable language demands, examine independence with appropriate support, and include unfinished work and less confident pupils. Retain separate judgments for language, agency, and participation. Improvements in enthusiasm should not be relabeled as language gains.

**Teacher outcomes include sustainability and judgment.** Useful indicators include clearer language objectives, more responsive scaffolding, better-distributed dialogue, purposeful collaboration, and increased capacity to interpret pupil evidence. Track preparation and assessment time alongside these changes. Saleem, Muhammad, and Masood’s interviews with 10 novice elementary teachers show why supportive administration alone may not resolve overcrowding, heavy teaching loads, or behavior-management demands. [40]

**Causal judgments require stronger comparisons.** A teacher can reasonably conclude that a revised routine coincided with broader participation in the class. Attributing improvement specifically to “holistic empowerment” requires evidence that separates it from additional teaching time, prior attainment, maturation, novelty, and other changes. For school evaluation, combine performance evidence with observations, pupil perspectives, implementation records, and workload information.

### Protect children’s participation and information

**Ethics is part of empowerment itself.** Formal classroom research should obtain institutional approval where applicable, appropriate parent or guardian consent, and developmentally appropriate child assent. Participation in research should be separable from access to teaching, and withdrawal should carry no academic penalty. The ERLQ study reported anonymous, voluntary participation, while the Lithuanian goal-setting study reported ethics approval, parental consent, and withdrawal rights. [36][11]

**Assent should remain responsive over time.** A 2025 cross-case synthesis of three studies with children aged 5–7 analyzed 27 video segments and showed how participation was negotiated through words, gestures, movement, silence, humor, and peer interaction. [41] Explain recording equipment and procedures in child-friendly ways, revisit the right to stop, and respond to discomfort rather than treating an initial agreement as permanent.

**Privacy safeguards apply to ordinary teaching.** Keep journals and identifiable work secure, minimize collected information, establish retention arrangements, and obtain appropriate permission before public sharing. For digital and AI activities, use school-approved arrangements, check applicable age and consent requirements, and avoid submitting identifiable pupil information to unapproved services. An offline or teacher-mediated route should remain available when access or privacy conditions are unsuitable. [21]

**Measure learning without ranking personalities.** The assessment system should help children and teachers make better decisions while protecting dignity, privacy, and equitable access to demanding English learning.

## Implications for professional learning and school development

### Protect the conditions teachers need

**Leadership must turn expectations into capacity.** Schools should protect co-planning time, mentoring, accessible materials, manageable assessment arrangements, and communication support. Martinez and McAbee’s review of 31 studies identifies induction, resources, professional development, autonomy, constructive evaluation, workload support, and assistance with difficult relationships as relevant administrative practices. [42] Leadership should therefore ask what must be resourced or removed when adding a new instructional expectation.

**Teacher education should model the approach.** Novices need to experience scaffolded practice, purposeful choice, feedback, and reflection as learners. Induction can use a recurring cycle of observing a model, rehearsing teacher talk, teaching a bounded task, reviewing pupil work, and revising with a mentor. This connects professional agency to evidence-based responsibility rather than leaving novices to improvise alone.

### Align curriculum and partnerships

**Curriculum should protect language and developmental goals.** Maintain explicit progression in vocabulary, decoding, comprehension, language conventions, and communication while providing purposeful contexts for reasoning, collaboration, and agency. Assessment requirements should recognize both discrete skills and authentic performance. Whole-child policy also requires coordinated support beyond the English classroom. [4]

**Transfer should preserve principles, not materials.** A rural class with scarce books may use storytelling and everyday objects; a linguistically diverse class may need multiple language bridges; a well-connected classroom may add supervised digital inquiry. Copland and colleagues’ four-country report supports locally responsive adaptation rather than importing one universal primary-English method. [22] The test remains whether the adaptation preserves language challenge, participation, meaningful influence, and fair access.

**Family partnership should redistribute influence.** Invite families to identify useful communication routes, contribute cultural and linguistic knowledge, and comment on classroom extensions. Share understandable examples of learning rather than only compliance information. Reciprocal partnership means schools also change in response to families. [26]

### Strengthen the next generation of evidence

**Future studies should test incremental benefit.** Priorities include validating age-appropriate domain measures; comparing integrated approaches with well-supported explicit English teaching; tracking language, agency, inclusion, and workload over time; and examining mentoring, leadership, and family-partnership mechanisms. Rural, low-resource, multilingual, and disability-inclusive settings should be represented, with implementation and subgroup outcomes reported. Digital studies should examine independent learning after support is withdrawn, alongside privacy, access, and authorship. [6][8]

**Interpretation depends on measurement and context.** The definition and indicator mapping proposed here are conceptual syntheses, not a validated composite scale. Evidence includes preliminary and single-site cases, convenience samples, self-reports, mixed-age transfer, and short follow-up; administrator-support evidence is predominantly observational. The empowerment review reports both 24 undefined studies and 27 defining or explaining empowerment, so these tallies are not treated as exact complements; Hayashi’s tabular and narrative scene counts also differ. [1][13] Reported counts refer to their stated populations or review corpora, ratings retain their original scales, and correlations and significance tests are not combined into an empowerment effect. Lesson timings, templates, and indicators are proposed design choices rather than measured outcomes or forecasts.

## Conclusion

**Empowerment strengthens rather than replaces teaching.** The defensible new paradigm is a coordinated shift toward explicit English development, supported learner influence, relational co-agency, multilingual inclusion, and whole-child participation. Its novelty lies in aligning these commitments across tasks, interactions, assessment, and institutional support.

**For novices, the first move is modest.** Teach one clear language goal, establish an accessible participation routine, give children one consequential decision, and use their work and responses to adjust support. Expansion is justified when English learning, equitable participation, and increasing responsibility are visible—and when teachers can sustain the work.

## Keywords and database expressions

**Related terms improve conceptual precision.** “Primary English,” “elementary ELT,” “primary EFL,” “English language development,” and “young learners” identify overlapping educational contexts; grade labels such as “Grades 1–6” and “upper elementary” require local interpretation. “Whole-child education” and “transformative education” are useful neighboring terms, while empowerment, agency, autonomy, voice, choice, self-regulation, and self-directed learning should retain their distinctions.

**Practice terms support novice-teacher readership.** Additional indexing terms include teacher induction, early-career teachers, mentoring, coaching, teacher agency, reflective practice, dialogic reflection, classroom management, differentiation, instructional scaffolding, and professional learning communities.

**A broad database expression connects domains.**

```text
("holistic empowerment" OR empowerment OR "whole-child" OR "whole child" OR "learner agency" OR autonomy OR "co-agency")
AND (elementary OR primary OR "upper elementary")
AND (English OR EFL OR ELT OR "language learning")
AND (classroom OR teaching OR assessment)
```

**A narrower expression targets autonomy assessment.** Liu, Abdullah, and Dong document the following assessment-focused formulation; its narrower terminology serves a different purpose from the broad expression above. [6]

```text
("learner autonomy" OR autonomous) AND English
AND (dimension* OR construct*)
AND (questionnaire* OR instrument OR assessment OR evaluation OR measure* OR rubric OR scale)
```
