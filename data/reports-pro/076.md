# Gut Microbiota, Probiotics and Prebiotics: What They Mean for Intestinal Health and Daily Diet

**A healthy gut depends on function.** Gut microorganisms help digest food, nourish the intestinal lining, maintain its protective barrier and regulate immunity. The predominant probiotic groups are lactobacilli and related lactic-acid bacteria, *Bifidobacterium*, and the yeast *Saccharomyces boulardii*, with benefits tied to particular organisms and indications. Prebiotics are substrates selectively used by host microorganisms that confer a health benefit—not simply another name for all dietary fiber. [1][2]

**Microbial activity can also cause harm.** Important colorectal-cancer-associated organisms include *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis* and colibactin-producing, *pks*-positive *Escherichia coli*. Their virulence products, together with excessive exposure to metabolites such as hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, phenols and deoxycholic acid, can damage the barrier, alter immunity or injure DNA. Specific probiotics have human evidence for some inflammatory and postoperative outcomes, but they are not established treatments for slowing colorectal-cancer progression. For everyday prevention, the strongest practical priorities are varied, fiber-rich plant foods, optional suitable live-culture foods, little or no processed meat, limited red meat and avoidance of alcohol. [3][4]

**The central questions connect.** This adult-focused review, covering evidence through 13 September 2026, explains normal microbial functions, the links between dysbiosis and cancer, the major probiotic types, how prebiotics work, which bacterial products warrant concern, and how to translate these findings into meals and safe decisions.

## 1. Gut microbiota and normal intestinal function

### The intestinal barrier is an active partnership

**Microbes help maintain their boundary.** The intestinal barrier must absorb nutrients while keeping most microorganisms and potentially harmful substances away from underlying tissues. Its defenses include mucus, epithelial cells joined by tight junctions, antimicrobial peptides, secreted antibodies and immune cells beneath the lining. These components compartmentalize microbes within the intestinal lumen—the space through which food and intestinal contents pass. [7]

**Barrier maintenance requires cellular support.** Epithelial cells must survive, renew themselves and preserve their connections. Microbial fermentation products, especially butyrate, provide energy and signals that support these functions. Short-chain fatty acids can strengthen tight-junction expression and influence epithelial defense, connecting what microbes metabolize with how effectively the intestine separates its contents from the rest of the body. [8]

**Direct microbial signals also matter.** Experimental work summarized in a 2023 review describes the proteins p40 and p75 from *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* as protecting epithelial cells against apoptosis, or programmed cell death. The same review describes *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* stimulating a host protein involved in epithelial adhesion. These examples show why microbial effects extend beyond producing nutrients: bacterial molecules can influence the maintenance machinery of the intestinal lining. [9]

**Protection is not permanent sealing.** A healthy barrier is selectively permeable and responsive. During infection, some signaling pathways temporarily change permeability to facilitate defense; persistent or excessive permeability is a different problem. Consequently, an intervention should be judged by whether it restores appropriate barrier function, not simply whether it changes a laboratory permeability marker in one direction. [10]

### Microbial digestion complements human digestion

**Human enzymes do only part.** Most readily digestible nutrients are processed and absorbed before reaching the colon. Dietary fibers, resistant starch and some other compounds escape that process and become substrates for colonic microorganisms. Microbes also use endogenous material, including mucus, shed epithelial cells and digestive secretions, so their metabolism reflects both diet and the condition of the intestine itself. [9]

**Fermentation recovers useful products.** Bacteria break down these substrates into organic acids, gases and other metabolites. Acetate, propionate and butyrate—the principal short-chain fatty acids, or SCFAs—are important products of carbohydrate fermentation. Colonocytes, the epithelial cells lining the colon, use butyrate extensively, while absorbed acetate and propionate also contribute to metabolism elsewhere in the body. [10]

**Microbes cooperate through shared products.** One organism may release smaller carbohydrates that another consumes; a third may use the resulting acetate or lactate to produce butyrate. This “cross-feeding” means that a health-supporting output can depend on a network rather than one especially abundant species. It also explains why increasing a bacterium that does not itself produce butyrate can nevertheless increase community butyrate production. [2]

**Metabolism reaches beyond dietary fiber.** Gut microorganisms transform bile acids, synthesize some vitamins and modify polyphenols, drugs and other foreign compounds. These transformations can change a molecule’s availability or biological activity. Their existence does not mean that microbial vitamin production replaces an adequate diet, or that changing the microbiota is a reliable way to alter a medicine’s effect; it establishes that microbes participate in the chemical environment in which nutrition and treatment operate. [9]

### Immune regulation means appropriate responses

**Healthy immunity includes restraint.** The intestine must tolerate food and ordinary resident organisms while responding to dangerous invasion. Microbial signals help train that distinction. Organized intestinal lymphoid structures, including Peyer’s patches and isolated lymphoid follicles, sample antigens and support adaptive immune responses rather than leaving the immune system indifferent to the organisms surrounding it. [1]

**Antibodies help exclude unwanted contact.** Secretory immunoglobulin A, usually abbreviated sIgA, binds microorganisms and toxins within the lumen and can prevent their attachment to or penetration of the epithelium. Antimicrobial peptides and mucus provide additional separation. Together, these defenses allow a densely populated microbial habitat to coexist with relatively controlled immune activity beneath the lining. [7][11]

**Recognition is not inherently inflammatory.** Toll-like receptors, or TLRs, recognize characteristic microbial molecules. Appropriate signaling supports mucosal defenses and tolerance; excessive, misplaced or persistent stimulation can sustain inflammation. This distinction matters when evaluating claims that a probiotic “stimulates immunity”: useful immune regulation is not equivalent to maximizing every immune response. [12]

**Resident communities resist incoming pathogens.** Established microbes occupy attachment sites, compete for nutrients and produce substances that make the environment less favorable to competitors. Organic acids can lower local pH, and some bacteria produce bacteriocins—antimicrobial peptides directed against other bacteria. This collective protection is called colonization resistance. [1]

### Homeostasis is resilience, not a fixed recipe

**Balance does not mean identical composition.** Microbial homeostasis is a sufficiently stable and resilient relationship that maintains barrier function, metabolic activity and appropriate immunity despite ordinary disturbances. Healthy people can carry substantially different communities. The WGO guideline therefore does not define one universal “healthy microbiota” composition or a numerical threshold that everyone should pursue. [1]

**Dysbiosis concerns composition and activity.** The term describes a persistent shift away from a health-supporting state, potentially involving depleted commensals, enriched pathobionts or altered metabolic outputs. A pathobiont is an organism that can reside without obvious disease but contribute to harm under particular host or ecological conditions. Dysbiosis is therefore more informative when connected to a function—such as weakened colonization resistance or increased genotoxic exposure—than when used as an unexplained label for an unusual stool profile. [13]

**Useful and harmful chemistry coexist.** Carbohydrate fermentation commonly supplies useful SCFAs, whereas protein and sulfur metabolism can generate ammonia, phenols, cresols and sulfides. Nevertheless, these pathways are not a simple division between “good bacteria” and “bad bacteria.” A compound’s concentration, the organisms producing it, its location and the host’s condition determine its consequences. [14]

**Function matters more than labels**: the practical goal is a resilient microbial community that supports the lining, digestion and immunity—not the highest possible abundance of one marketed organism.

## 2. Dysbiosis, intestinal inflammation and colorectal cancer

### What human probiotic evidence actually shows

**Clinical benefits depend on the endpoint.** The clearest human findings concern selected inflammatory measures and complications around colorectal surgery, rather than a consistent effect on the progression of established colorectal cancer. These outcomes matter clinically, but they answer different questions: reducing postoperative infections is not the same as preventing a recurrent tumor or extending survival. [15]

| Setting | Intervention or evidence | Main finding | Evidence level | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Moderate ulcerative colitis | *L. delbrueckii* plus *L. fermentum*, 8 weeks | Reduced inflammation and inflammatory markers | Human clinical study | Regimen-specific anti-inflammatory signal |
| After colorectal-tumor removal | Shirota preparation, 4 years | Reported suppression of tumor development | Human trial summarized in review | Post-removal neoplasia signal |
| Colorectal-cancer surgery | 21 randomized trials | Postoperative infection RR 0.54 | Human meta-analysis | Supportive perioperative benefit |
| Experimental colorectal cancer | Various probiotics and microbial products | Barrier, immune and tumor effects | Mouse and organoid studies | Mechanistic and preclinical support |

**Inflammatory improvement has human examples.** An eight-week intervention involving *L. delbrueckii* and *L. fermentum* in people with moderate ulcerative colitis was reported to alleviate intestinal inflammation, with reductions in IL-6 and expression of TNF-α and NF-κB p65. These findings connect a clinical observation with measured inflammatory signaling. They support that particular intervention in that disease setting, rather than a conclusion that any lactobacillus product treats inflammatory bowel disease. [11]

**Post-removal tumor evidence is narrower.** A 2022 review lists Ishikawa and colleagues’ 2005 trial in adults aged 40–65 after colorectal-tumor removal. The preparation contained \(1 × 10^{10}\) colony-forming units, or CFU, of strain Shirota in 1 g of powder, taken after each meal for four years; the review describes suppression of colorectal-tumor development. This is a positive, strain-specific post-removal signal, not a direct estimate of how much an established invasive cancer would slow. [16]

**Surgical infection reduction is better quantified.** A 2025 meta-analysis included 21 randomized trials involving 1,602 patients undergoing or scheduled for colorectal-cancer surgery. Its random-effects estimate for postoperative infection was **RR 0.54**, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.45–0.65. A relative risk, or RR, compares event probabilities between groups; this estimate corresponds to a 46% relative reduction in the trials’ postoperative-infection endpoint. [15]

**Absolute differences tell another part.** Reported infections occurred in 119 of 805 probiotic recipients and 232 of 797 controls—14.8% versus 29.1%. The crude absolute-difference estimate is 14.3 percentage points, calculated as \(232/797 − 119/805 = 0.1433\). The meta-analytic RR is study-weighted, so it need not equal the ratio obtained by merging all participants into two large groups. [15]

**Formulations did not perform identically.** In the same synthesis, the single-strain *Saccharomyces* subgroup did not show a statistically significant infection reduction: RR 0.34, 95% CI 0.08–1.41. That wide interval reflects considerable uncertainty about the subgroup effect and does not justify ranking yeast above bacterial preparations on the point estimate alone. The overall analysis also reported heterogeneous trials and possible publication bias. [15]

### How altered ecology becomes inflammation

**Ecological disruption can remove protective functions.** Dietary shifts, antibiotics, infection and aging can change microbial composition and activity. When these changes reduce useful fermentation or colonization resistance, potentially harmful organisms may gain access to resources or epithelial surfaces that were previously less accessible. This is one route from community disturbance to altered host exposure. [17]

**Barrier injury changes immune exposure.** Damage to mucus, epithelial cells or their junctions brings microbial molecules into closer contact with epithelial receptors and immune cells in the underlying lamina propria. Bacterial translocation—the movement of organisms or their products beyond their usual compartment—can then activate pathways that were relatively restrained while the barrier remained intact. [18]

**Persistent signaling sustains tissue injury.** TLR-associated pathways, inflammasomes and NF-κB can increase cytokines and inflammatory enzymes, including IL-6, TNF and cyclooxygenase-2. These responses are useful during appropriately controlled defense, but chronic activation can perpetuate immune-cell recruitment, epithelial injury and repeated repair. Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species generated in this environment can also damage cellular molecules, including DNA. [18]

**The relationship can become self-reinforcing.** Inflammation alters the intestinal habitat, and the changed habitat can favor organisms or activities that sustain inflammation. Loss of epithelial integrity is therefore not merely the final result of dysbiosis; it can become a cause of further microbial and metabolic change. This feedback helps explain why adding one organism does not necessarily restore a disturbed ecosystem. [10]

### How inflammation intersects with cancer biology

**DNA injury can contribute to initiation.** Cancer initiation involves changes that allow a cell to escape normal growth control. Microbial genotoxins such as colibactin can damage DNA directly, while inflammatory oxidants and some metabolites can contribute indirectly. Whether damage becomes a persistent mutation depends on exposure, DNA repair, cell survival and the tissue’s existing genetic state. [19]

**Repeated repair can promote abnormal clones.** Epithelial proliferation is essential for a healthy intestine, but chronically activated survival and growth pathways can also favor cells that already carry oncogenic changes. IL-6-linked signaling, β-catenin/Wnt activation and altered cell-death control provide mechanisms through which microbial exposure may promote expansion without necessarily causing the first mutation. [18]

**Tumor progression involves additional mechanisms.** Once a tumor exists, microbes may affect its immune environment, invasiveness or response to treatment. For example, *F. nucleatum* has experimental mechanisms involving immune suppression and chemotherapy resistance. This is biologically different from directly initiating a tumor, which is why an organism can plausibly matter during progression even when its role in the first oncogenic event is less clear. [3]

**Tumors also select their microbial occupants.** A tumor can alter mucus, bleeding, nutrient availability, local metabolism and physical structure. Bacteria enriched in that environment may be drivers, passengers exploiting the new habitat, or both at different stages. The driver–passenger distinction prevents a common error: treating every organism detected in tumor tissue as the cause of that tumor. [14]

**Confounding can change an apparent association.** The 2026 *Annals of Coloproctology* review highlights Tito and colleagues’ quantitative microbiome work, in which transit time, fecal calprotectin and body mass index were major correlates of microbial profiles. An apparent association between *F. nucleatum* and cancer stage disappeared after adjustment. Such results do not erase experimental mechanisms, but show that microbial abundance can partly reflect inflammation, bowel function or host characteristics rather than cancer stage itself. [3]

### Why probiotic mechanisms exceed current cancer claims

**Several mechanisms could interrupt inflammation.** Selected probiotics can compete with pathogens, produce antimicrobial compounds, support mucus and junctions, or influence immune-cell signaling. Some experimentally inhibit NF-κB activation; others alter regulatory T cells or help establish metabolic conditions favoring SCFA production. These mechanisms provide a coherent explanation for selected anti-inflammatory findings without implying that every strain performs all these functions. [11]

**Immune regulation is not simple suppression.** Reducing chronic tissue-damaging inflammation and strengthening antitumor immune surveillance can both be useful, even though they involve different immune responses. Experimental enhancement of natural-killer-cell activity, for instance, should not be interpreted as the same mechanism as lowering an inflammatory cytokine in colitis. The cell type, timing and disease context determine what “beneficial immune modulation” means. [11]

**Different reviews sometimes address different questions.** The 2022 probiotic–CRC synthesis included 77 clinical and experimental articles and reported a significant pooled clinical signal, but substantial heterogeneity, with I² of 71%. The 2026 clinical review places probiotic, synbiotic and postbiotic antitumor findings mainly in mouse and organoid models; it separately describes recurrence trials of dietary modulation and prebiotic strategies as largely negative or neutral. These findings should not be collapsed into either “all probiotics work” or “all microbiome interventions fail.” [16][3]

**Supportive benefits are not tumor control**: probiotics can be useful adjuncts for defined outcomes, while a general claim that they retard human colorectal-cancer progression remains beyond the clinical evidence.

## 3. Predominant probiotic types and their evidence

### Organism identity comes before product claims

**The main groups are well defined.** Lactobacilli and related lactic-acid bacteria, together with *Bifidobacterium*, dominate established bacterial probiotic use. *Saccharomyces boulardii* is an important probiotic yeast. Selected *Bacillus*, *Escherichia coli*, *Clostridium butyricum* and *Enterococcus* preparations are also used, but their evidence and safety profiles require organism-specific consideration. [1]

**Common probiotics are not dominant residents.** The organisms most often sold as probiotics are not necessarily the most abundant or functionally important organisms in a healthy adult colon. Resident *Bacteroides*, *Roseburia* and *Faecalibacterium*, for example, participate extensively in intestinal metabolism. Calling a resident organism health-associated does not automatically establish it as a probiotic product: the latter designation requires evidence of benefit when administered. [1]

**Taxonomic levels are not interchangeable.** A genus is a broad group; a species is narrower; a strain is a particular lineage within that species. In *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG, *Lacticaseibacillus* is the genus, *rhamnosus* the species and GG the strain designation. The WGO guideline also identifies GG as ATCC 53103, illustrating how a precise identifier links a commercial preparation with the organism used in research. [1]

**Older names remain common in studies.** The 2020 restructuring of *Lactobacillus* introduced 23 new genera, including *Lacticaseibacillus*, *Lactiplantibacillus* and *Limosilactobacillus*. Thus, older studies may call GG *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* and P-8 *Lactobacillus plantarum*, whereas current names are *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* and *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum*. Recognizing both names helps readers match evidence without assuming that a renamed organism is a different probiotic. [20]

**GG illustrates disease specificity clearly.** McFarland and colleagues’ 2018 synthesis examined GG across 23 randomized trials, 24 treatment arms and six indications. It found reduced pediatric antibiotic-associated diarrhea, RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.21–0.95, but no demonstrated benefit in the other five pooled indications: adult antibiotic-associated diarrhea, Crohn’s disease, *C. difficile* infection, other hospital-acquired infections and travelers’ diarrhea. A result in children receiving antibiotics therefore does not establish an adult cancer-prevention effect. [21]

**Same-species products can differ substantially.** One direct randomized comparison reported antibiotic-associated diarrhea in 6.7% of participants receiving a fermented preparation containing DN-114001 versus 33.3% receiving a Shirota-containing preparation. This was one trial, not a universal ranking of the organisms. Its value is showing why “contains *L. casei*” is too broad a description for selecting an intervention. [21]

**Defined mixtures are also specific preparations.** The three-strain combination in the table contains *L. acidophilus* CL1285, historically named *L. casei* Lbc80r and *L. rhamnosus* CLR2. A 2019 evidence guide found strong support for that mixture for preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhea. That evidence belongs to the studied combination; it does not establish that adding more strains to an unrelated product improves its performance. [22]

**Bifidobacterial evidence includes mixed results.** For HN019, a two-week study in 100 constipated adults reported reduced colonic transit time at 1.72 × 10¹⁰ or 1.8 × 10⁹ CFU/day. A larger, four-week study in 228 adults found no overall transit-time improvement, although a subgroup with fewer than three bowel movements weekly showed increased bowel-movement frequency. This is a more useful account than describing the species as uniformly “good for constipation.” [23]

**Microbiota changes are intermediate outcomes.** Oral P-8 administration for four weeks was reported to increase bifidobacteria and acetate and propionate while reducing *Desulfovibrio* and some opportunistic organisms. These findings show that an administered strain can alter community activity. Whether such changes improve symptoms or affect disease must be determined using those outcomes, rather than inferred from the microbial shifts alone. [9]

**Yogurt has a concrete digestive mechanism.** Its traditional starter bacteria supply β-galactosidase, the enzyme that breaks down lactose. The resulting benefit for lactose maldigestion is a food-level example with a specific mechanism and clinical support. It also demonstrates that a useful effect need not depend on permanent intestinal colonization: activity during digestion can be sufficient for the relevant benefit. [9]

**Yeast benefits can depend on treatment.** A historical multicenter trial tested *S. boulardii* at 1 g/day for four weeks alongside different antibiotic regimens for initial or recurrent *C. difficile* infection. Recurrence was 16.7% with the yeast plus high-dose vancomycin, compared with 50% with that antibiotic regimen plus placebo. The same benefit was not demonstrated with low-dose vancomycin or metronidazole. This is a regimen-specific finding, not a reason to self-treat *C. difficile* infection. [26]

**Mouse doses are not human instructions.** *C. butyricum* suppressed tumors in *Apc*^Min/+ and chemically induced mouse models when administered by gavage at 10⁸ CFU twice weekly. The result supports experimental investigation of a butyrate-producing organism; it does not establish an equivalent adult dose or a validated human colorectal-cancer indication. [25]

**Enterococci require additional safety scrutiny.** Some preparations have clinical evidence, and certain strains produce antimicrobial enterocins. However, the genus also includes opportunistic pathogens and strains capable of carrying transferable antibiotic-resistance determinants. A beneficial property such as bacteriocin production does not remove the need to assess strain identity, virulence and resistance transfer. [27]

**Next-generation candidates need their own evidence.** Reviews identify *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Roseburia* species as promising candidates for microbiome-directed interventions. Their association with health and their metabolic capabilities explain the interest. They should nevertheless be evaluated as particular preparations, rather than assigned the efficacy history of established lactobacilli, bifidobacteria or yeast products. [9]

### Foods and supplements are different delivery systems

**Fermentation does not guarantee probiotic status.** Yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, miso, tempeh, some pickles and cheeses may contain living microorganisms at some stage of production. Whether they remain alive when eaten depends on manufacturing, storage and preparation. The NIH distinguishes foods containing live cultures from foods containing microorganisms with demonstrated probiotic benefits. [5]

**Heating changes the live-culture question.** A fermented product cooked before eating may still be nutritious and contain fermentation-derived compounds, but it should not automatically be counted as a source of viable organisms. Likewise, heat treatment after fermentation differs from making yogurt by fermenting milk that was pasteurized beforehand. “Fermented,” “live-culture” and “clinically supported probiotic” describe related but distinct attributes. [28]

**Supplement form can improve standardization.** Capsules, powders and liquids can deliver a named single strain or mixture in a specified amount. CFU is a culture-based measure of viable organisms, not a measure of health benefit. A supplement may make it easier to reproduce a studied regimen, while a food supplies a nutritional matrix and other nutrients; neither form is inherently superior for every purpose. [5]

**Choose a preparation, not a category**: the meaningful unit of evidence is the identified organism or mixture, its formulation and the outcome studied.

## 4. Prebiotics: precise definition and mechanism

### Two criteria distinguish prebiotics from ordinary fiber

**The definition includes a health benefit.** ISAPP defines a prebiotic as “a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit.” Both selective microbial utilization and a demonstrated benefit to the host are necessary. A substance does not qualify simply because bacteria can ferment it in a laboratory vessel. [2]

**Selective does not mean one species.** A substrate may preferentially support several organisms or a functional network, including cross-feeding partners. Selectivity means that utilization is biased toward a defined part of the host-associated ecosystem rather than being an indiscriminate growth stimulus. The resulting change must connect to a health benefit, not merely a higher count of a familiar bacterium. [2]

**Fiber is the broader nutritional category.** Many dietary fibers provide useful bulk, water retention or fermentation products without satisfying the full prebiotic definition. Conversely, the modern prebiotic concept can include non-carbohydrate substrates when they meet the same criteria. Thus, “not established as a prebiotic” does not mean “not worth eating,” and a food need not carry a prebiotic claim to contribute to a healthy diet. [2]

| Category | What is administered | Defining requirement | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Probiotic | Living microorganism or defined mixture | Adequate administration confers benefit | An evidence-supported GG preparation |
| Prebiotic | Substrate used by host microorganisms | Selective utilization confers benefit | A supported inulin or GOS preparation |
| Synbiotic | Live microorganisms plus selectively utilized substrate | The mixture confers benefit | A validated microbe–substrate combination |

**The distinctions concern function, not packaging.** A prebiotic can be part of food or an added ingredient, and a synbiotic need not be a capsule. What matters is the evidence supporting the components and mixture, not whether the product is sold as a food, powder or supplement. [17]

### Main substrates and their food context

**Fructans and galactans have the strongest history.** Inulin-type fructans, including FOS or oligofructose, and GOS are the most extensively documented dietary prebiotics in the consensus literature. Resistant starch and several other fibers are important fermentable substrates, but their prebiotic status should be attached to the particular material and evidence rather than assumed for the entire category. [2][29]

**Food sources contain mixtures of substrates.** Garlic, onions, leeks and wheat contribute fructans alongside other nutrients; fruits and vegetables provide combinations of fibers and polyphenols. The amount and structure of each substrate differ among foods and processing methods. A food-source list therefore helps dietary variety but does not reproduce the dose or chemical composition of a trial using a purified ingredient. [29]

**GOS demonstrates a targeted human effect.** Studies in older adults summarized in a 2023 review reported that ten weeks of daily GOS consumption increased phagocytic and natural-killer-cell activity. These are immune-function outcomes in a defined population. They support the biological importance of selective microbial feeding without turning GOS into a general anticancer agent. [9]

**Resistant starch has a distinct role.** It resists digestion by host enzymes in the small intestine and reaches the large intestine for microbial processing. One study of butyrylated high-amylose maize resistant starch reported reduced formation of the red-meat-associated pro-mutagenic DNA adduct O⁶-methyl-2-deoxyguanosine. That is a relevant biomarker finding, but the chemically modified study material should not be equated with every ordinary starchy food. [10]

**Other candidates extend the concept.** Polyphenols, conjugated linoleic acid and several additional carbohydrates have been investigated for prebiotic properties. Some may affect the microbiota through mechanisms other than serving as a selectively used substrate. The decisive question is therefore not whether they change microbial composition, but whether the full selective-use-and-benefit relationship is demonstrated. [1]

### Fermentation connects substrates to host signaling

**Microbial enzymes explain selective utilization.** Bifidobacteria possess enzymes and transport systems that allow efficient use of particular fructans and galactans. Other organisms specialize in initiating the breakdown of larger structures. For example, *Ruminococcus bromii* can help degrade resistant starch, making smaller products accessible to additional members of the community. [2][9]

**Butyrate can emerge through cooperation.** Bifidobacteria commonly produce acetate and lactate rather than butyrate. Other organisms can use these metabolites to generate butyrate, so the effect of a prebiotic may depend on the presence and activity of multiple partners. This is why feeding one bacterial group can influence the host through products made by another. [2]

| SCFA | Representative production routes | Important host role | Relevant signaling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acetate | Produced by many intestinal bacteria | Energy metabolism and microbial cross-feeding | SCFA-receptor signaling |
| Propionate | Several pathways, including the Bacteroidetes succinate pathway | Metabolic and immune effects | Receptor signaling; HDAC effects |
| Butyrate | Producers include *Faecalibacterium* and *Roseburia* | Colonocyte fuel and epithelial regulation | Receptor signaling; HDAC inhibition |

**SCFAs are signals as well as fuels.** They activate receptors including GPR41, GPR43 and GPR109A on epithelial or immune cells. These pathways can influence junctional proteins, antimicrobial peptides, IL-18 production and immune-cell behavior. The effects connect microbial nutrition to barrier defense rather than requiring a probiotic organism to interact directly with every host cell. [10]

**Some effects involve gene regulation.** Butyrate and propionate can inhibit histone deacetylases, or HDACs—enzymes involved in regulating how accessible DNA is for gene expression. In cell and animal models, this can alter inflammatory signaling and favor regulatory T-cell responses. Butyrate’s ability to influence inflammation and cancer-cell behavior is therefore mechanistically richer than the idea that it merely “feeds the colon.” [30]

**Other benefits involve physical intestinal function.** Prebiotic fermentation may alter luminal pH, microbial competition, stool characteristics and signaling involved in intestinal motility. Some fibers also retain water or add bulk independently of a selective microbial mechanism. These parallel effects help explain why an improvement in bowel function does not identify one exclusive pathway. [9]

### Synbiotics and the limits of “more”

**Synbiotic combinations require combination evidence.** Complementary synbiotics combine independently supported probiotic and prebiotic components, generally allowing each to act through its own established role. Synergistic synbiotics are designed so that the co-administered microorganism selectively uses the accompanying substrate. For the latter, demonstrating utilization and benefit together in the target host is central to the claim. [17]

**A positive outcome alone is insufficient.** In a randomized trial of 225 adults with overweight or obesity, *B. animalis* subsp. *lactis* 420 plus polydextrose at 12 g/day produced a 4.5% relative reduction in body-fat mass, while the individual interventions did not. ISAPP explains that this result alone does not establish synbiotic status, because selective substrate utilization was not demonstrated. The example concerns body composition, but the evidentiary principle applies equally to intestinal-health claims. [17]

**Biological benefit is not limitless.** Butyrate has promoted epithelial hyperproliferation and polyps in a particular *Apc*^Min/+;*Msh2*−/− mouse model. Long-term inulin, pectin or FOS also promoted adverse liver outcomes, including liver cancer, in TLR5-deficient mice—a different organ and genetically unusual experimental setting. These counterexamples challenge indiscriminate high-dose fermentation claims; they do not overturn population guidance favoring fiber-rich foods. [30]

**Feed useful functions, not maximum fermentation**: prebiotic value rests on a demonstrated host benefit from selective microbial use, not on producing the largest possible bacterial or SCFA increase.

## 5. Concerning bacteria, virulence products and metabolites

### The main colorectal-cancer-associated organisms

**Concern centers on particular capabilities.** The most prominent CRC-associated trio is *F. nucleatum*, enterotoxigenic *B. fragilis* and *pks*-positive *E. coli*. *Enterococcus faecalis* provides another important example involving oxidative damage. Their products act differently: adhesins change attachment and signaling, enterotoxins disrupt epithelial function, and genotoxins directly damage genetic material. [3]

| Organism or subtype | Product | Main target | Biological consequence | Evidence context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *F. nucleatum* | FadA and Fap2 | E-cadherin; TIGIT | Growth signaling and immune evasion | Human enrichment plus models [31] |
| Enterotoxigenic *B. fragilis* | BFT, or fragilysin | E-cadherin and inflammatory pathways | Barrier injury, proliferation and oxidative damage | Cell, animal and human associations [14] |
| *pks*-positive *E. coli* | Colibactin | DNA | Crosslinks, breaks and characteristic mutations | Organoids and human cancer genomes [19] |
| *E. faecalis* | Superoxide and hydrogen peroxide | Epithelial DNA and cellular redox balance | DNA injury and chromosomal instability | Mechanistic and observational evidence [32] |

**FadA links attachment to proliferation.** *F. nucleatum* uses the adhesin FadA to bind E-cadherin, a protein involved in epithelial cell–cell adhesion. This interaction can activate β-catenin signaling and inflammatory or growth responses. In Rubinstein and colleagues’ experiments, deleting *fadA* weakened stimulation of cancer-cell growth, while restoring it restored the response—evidence that the molecule itself contributes to the experimental effect. [33]

**Fap2 acts through immune evasion.** Fap2 can bind TIGIT, an inhibitory receptor on natural killer and T cells. This interaction can reduce their cytotoxic activity, providing a route by which bacteria associated with a tumor may help it escape immune attack. FadA and Fap2 are therefore important virulence proteins, but they should not be described as ordinary toxic fermentation metabolites. [34]

**Strain-level findings sharpen the concern.** A 2024 *Nature* study sequenced 135 *F. nucleatum* strains, including 55 unique tumor-derived strains from 51 patients. It separated subspecies *animalis*, or Fna, into clades C1 and C2 and found that C2 dominated the CRC tumor niche. In tissue comparisons, C2—not every examined *F. nucleatum* subgroup—showed significant tumor enrichment. [31]

**Human enrichment and experimental promotion align.** In the same report’s stool datasets, Fna was detected in 29.2% of samples from 627 CRC patients versus 4.8% from 619 healthy individuals. In a colitis-associated, tumor-susceptible mouse model, Fna C2 increased adenoma numbers compared with C1 and vehicle controls. The combination supports a clade-specific tumor-promoting hypothesis without implying that carriage inevitably initiates human cancer. [31]

**BFT damages adhesion and amplifies inflammation.** Enterotoxigenic *B. fragilis*, abbreviated ETBF, produces BFT, a metalloproteinase enterotoxin also called fragilysin. BFT cleaves E-cadherin, increasing permeability and activating β-catenin-linked growth signaling, including *c-myc*, alongside IL-8 release. It can also promote Th17/IL-17-dominant inflammation, connecting epithelial damage with sustained immune activation. [32]

**Oxidative damage adds another pathway.** BFT can increase epithelial spermine oxidase activity, generating reactive oxygen species and DNA damage. This is distinct from a toxin directly cutting DNA: bacterial exposure alters host metabolism, which then creates damaging products. The distinction helps explain how multiple organisms and host pathways can cooperate in carcinogenesis. [13]

**Colibactin offers unusually direct mechanistic evidence.** The *pks* island contains genes for producing colibactin, which can alkylate DNA and generate interstrand crosslinks and double-strand breaks. Pleguezuelos-Manzano and colleagues repeatedly exposed human intestinal organoids to *pks*-positive *E. coli* over five months. A distinctive mutational signature appeared after exposure to the genotoxic bacteria but not the corresponding *pks*-mutant control. [19]

**Human tumor genomes carry related signatures.** The researchers examined 5,876 human cancer genomes from two independent cohorts and found the experimental signature in a subset, predominantly colorectal cancers. This connects a controlled bacterial exposure with a recognizable pattern in human tumors more closely than abundance comparisons alone. It still does not make every *E. coli* strain genotoxic or make a positive stool result a cancer diagnosis. [19]

**Enterococcal damage is primarily oxidative.** *E. faecalis* can generate extracellular superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, with downstream oxidative products capable of damaging mammalian-cell DNA and disturbing chromosome stability. Human associations are less uniform than the mechanistic possibilities might suggest. In a 2021 Iranian case–control study of 40 CRC and 40 control tissue samples, *E. faecalis* and ETBF were enriched in cancers, whereas *Fusobacterium* and *S. gallolyticus* did not differ significantly between groups. [32]

### Harmful metabolites are often community products

**Metabolic risk is not one-to-one.** Unlike a named toxin tied to a virulence gene, ammonia, sulfide and phenolic products may arise from distributed capabilities across many organisms. Their production depends on available substrates, microbial interactions and host handling. A 2022 metagenomic analysis of 667 participants identified sulfidogenic genes across 142 bacterial genera, illustrating why sulfur metabolism cannot be reduced to one “bad bacterium.” [37]

**Sulfide can undermine butyrate’s usefulness.** High H₂S exposure can inhibit butyrate oxidation, threatening the energy supply of colonocytes even when butyrate is present. Sulfide can also weaken mucus through effects on disulfide bonds and contribute to DNA damage. This interaction shows why health depends on the balance and handling of metabolites, not just measuring one apparently beneficial product. [35]

**Different sulfur routes feed this exposure.** *Desulfovibrio* includes organisms that reduce sulfate, while *Bilophila wadsworthia* can use sulfur associated with taurine metabolism. Dietary fat can influence bile-acid conditions that favor such organisms in susceptible animal models. However, the presence of sulfur-metabolism genes measures potential; it does not directly quantify the sulfide dose reaching a person’s epithelium. [39][37]

**Protein fermentation produces mixed consequences.** Proteolytic communities include members of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria and can produce ammonia, branched-chain fatty acids and aromatic metabolites. A strain-specific example is *F. nucleatum* ATCC 25586, in which clustered histidine-degradation pathways can release ammonia. Phenol and p-cresol have experimental associations with increased colonic DNA damage, but their production is better understood as a functional-community property than a universal characteristic of one species. [9][38]

**Protein itself is not the enemy.** These mechanisms concern what reaches the colon, how it is metabolized and the resulting exposure. They do not imply that all protein-containing foods have equivalent effects or that adequate protein should be avoided. Legumes, for example, provide protein alongside fermentable carbohydrates and fiber, while meat-heavy, low-fiber patterns create a different substrate environment. [9]

### Secondary bile acids and indoles resist simple labels

**DCA has several plausible harmful mechanisms.** Gut bacteria transform primary bile acids produced by the liver; *Clostridium scindens* and *C. hylemonae* are examples with 7α-dehydroxylating activity. Conversion of cholic acid yields DCA, which can perturb membranes, stimulate β-catenin-related proliferation and invasiveness, and interfere with p53 signaling in experimental systems. Elevated fecal DCA has also been associated with colorectal neoplasia. [8][10]

**Not all secondary bile acids behave alike.** LCA, derived from chenodeoxycholic acid, can have protective effects under particular conditions. In a 2026 human intestine-on-chip experiment, 20 μM LCA helped preserve epithelial structure and barrier integrity and reduced vascular IL-6 and IL-8 during an inflammatory challenge. Yet intestinal TNF-α was not reduced and IL-1β increased after 48 hours, demonstrating that even a broadly protective model result can contain divergent immune responses. [40]

**Receptors and exposure help explain differences.** Bile acids interact differently with receptors such as FXR and TGR5, and their membrane effects depend on concentration and cell type. Human inflammatory bowel disease can also show reduced secondary bile acids, whereas CRC studies often report increased DCA. “Secondary bile acid” is therefore a chemical category, not a synonym for “toxin.” [41]

**Indoles are another important counterexample.** Although older descriptions of protein putrefaction grouped indoles with harmful products, several microbial tryptophan-derived indoles support epithelial integrity and immune tolerance through receptors including AhR and PXR. Host-derived kynurenine can have different effects on the tumor immune environment. These distinctions argue against treating every amino-acid-derived metabolite as damaging. [10]

**Target exposures, not microbial eradication**: the hazard map supports healthier ecological and dietary conditions, not self-directed antibiotics, toxin “cleanses” or attempts to remove whole bacterial genera.

## 6. Turning the evidence into daily dietary choices

### Put dietary patterns ahead of isolated products

**Food-pattern evidence deserves first priority.** For generally healthy adults, improving the overall diet has a firmer cancer-prevention basis than purchasing a probiotic with a broad “gut health” claim. WCRF’s recommendations emphasize fiber-containing foods and lower exposure to processed meat and alcohol. These priorities are compatible with microbial mechanisms but do not depend on proving that every dietary benefit is mediated by the microbiota. [4]

| Dietary exposure | Population or design | Comparison | Reported estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total dietary fiber | Prospective-study meta-analysis | Each additional 10 g/day | CRC RR 0.90; 95% CI 0.86–0.94 [42] |
| Whole grains | Six prospective studies | Additional 90 g/day | CRC RR 0.83; 95% CI 0.78–0.89 [42] |
| Planetary Health Diet adherence | UK Biobank, 204,369 participants | Highest versus lowest quartile | CRC HR 0.66; 95% CI 0.58–0.75 [43] |
| Healthy Plant-Based Diet Index | Same UK Biobank analysis | Highest versus lowest quartile | CRC HR 0.77; 95% CI 0.68–0.87 [43] |
| Sulfur-metabolizing dietary pattern | Prospective cohort, 51,529 men | Long-term pattern adherence | Distal-CRC adjusted RR 1.43 [44] |

**Fiber findings have substantial prospective support.** Aune and colleagues’ 2011 dose-response meta-analysis included 25 prospective studies overall. The total-fiber estimate drew on 14,514 CRC cases among 1,985,552 participants, while the whole-grain estimate came from six studies. The figures describe population associations with specified increments, not guaranteed reductions for someone adding a single food. [42]

**Recent pattern findings point similarly.** The UK Biobank analysis reported on 4 September 2026 followed participants for a median of 11 years, during which 2,220 developed CRC. Higher adherence to both examined plant-forward patterns was associated with lower cancer hazards. A hazard ratio, or HR, compares event rates over follow-up; the two scores use different definitions, so their numerical estimates do not establish that one named diet is superior to the other. [43]

**Mechanisms support, but do not prove, mediation.** Fiber can increase fecal bulk, shorten transit and reduce contact between potential mutagens and the mucosa. Fermentable fractions also provide substrates for SCFA production. These mechanisms can operate together, meaning that a benefit associated with whole grains should not be attributed exclusively to butyrate or one bacterial species. [45]

**Sulfur-pattern evidence is directional rather than prescriptive.** The cohort of 51,529 men associated a sulfur-metabolizing dietary pattern with 43% higher distal-CRC risk, adjusted RR 1.43. This supports attention to long-term dietary conditions that favor harmful metabolism. It does not justify eliminating all sulfur-containing vegetables or calculating a personal risk from one meal. [44]

### Build fiber intake from several food groups

**Whole grains provide more than fermentation.** Oats, barley, wholegrain bread, brown rice and wholegrain pasta contribute fiber while replacing refined staples. Their fibers differ in structure and fermentability, so they need not produce the same microbial response. The 90 g/day whole-grain exposure in the meta-analysis is a whole-grain measure, not 90 g of fiber; actual food labels remain useful for working toward the daily fiber target. [42][4]

**Legumes make a useful substitution.** Beans, chickpeas, lentils and peas supply plant protein together with fiber and fermentable substrates. Replacing part of a meat-centered meal with legumes changes both sides of the microbial equation: it supplies carbohydrate substrates while reducing reliance on meat-heavy patterns. This is more consequential than adding a capsule while leaving the rest of the meal unchanged. [4]

**Vegetables and fruit broaden substrate supply.** Onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus, artichokes and bananas are useful examples of foods containing inulin-type fructans or FOS. Other fruits and vegetables contribute pectins, additional fibers and polyphenols. Their value is not restricted to whether every component has independently earned a prebiotic designation. [29]

**Different fibers perform complementary jobs.** A 2022 review describes fruit and vegetable fiber as generally more extensively fermented than whole-grain fiber. That does not make whole grains inferior: less-fermented material can still contribute bulk and intestinal function. Combining food groups is therefore more defensible than trying to maximize only rapidly fermentable fiber. [29]

**Resistant-starch foods can fit naturally.** Legumes and suitable grain or starchy staples can contribute resistant starch alongside other fibers; the amount depends on the food and its preparation. The useful principle is to retain varied, minimally refined carbohydrate sources rather than equating all carbohydrates with rapidly absorbed sugar. Resistant-starch research supports this substrate-based reasoning without specifying a universal food dose for cancer control. [29]

**Nuts and seeds add practical variety.** Unsalted nuts and seeds can contribute fiber and replace lower-fiber snacks. They are useful additions to a broader pattern rather than essential specialty ingredients. Affordable local pulses, grains and seasonal produce can supply the same overall dietary structure without requiring imported “microbiome foods.” [4][46]

**Tolerance should guide the transition.** Inulin-rich foods and other highly fermentable carbohydrates may provoke gas, bloating or discomfort in susceptible people. People with IBS may benefit from selected restriction of fermentable carbohydrates, whereas unnecessarily broad restriction can reduce useful substrates. The practical response is to adapt the foods and pace of change, with dietetic help when symptoms or disease make this difficult. [9]

### Use fermented foods as foods

**Live-culture yogurt is a practical option.** Plain yogurt or kefir can combine live microorganisms with protein and, for dairy products, calcium. WCRF also identifies dairy and calcium-containing foods as relevant to bowel-cancer prevention, so any benefit of a yogurt-containing pattern should not automatically be assigned entirely to its bacteria. Product choice should consider the whole food, including added sugar and suitability for the individual. [5][4]

**Plant fermentations offer cultural flexibility.** Kimchi, sauerkraut and fermented legumes can be included when suitable and enjoyed. Check whether the product is genuinely fermented, still contains live cultures and is eaten without subsequent heating if live organisms are the goal. Miso, tempeh and other cooked fermented foods can still contribute to dietary variety even when viable cultures are not the relevant feature. [28]

**Food interventions show functional possibilities.** A small Japanese intervention summarized in a 2025 review studied natto-containing miso soup in eight healthy adults for two weeks. It reported increased fecal *Bacillus* and *Bifidobacterium*, increased acetate and propionate, and decreased ammonia, p-cresol and sulfide, without a significant change in total bacterial count. This is a concrete illustration of changed microbial activity rather than simply “more bacteria.” [47]

**Short-term changes are not treatment outcomes.** The natto study measured fecal organisms and metabolites, not colorectal-cancer progression. Likewise, differences associated with habitual fermented-food consumption can reflect the wider diet and lifestyle. These foods can be sensible optional components of a good diet without being promoted as necessary anticancer products. [28]

### Replace higher-risk exposures and adapt familiar meals

**Replacement is more useful than compensation.** A fermented sausage remains processed meat; adding a live-culture food does not cancel the risk associated with regularly eating processed meat. The weekly red-meat limit is an upper boundary, not an amount everyone should aim to consume. Beans, lentils and other suitable protein foods can take a larger place in the pattern. [4]

**Microbiome claims do not justify alcohol.** Small studies reporting microbial changes after wine consumption do not overturn the cancer-prevention recommendation to avoid alcohol. Plant polyphenols can be obtained from non-alcoholic foods. Similarly, a fermented beverage should be assessed for alcohol and sugar content, not presumed healthy because microbes were involved in its production. [4][48]

**Meal structure can remain familiar.** The following are illustrative food-group combinations, not trial-tested menus or calculated nutrient prescriptions. Their shared purpose is to place fiber-containing staples and several plant foods at the center, with an optional suitable cultured food.

**Diversity means sustainable rotation.** Rotate among grains, pulses, vegetables, fruits, nuts and seeds according to affordability, culture and tolerance. The evidence supports variety and food quality, rather than one universal numerical plant-count target. A pattern that can be maintained is more useful than a short-lived attempt to consume every advertised prebiotic ingredient. [46]

**Other prevention measures still matter.** Physical activity, maintaining an appropriate body weight and avoiding smoking complement dietary choices. WCRF’s overall-pattern assessment favors following multiple evidence-based recommendations together, rather than concentrating on one supposedly protective food. Coffee and calcium-containing foods can fit that pattern when suitable, but they are not substitutes for its foundations. [4]

**Improve the pattern before adding products**: prioritize repeatable food substitutions and sufficient fiber, while treating fermented foods as optional foods and supplements as purpose-specific interventions.

## 7. Using the evidence safely

### Product quality and individual response matter

**A larger CFU count is not a better outcome.** The WGO guideline gives an IBS example in which *B. longum* 35624 was effective at 100 million CFU/day, while some other studied formulations use 300–450 billion CFU three times daily. Many over-the-counter products provide 1–10 billion CFU per dose. These amounts belong to different preparations and purposes; they are not a dose–response ranking for general gut health or colorectal cancer. [1]

**The complete label should be meaningful.** Look for genus, species, strain designation, intended storage and viable potency through the end of shelf life. The delivered preparation should match the formulation supporting the intended claim, including whether it is a single strain or defined mixture. An impressive brand name or long ingredient list does not establish that match. [1]

**Commercial quality has measurable weaknesses.** In a 2019 study of 21 commercial probiotic products purchased in Connecticut in 2016–2017, only seven listed strain-level identification. Culture and molecular testing also revealed species-label discrepancies in some products. This was a particular market sample, not an estimate for every current product, but it demonstrates why manufacturing identity and labeling belong in an efficacy discussion. [24]

**Responses depend on the host.** Baseline microbiota, habitual diet, medication, age, genetics and disease state can influence an intervention’s effects. In an intervention summarized in a 2025 review, 48 healthy Korean men consumed cheonggukjang for seven days and showed differing shifts among microbial community types. That variability is more consistent with an ecological intervention than with a product that predictably “resets” everyone to the same microbiome. [47]

**Supplement oversight is not drug approval.** Regulatory categories vary across countries and intended uses. In the United States, probiotic dietary supplements do not undergo the same premarket demonstration of disease-treatment efficacy as drugs. A structure/function claim such as supporting intestinal health should therefore not be read as proof of cancer prevention or treatment. [26]

### Safety and when to seek medical advice

**Healthy-adult tolerability is generally reassuring.** A safety review discussed by the 2023 ISAPP perspective identified 622 studies and found no significant increase in overall adverse events: RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.93–1.07. However, safety documentation was often nonspecific, including such statements in only 235 studies. Reassuring average results should therefore be interpreted alongside the recipient’s medical condition and the preparation’s quality. [6]

**Serious infections occur in vulnerable settings.** Rare probiotic-associated bacteremia, fungemia and other invasive infections have been reported, especially when immunity or the intestinal barrier is compromised. *S. boulardii* warrants particular caution in critically ill people and those with central venous catheters; contamination during handling can provide a route to bloodstream infection as well as intestinal translocation. [26]

| Population or situation | Main concern | Practical response |
|---|---|---|
| Generally healthy adults | Product quality and indication-specific benefit | Choose a supported preparation only for a clear purpose |
| Immunocompromised or critically ill people | Invasive infection and impaired defenses | Do not self-start live microbial supplements |
| Central venous catheter users | Bloodstream contamination, particularly with yeast products | Seek explicit clinical approval |
| Diagnosed cancer or active treatment | Treatment context, immunity and barrier injury | Discuss the exact product with the oncology team |
| Pregnancy and lactation | Preparation-specific maternal and infant safety | Review supplements with the maternity clinician |
| Children, especially premature infants | Age-specific dosing and vulnerability | Use pediatric guidance, not adult regimens |
| Severe active intestinal inflammation | Damaged barrier and possible translocation | Follow the treating gastroenterology team’s advice |

**Cancer patients need individualized decisions.** The postoperative-infection evidence gives a legitimate reason for clinical teams to consider selected regimens around surgery. It does not establish that every patient receiving chemotherapy, every person with severe mucosal injury or every immunocompromised patient has the same benefit–risk balance. Decisions should sit within cancer care rather than alongside it as an unreported supplement experiment. [15]

**Pregnancy evidence needs careful interpretation.** A 2021 systematic-review preprint included 70 pregnancy and lactation studies; 25 contained no safety information, and 11 entered the adverse-effect analysis. One study reported increased vaginal discharge and stool-consistency changes with *L. rhamnosus* plus *L. reuteri*, RR 3.67, 95% CI 1.04–12.2. These reported effects were not a demonstration of serious maternal or infant harm, but the uneven reporting supports reviewing the exact supplement rather than assuming universal safety. [49]

**Pediatric risk concentrates in predisposed children.** A review of reports from 1995–2021 identified 49 probiotic-associated invasive infections in children, most commonly sepsis. Most affected children were under two years and had factors such as prematurity or an indwelling intravenous catheter. These are reported cases, not an incidence rate among all probiotic users, and they explain why neonatal or medically complex pediatric use requires specific clinical oversight. [6]

**Ordinary food and therapeutic use differ.** These precautions do not mean that all people in special populations must avoid ordinary fermented foods. They mean that supplements, unusually concentrated preparations and major therapeutic dietary changes require assessment of food safety, medical condition and intended benefit. During severe active intestinal disease, increasing live organisms or fermentation products is not automatically helpful simply because the same products support health under other conditions. [30][40]

### Which conclusions deserve the most weight?

**The strongest claim is not always the most dramatic.** Normal microbial physiology, indication-specific probiotic benefits and dietary prevention guidance can all be useful without supporting a universal anticancer supplement. A stronger claim about tumor control would require replicated human trials using identified strains, formulations and doses, with meaningful progression or recurrence endpoints and adequate safety assessment. [3]

| Topic | Most defensible conclusion | Main evidence basis | Practical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal intestinal function | Microbes support barrier, metabolism and immune regulation | Physiology and mechanistic studies | Support ecosystem function |
| Probiotic inflammation effects | Particular regimens can benefit particular outcomes | Selected human trials and models | Match the preparation to the indication |
| CRC-associated bacteria | Some strains have genotoxic or tumor-promoting capabilities | Human associations, organoids and animals | Avoid simplistic species-level diagnoses |
| Prebiotics | Selective microbial use must confer host benefit | Consensus and substrate-specific studies | Distinguish prebiotic claims from general fiber value |
| Daily diet | Fiber-rich patterns and lower-risk substitutions are the clearest priorities | Prospective evidence and prevention guidance | Build sustainable, culturally adaptable meals |

**Reading the evidence requires compatible measures.** Stool abundance, microbial genes, fecal metabolites, inflammatory markers, infections and cancer outcomes measure different things; gene presence does not equal metabolic output, and fecal concentration does not directly equal epithelial exposure. Laboratory and animal mechanisms inform interpretation but do not substitute for human clinical outcomes, while observational RR and HR estimates are not personal absolute-risk predictions. CFU refers to viable culture-based counts, food quantities retain their stated daily or cooked-weekly basis, and the 14.3-percentage-point infection difference is a calculation from reported counts; the meal combinations are illustrative rather than tested prescriptions.

**The practical conclusion is food-first.** Support the microbiota with a varied, fiber-rich dietary pattern, use live-culture foods when suitable, and reserve probiotic supplements for purposes supported by the exact preparation’s evidence. Neither microbiome products nor dietary improvement should replace colorectal-cancer screening, investigation of persistent intestinal symptoms, prescribed inflammatory-bowel-disease care or oncology treatment.
