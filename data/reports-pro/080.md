# Shōjo Manga and Queer Self-Expression in Japan: Pronouns, Sentence Endings, and Media Influence

**Plausible influence, not established causation:** Shōjo manga offers recognizable ways of expressing gender, age, intimacy, and authority, making it a plausible resource for queer Japanese young adults’ personal expression. The evidence through **13 September 2026**, however, supports neither a measured exposure–language association nor a causal effect for this specific population. Its strongest finding is that pronouns and sentence-ending particles behave differently: manga pronouns often preserve conventional character distinctions, while sentence endings show substantial variation across ages, titles, situations, and speakers. [1]

**The initial hypothesis needs refinement:** There is no single shōjo linguistic pattern opposed to a single majority-Japanese pattern. Everyday conversation and queer-language research both show that the same form can perform different social functions. A reader might adopt a character’s expression, reinterpret it, quote it playfully, or deliberately avoid it; simple resemblance does not distinguish these possibilities. [2][3]

|---|---|---|
| Who is the focal population? | Proposed definition: self-identifying queer Japanese residents aged 18–29 | Keep identity participant-defined |
| What constitutes exposure? | Reading days, time, history, titles, and language engagement | Separate consumption from readership demographics |
| What do manga pronouns show? | Eight-title corpus: 603 *ore* and 452 *atashi* tokens | Genre and scene matter |
| What do shōjo endings show? | *Wa*: 249, 145, and 44 tokens across three dated titles | Substantial title-period variation |
| What does everyday Japanese show? | TalkBank: men produced 43/68 *wa* and 47/84 declarative *no* tokens | Conventional gender labels are insufficient |
| What does queer expression show? | Documentary analysis: 23/32 subjects omitted first-person forms | Contextual expression, not one queer style |
| Does expression resemble manga? | Documentary proxy favors omission and *watashi* | Ordinary contextual usage is a stronger interpretation |
| Does reading cause change? | Recognition and self-attribution support plausible pathways | Association and causation remain separate questions |

**The report proceeds comparatively:** It first defines the population and the two linguistic outcomes, then examines manga pronouns and sentence endings separately. It next evaluates everyday-Japanese comparisons, reading and fandom evidence, and observed queer expression. The final sections explain what the three-way comparison establishes, which competing mechanisms matter, and how to interpret the question ethically.

## Scope: queer Japanese young adults and personal expression

### Defining the population without assigning identities

**Use an explicit age boundary:** For the empirical question, this report proposes **18–29 years inclusive** as the operational definition of “young adults.” The focal population is Japanese young adults living in Japan who use Japanese in everyday life and identify themselves as belonging to a gender or sexual minority. This is a proposed eligibility definition, not a claim that the existing studies sampled this exact population.

**Self-description determines inclusion:** Participants should be able to describe themselves using locally meaningful terms, including lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, nonbinary, X-gender, queer, questioning, or other self-chosen descriptions. Sexual orientation and gender identity should be elicited separately because neither determines the other. The Japanese Psychological Association’s March 2023 guidelines explicitly recognize nonbinary and X-gender identities and recommend specifying which dimension of sex or gender a question concerns. [4]

**“Queer” need not be obligatory:** Someone can meet the intended population definition without personally adopting the English-derived label *queer*. Mizoguchi and colleagues’ 2014 Japanese-language research guideline describes *kuia* as broader and more ambiguous than its English counterpart and centers people’s own identification as sexual minorities. An inclusive study therefore needs an explanation of its scope, open self-description, and an option to decline particular identity questions. [5]

**Community membership is not uniform:** “The queer community of Japan” includes overlapping networks, institutions, relationships, and individual lives. Tokyo bar ethnography, documentary interviews, university conversations, and manga fandom each reveal different settings. Their value lies in identifying practices and mechanisms, rather than supplying interchangeable samples of one homogeneous population.

### Defining the language outcomes

**Pronouns are interactional choices:** The relevant Japanese forms primarily concern first-person self-reference—such as *watashi*, *atashi*, *boku*, and *ore*—and second-person address, including *anata*, *anta*, and *omae*. This differs from studying English third-person pronoun preferences. The central questions are how speakers refer to themselves and others, when they make that reference explicit, and what the choice accomplishes socially. [6]

**Omission is a substantive outcome:** Japanese speakers frequently leave person reference unspoken or use names, titles, and kinship terms. Consequently, a study limited to the distribution of overt pronouns would omit an important part of personal expression. It should distinguish the decision to use an explicit reference form from the subsequent choice among forms.

**Sentence endings organize interpersonal meaning:** Sentence-final particles are short expressions occurring at the end of clauses or utterances. They can seek agreement, present information, emphasize emotion, mark questions, or shape the speaker–listener relationship. Their social meanings depend on the construction, conversational sequence, and delivery—not simply the particle’s spelling. [7]

**Gender indexing is indirect:** A linguistic form can suggest forcefulness, softness, intimacy, refinement, youthfulness, or authority; those qualities may then evoke gendered expectations. This is *indexicality*: language points toward social meanings without mechanically identifying the speaker. Ueno applies this distinction to manga, while Abe demonstrates why it is particularly important for interpreting queer Japanese expression. [8][3]

### Keeping the investigation contemporary

**Publication dates and language dates differ:** The question specifies no historical starting point, so the relevant boundary is evidence available by 13 September 2026. Recent anchors include a 2023 conversational study, a 2024 Japanese demographic preprint using September–October 2022 data, and Japanese research guidance issued or revised in 2023–2025. Older manga studies remain useful because they directly examine the requested forms; their findings describe their dated material, not automatically contemporary readership or speech. [9][10][11]

**The central judgment is contextual:** The appropriate object of study is a repertoire of forms and meanings used by self-defined people in particular relationships—not a test of whether queer speakers depart from supposedly correct gendered Japanese.

## Manga pronouns: conventional distinctions with contextual exceptions

### What the corpus counts show

**The strongest numerical anchor is heterogeneous:** Unser-Schutz’s eight-title extended abstract analyzes volumes 1–3 of four boy-oriented and four girl-oriented series. The complete corpus contains 578,709 written characters and 45,055 utterances across dialogue and other textual categories. The pronoun analysis concentrates on 17,623 male-character dialogue utterances and 9,284 female-character dialogue utterances, using the study’s character classifications. [12]

| Corpus division | Named series | Sampling scope |
|---|---|---|
| Boy-oriented | *Death Note*; *Meitantei Konan* | Volumes 1–3 each |
| Boy-oriented | *Naruto*; *One Piece* | Volumes 1–3 each |
| Girl-oriented | *Kimi ni Todoke*; *Nana* | Volumes 1–3 each |
| Girl-oriented | *Nodame Cantabile*; *RabuKon* | Volumes 1–3 each |

**Counts support a transparent density comparison:** The table below uses the reported token counts and explicitly normalizes them by the document’s stated dialogue totals. A *token* is one occurrence. These calculated densities measure occurrences per 100 dialogue utterances, rather than the percentage of utterances containing a form.

| Form | Referent | Character category | Reported tokens | Calculated tokens/100 utterances |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| *Ore* | Speaker | Male | 603 | 3.42 |
| *Atashi* | Speaker | Female | 452 | 4.87 |
| *Washi* | Speaker | Male | 79 | 0.45 |
| *Omae* | Addressee | Male | 416 | 2.36 |
| *Anta* | Addressee | Female | 53 | 0.57 |
| *Anata* | Addressee | Female | 46 | 0.50 |

**The arithmetic is directly reproducible:** For example, *ore* density is calculated as 603 ÷ 17,623 × 100 = **3.42 tokens per 100 male-character utterances**; *atashi* density is 452 ÷ 9,284 × 100 = **4.87 tokens per 100 female-character utterances**. The other rows use the same respective dialogue denominators. All input counts come from the eight-title report. [12]

**Genre changes female self-reference:** Within the male-oriented series, female characters used *watashi* 209 times and *atashi* 15 times. Considering only these two forms, their calculated shares are 209 ÷ 224 = **93.3% watashi** and 15 ÷ 224 = **6.7% atashi**. This is a conditional distribution within a particular pair of forms and genre subset, not a distribution of all female-character self-reference.

**That contrast challenges a simple imitation account:** A person’s exposure to female characters can differ substantially depending on which manga they read. “Reading manga” therefore does not identify a consistent pronoun model. Even a division between shōjo and shōnen leaves substantial variation in scene type, character relationships, and narrative roles.

### What pronoun choices accomplish

**Public scenes offer a counterexample:** In *Death Note* and *Meitantei Konan*, the reported male-character counts favor *boku* and *watashi* rather than *ore*. These are actual alternatives within the same first-person category.

| Series | *Ore* tokens | *Boku* tokens | *Watashi* tokens | Total of these forms |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| *Death Note* | 3 | 122 | 122 | 247 |
| *Meitantei Konan* | 8 | 30 | 62 | 100 |

**Formality offers a plausible explanation:** Unser-Schutz suggests that the numerous public scenes in these series may encourage the comparatively formal *boku* and *watashi*. The explanation is conjectural, but its importance is clear: a genre difference could emerge because genres contain different social situations, rather than because they promote different gender identities. [12]

**Self-reference and address can diverge:** In *Nana*, the punk character Nana uses the conventionally feminine *atashi* for herself while using the strongly confrontational second-person form *temee* toward others. The combination is more informative than classifying her speech as simply feminine or masculine. Self-reference positions the speaker, while address can dramatize anger, familiarity, antagonism, or authority in relation to a particular listener.

**Character role matters alongside gender:** Female-character uses of *omae* in the same report tend to involve older women or nonhuman characters. A female-identifying trans character in *RabuKon* uses *atashi*, although the abstract places that character in its male-coded category. These examples expose two distinct issues: characters combine linguistic resources selectively, and researchers’ character classifications can themselves shape apparent gender distributions. [12]

**First-person uptake need not follow heroines:** The report also raises the possibility that female readers identify with male characters, especially because girls read across gender-targeted publishing categories. Consequently, even a genuine manga influence on a reader’s *boku* or *ore* would not necessarily originate in female characters using those forms. The relevant exposure is the speech of characters the reader attends to, not merely the demographic category assigned to the publication.

### What this means for the shōjo hypothesis

**The straightforward model receives counterevidence:** The eight-title report records no female-character examples of masculine first-person pronouns. A separately described six-series LSA study published in 2010 reports the same absence, while Unser-Schutz’s 2015 ten-series paper concludes that personal-pronoun usage is generally normative. These differently scoped reports should be treated as separate descriptions, rather than pooled into one enlarged sample. [13][1]

**Normative representation does not eliminate influence:** Readers can borrow from characters of another gender, exaggerate a recognizable style, or invert a conventional association. What the corpus challenges is the narrower claim that girls encounter widespread female *boku/ore* use in these manga and then directly reproduce it. That proposed route predicts a textual pattern the studies do not show.

**Pronoun influence should therefore be selective:** The most defensible manga-pronoun hypothesis concerns identification with particular voices and their social meanings. A convincing shōjo-specific pattern would be one that persists across comparable scenes and character roles—not simply a difference in the number of male and female characters represented.

## Shōjo sentence endings: variation by title, age, and stance

### Three titles provide a dated particle comparison

**Aizawa finds a marked redistribution:** Aizawa’s 2003 study examines nine feminine or feminine-associated endings in three female-protagonist shōjo titles. Its source material comes from *Glass Mask* in 1976–1979, *Tokimeki Tonight* in 1982–1986, and *Boys Over Flowers* in 1996–1998. These are source-material windows, not the complete publication histories of the series. [14]

| Title and material years | All nine endings | *Wa* | *Dawa* | *Yo* | Non-interrogative *no* |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| *Glass Mask*, 1976–1979 | 840 | 249 | 79 | 161 | 113 |
| *Tokimeki Tonight*, 1982–1986 | 494 | 145 | 29 | 101 | 61 |
| *Boys Over Flowers*, 1996–1998 | 292 | 44 | 18 | 61 | 91 |

**Composition sharpens the raw-count finding:** Calculated within the nine-ending inventory, *wa* represents 249 ÷ 840 = **29.6%**, 145 ÷ 494 = **29.4%**, and 44 ÷ 292 = **15.1%**, respectively. Its raw count falls by 82.3% between the first and third samples, calculated as (249 − 44) ÷ 249 × 100. The within-inventory comparison is especially useful because it shows a change in composition, not merely fewer counted endings.

**The pattern is not uniform disappearance:** Non-interrogative *no* falls from 113 to 61 tokens and then rises to 91. Its calculated inventory share therefore increases from 13.5% in *Glass Mask* to 31.2% in *Boys Over Flowers*. The later sample uses fewer of the selected endings overall, but that does not mean every form becomes less important.

**Convergence occurs within constructions:** Aizawa reports that both female and male characters use *da + yo* in the *Boys Over Flowers* material. Male characters also produce interrogative *no* 24 times. These findings matter because they distinguish an ending’s grammatical function from a general label such as “women’s language.” [14]

### Forms need functional interpretation

**The inventory contains different discourse resources:** Aizawa’s nine forms include simple particles and combinations conventionally treated together as sentence endings. Their interpretation should begin with what they do in discourse.

| Ending | Relevant discourse role | Interpretation for this question |
|---|---|---|
| *Wa* | Emotive emphasis; softened assertion | Stance and delivery matter |
| *Dawa* | Copular assertion with *wa* | Code the whole construction |
| *Yo* | Information presentation; assertion | Not inherently female or male |
| *Ne* | Alignment; listener involvement | Relationship-sensitive |
| *None* | *No*-based presentation plus alignment | Separate from standalone *no* |
| *Wane* | Emotive assertion plus alignment | Separate from standalone *wa* |
| *Kashira* | Wondering or questioning | Often stylized as feminine |
| *Mono* | Explanation or justificatory insistence | Interpret in its local sequence |
| *No* | Questioning or explanatory presentation | Separate interrogative and declarative uses |

**These functions require constructional coding:** Aizawa characterizes *wa* as a light assertion that can soften an utterance; Nishiguchi identifies questioning, focus, and tag-like functions for several particles; Ogi analyzes *ne* as inviting listener involvement. Combining these perspectives is more informative than treating the nine spellings as equivalent indicators of femininity. [14][7][15]

**Grammatical environment changes the classification:** Ueno’s coding scheme, for example, distinguishes *yo* after a noun or adjectival noun from *yo* after a verb or *i*-adjective. It also includes copulas, auxiliaries, commands, and some sound changes alongside particles. Thus, “gendered sentence-final expression” is broader than “sentence-final particle,” and a percentage from one category should retain that category’s definition. [8]

**Assertiveness and femininity can coexist:** Damalas’s 2016 honors thesis examines *wa* in *Sailor Moon* and argues that feminine forms can index qualities beyond fragility and soft-spokenness, including a stance of power. This provides a concrete alternative to the assumption that using a feminine-associated particle necessarily reproduces subordinate femininity. [16]

### Character age produces another substantial difference

**Teenage characters use the broadest mixture:** Ueno’s 2006 study examines nine magazines published in 2003–2004: four shōjo magazines—*Nakayoshi*, *Ribon*, *Hana to Yume*, and *The Margaret*—and five ladies’ magazines—*Young You*, *Office You*, *You*, *For Mrs.*, and *Jour Sutekina Shufutachi*. The analysis codes complete informal utterances by female characters and groups them by stated age or narrative role. [8]

| Character age | Strongly feminine | Moderately feminine | Strongly masculine | Moderately masculine | Neutral |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Teens | 8.3% | 15.0% | 4.0% | 21.4% | 51.3% |
| Twenties | 26.8% | 23.0% | 1.4% | 6.6% | 42.2% |
| Thirty and above | 36.3% | 21.5% | 0.8% | 9.3% | 32.1% |

**Age differences are large within fiction:** Adding the two feminine categories gives calculated shares of **23.3%** for teenage characters, **49.8%** for characters in their twenties, and **57.8%** for those aged thirty and above. Neutral forms remain substantial in every group. The contrast therefore concerns the distribution of several resources, not a categorical division between masculine teenagers and feminine adults.

**Publishing category also changes adolescent speech:** Within shōjo alone, teenage characters’ neutral share is 52.2% and their moderately masculine share is 20.2%. In ladies’ manga, the corresponding shares are 36.8% and 41.4%. The same represented age group is therefore portrayed differently for different intended audiences. [8]

**Narrative context explains part of this variation:** Ueno finds strongly feminine forms when characters emphasize femininity, have an aristocratic background, or adopt a somewhat more formal presentation style. Strongly masculine forms appear in confrontation and, in ladies’ manga, when female teachers address male students. These are representations of social action: flirting, presenting expertise, exercising authority, and expressing anger.

**The resulting message is ambivalent:** Shōjo can offer young characters considerable stylistic freedom while associating adult femininity with more conventionally feminine expression. That pattern could invite resistance to gender expectations, but it could also represent linguistic conformity as something expected with maturity. A reader’s interpretation matters as much as the presence of a particular ending.

**The particle judgment differs from pronouns:** The evidence supports a heterogeneous, stance-sensitive sentence-ending repertoire in manga. It does not support treating increased use of any conventionally masculine or feminine particle as an automatic sign of shōjo imitation.

## Everyday Japanese: the appropriate societal comparison

### Pronoun avoidance and contextual self-reference

**Everyday comparison starts with reference strategy:** In Barke’s 2000 study, *atashi* accounts for 72% of first-person-pronoun uses among younger women and 76% among older women. The younger group produces no second-person forms. These results accompany the broader observation that Japanese speakers frequently replace pronouns with names, titles, kin terms, or omission. [6]

| Feature | Younger group | Older group |
|---|---|---|
| Reported age range | 19–30 years | 45–60 years |
| Participants | Eight students | Seven workers |
| Recording setting | New Zealand | Tokyo |
| *Atashi* share of first-person uses | 72% | 76% |
| Observed *wa* uses | One | One |

**This is a Japanese-speaker comparison:** The younger participants were Japanese students in New Zealand, whereas the older participants worked together in Tokyo. Their interactional settings differ as well as their ages. The study nevertheless supplies two useful descriptive anchors: overt *atashi* can be common within a small set of explicit first-person references, and explicit second-person reference can be absent from an otherwise active conversation.

**The denominator determines the conclusion:** “72% of first-person-pronoun uses” does not mean that 72% of younger women, or 72% of their utterances, use *atashi*. Nor can that figure be directly compared with the manga corpus’s occurrences per dialogue utterance. One measures choice conditional on overt pronoun use; the other measures token density across dialogue.

**Address forms are socially consequential:** A second-person form directly positions an addressee. In a 2012 drama-script analysis, Park reports that *anata* can occur in formal and informal settings, while its use among peers or friends often accompanies heightened emotion. *Anta* also appears in male characters’ expressions of displeasure. These scripted examples are useful for interpreting functions, alongside the conversational evidence of avoidance. [17]

**The practical baseline is not a binary list:** A comparison should establish when a speaker makes reference explicit, which form they select, and whether the utterance concerns intimacy, disagreement, authority, or public presentation. Otherwise, greater *watashi* use in one group may simply indicate more formal interaction.

### Conversational particles challenge conventional gender labels

**Male speakers produce many relevant tokens:** The 2023 *Languages* study reports 68 standalone *wa* tokens and 84 declarative *no* tokens in selected TalkBank Sakura conversations among Japanese college students.

| Particle | Male-produced tokens | Female-produced tokens | Total tokens | Male share of tokens |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| *Wa* | 43 | 25 | 68 | 63.2% |
| Declarative *no* | 47 | 37 | 84 | 56.0% |

**These shares answer a particular question:** They show that conventionally feminine-associated forms are used by speakers classified as male in this corpus. They are shares of observed tokens, not rates per speaker, per minute, or per eligible conversational turn. The article reports 24 male and 13 female speakers and selects fourteen conversations: six mixed, four all-male, and four all-female. [9]

**Concrete sequences clarify the social meaning:** In one example, a man uses *ore* together with an utterance ending in *wa*, then expresses envy with another *wa*-final utterance. In another, *iru wa*—“I do have [friends]”—rejects a friend’s teasing suggestion that the speaker has no workplace friends. These combinations show why *ore* and *wa* should not be scored as contradictory pieces of male and female identity.

**Emotion is a prominent environment:** *Wa* follows emotion-related adjectives in 12 of 43 male-speaker cases and 9 of 25 female-speaker cases. Those calculated within-group shares are 27.9% and 36.0%, respectively. Roughly half of all *wa* tokens concern sharing information, opinions, or decisions, and six male-speaker examples involve teasing. [9]

**The explanation concerns interpersonal license:** The article interprets some male uses through *amae*, broadly the presumption that a close other will accept one’s dependence or emotional demands. That account links the particle to familiarity and confidence in the relationship. It is an interpretation of the observed sequences, not evidence that a gender group possesses an inherent psychological trait.

**Declarative *no* manages information:** The same study identifies three functions: presenting something unknown or unexpected, reconfirming an earlier statement with evidence, and explaining reasons or background. For example, a speaker uses *no* while revealing that he attended a concert; elsewhere, a speaker uses it to explain an allergy-related reaction. The relevant social work is news presentation and explanation, rather than automatic femininity. [9]

**Function counts can overlap legitimately:** The study assigns 87 functional classifications to 84 *no* tokens because some tokens perform more than one function. Such overlapping classifications should not be interpreted as mutually exclusive shares of a whole. This is especially important when comparing conversational functions with a manga inventory based on form counts.

### Prosody supplies information that print does not

**Delivery changes perceived gender styling:** Hiramoto-Sanders’s 2002 production study used six Tokyo Standard Japanese speakers—three men and three women in their twenties and thirties—recorded in Hawai‘i in April 2001. Each read six *ne/yo* sentences in natural, male-like, and female-like styles. The reported durations differ substantially across the requested styles. [18]

| Requested style | Male-group mean duration | Female-group mean duration |
|---|---:|---:|
| Natural | 198.3 milliseconds | 234.9 milliseconds |
| Male-like | 203.0 milliseconds | 205.7 milliseconds |
| Female-like | 267.3 milliseconds | 359.2 milliseconds |

**The lexical form is only one component:** The female-like performances have longer mean durations in both groups, and the study also discusses contour differences. Its framework treats *ne* and *yo* as gender-neutral forms whose delivery can nevertheless contribute to gender stereotyping. It treats *wa* as particularly dependent on prosody and dialect, rather than assigning it one invariant gender meaning.

**Relational meaning also distinguishes particles:** Ogi’s 2014 analysis argues that *ne* and *na* both invite listener involvement, while *na* adds a tone of camaraderie. In one example, its use situates an older man relative to an equal, younger, or socially lower addressee. Importantly, Ogi develops this functional analysis from manga dialogue in *Tatchi* and *Hana yori Dango*; it is an interpretive resource, not an independent sample of everyday spoken frequency. [15]

### Which broader corpora are appropriate?

**Different resources answer different comparisons:** A large corpus is useful only when its setting and measurement units suit the question.

| Resource | Material or scale | Relevant strength | Best comparative role |
|---|---|---|---|
| CEJC | Everyday Japanese conversations | Relationships, activities, age, location | Context-matched spoken comparison |
| TalkBank Sakura | Eighteen video conversations; fourteen selected in 2023 study | Multiparty peer interaction | Particle functions and sequences |
| CSJ | 3,302 lectures; 1,417 distinct speakers | Searchable speech and metadata | Selected comparable speech contexts |
| BCCWJ | Approximately 100 million words | Diverse written registers | Register-specific written comparison |
| Carpi–Iacus Twitter corpus | 408 million tweets, 2015–2019 | Large-scale online variation | Online expression, not spoken delivery |

**CEJC has especially relevant contextual fields:** Its metadata includes conversation format, activity, place, speaker relationships, age, birthplace, residence, and occupation. Omura and colleagues’ 2023 annotation work adds tools for analyzing spoken syntax. The metadata also makes the capital-region concentration of many speakers visible, enabling a geographically explicit comparison. [19][20]

**CSJ requires careful contextual selection:** Approximately 90% of its 3,302 recordings are monologues, with the remainder comprising dialogue, reading, or rereading. Its core contains about 45 hours and 500,000 words. These are valuable speech resources, but a monologue-heavy aggregate is a poor direct counterpart for particles that invite responses during peer conversation. [21]

**Written balance is not spoken representativeness:** The BCCWJ excludes manga and samples publication production rather than individual reading exposure. Its registers also cover different periods: publication materials include 2001–2005, some official and bestselling materials span 1976–2005, and blogs include April 2008–April 2009. Its 2014 description therefore supports register-specific comparison, rather than one timeless societal percentage. [22]

**Online language adds another relevant contrast:** Carpi and Iacus’s 2020 analysis examines 408 million Japanese tweets from 2015–2019 and 2,355 manually classified account timelines. About 6% of tweets contain the study’s gendered-language features, with notable exceptions to prescriptive male/female classifications. This supports studying online self-presentation separately from conversation; the 6% is a tweet-level feature prevalence, not a pronoun or particle rate among queer young adults. [23]

**The societal comparison should remain plural:** Everyday peer talk is the strongest counterpart for informal participant conversation; public interviews, social-media writing, and fictional dialogue require their own comparisons. “Majority use” is best treated as contextually matched general-population usage, rather than presumed heterosexual or cisgender speech.

## Reading exposure: distinguish genre, dose, history, and engagement

### Named genres and publications are not interchangeable

**Shōjo is an audience-oriented category:** Ueno describes shōjo as primarily directed toward school-age girls, while noting romance, fantasy, mystery, and science-fiction themes. Its target audience does not define the age or identity of every actual reader. Nor does it imply that all characters speak in a consistently feminine style. [8]

**BL and yuri need separate exposure measures:** Lee’s 2017 thesis describes Boys’ Love as a shōjo-related genre centered on male–male romance and eroticism, largely written by women for a presumed female audience. A 2019 study similarly situates yuri within shōjo traditions while examining female–female relationships and *Comic Yuri Hime*. These relationships explain why the categories overlap, but do not justify combining them into a single linguistic stimulus. [24][25]

| Exposure category | Supported examples | Relevant distinction |
|---|---|---|
| General girl-oriented manga | *Kimi ni Todoke*, *Nana*, *RabuKon* | Different characters and interactional settings |
| Ladies’ manga | *Young You*, *Office You*, *For Mrs.* | Adult-oriented audience and character roles |
| BL and BL-informed works | Male–male romance and fan transformations | Separate themes, readerships, and practices |
| Yuri | *Comic Yuri Hime/Yurihime* | Female–female narratives and audience design |
| Other manga and adaptations | *Death Note*, *One Piece*; anime versions | Cross-genre and audiovisual exposure |

**Audience design can affect the text itself:** The 2019 yuri study examines paratextual *anata*—the publication’s address to its imagined reader—as evidence of changing target-demographic positioning. That is a different use from a character addressing another character. A reader’s exposure should therefore distinguish character dialogue, narration, editorial address, and fan commentary. [25]

### Readership figures provide a bounded demographic anchor

**Yurihime reaches beyond teenage readers:** Nagaike’s 2010 article reports an Ichijinsha readership survey with the following approximate age distribution.

| Reported reader age | Share of surveyed readership |
|---|---:|
| Teenagers | 27% |
| 20–24 years | 27% |
| 25–29 years | 23% |
| Over thirty | 23% |
| Total reported bands | 100% |

**Young-adult readership is demonstrably substantial:** The calculated share aged 20–29 is approximately **50%**, from 27% + 23%. For the proposed 18–29 definition, the corresponding bounded estimate is **50–77%**: the 20–29 share contributes 50%, while anywhere from none to all of the 27% teenage category could fall at ages eighteen or nineteen. This is a bound on that publisher-reported readership distribution, not an estimate of queer Japanese young adults’ consumption. [26]

**Reader gender does not identify sexuality:** The same account reports approximately 70% female readers and discusses comments from both self-identified lesbian readers and heterosexual women. Yuri readership therefore cannot serve as a substitute for queer self-identification. Conversely, queer young adults should not be assumed to read yuri or BL rather than other manga.

**Publication cadence is a different quantity:** Nagaike describes the then-quarterly *Yurihime* as publishing four issues annually, with approximately fifteen narratives and 350–400 pages per issue. Those figures describe the publication’s format. They do not establish whether an individual reads every issue, follows one serial, rereads old material, or encounters the magazine mainly through discussion.

### Operationalizing consumption without inventing a reader profile

**Exposure requires more than reader status:** A useful operational definition separates recent frequency, time spent reading, cumulative history, specific material, and engagement with language. The following measures are proposed definitions for interpreting the empirical question, rather than observed values assigned to existing participants.

| Exposure dimension | Proposed measure | Unit or response | Analytical purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recent frequency | Days reading shōjo during the preceding 28 days | 0–28 days | Distinguish regularity |
| Reading intensity | Time spent reading during that period | Minutes per week | Distinguish time investment |
| Duration | Time since first sustained reading | Months or years | Identify cumulative history |
| Reading history | Starting age, intensive periods, interruptions | Dated account | Establish temporal ordering |
| Material | Titles, volumes, subgenres, rereading, adaptations | Named works and formats | Identify actual linguistic models |
| Language engagement | Noticing, discussing, quoting, imitating, rejecting forms | Report plus contextual example | Identify selective uptake |

**Current frequency and lifetime exposure differ:** Someone who rarely reads now may have encountered a formative series repeatedly during adolescence. Another person may read intensively in the present without having incorporated its language into ordinary interaction. These are alternative exposure histories with different implications for causation; neither can be reconstructed from a magazine’s audience profile.

**Units should remain interpretable:** Reading days capture regularity, while minutes capture intensity. Chapters, pages, volumes, and reading sessions are useful supplementary measures but are not equivalent doses. A volume rich in dialogue and a largely visual volume can provide very different exposure to pronouns and particles.

**Language engagement is the crucial bridge:** Enjoyment and emotional identification make attention plausible, but the linguistic question concerns whether particular expressions become noticeable, discussable, or usable. An account of quoting *ore* from a named character is more specific to pronoun uptake than identifying as a manga fan. Likewise, deliberately avoiding *wa* because it sounds theatrical is evidence of engagement even when production decreases.

**Fandom evidence supports social embedding:** Tanaka and Ishida’s 2015 qualitative study identifies immersive enjoyment or “flow,” a lack of oppositional reading, and interaction with other Fujoshi as important features of manga enjoyment. Those interactions can foster friendship and also produce tensions. The implication is that reading and social participation are interconnected, not that immersion necessarily produces grammatical imitation. [27]

**The exposure judgment is therefore differentiated:** The relevant predictor is not simply how much shōjo someone consumes, but which voices they encounter, when they encounter them, and how they engage with those voices.

## Queer Japanese expression: the strongest participant evidence

### Ethnography shows negotiated meanings

**Abe rejects fixed form–identity mappings:** In *Queer Japanese* (2010), Hideko Abe treats language as something produced through the speaker, the interlocutor, and their relationship. Men’s use of *watashi* and women’s use of *ore* demonstrate why grammatical choices should not be read as direct evidence of a fixed gender. The same principle applies to sentence-final expressions. [3]

**A repeated account illustrates reinterpretation:** Abe reports that Mitsuhashi Junko initially described *watashi* as usable across gender and therefore neutral during late-1990s fieldwork. In a 2008 account, Mitsuhashi understood *watashi* as feminine and sometimes used *atashi* with other women. The social meaning changes even where the lexical form remains the same.

**Self-report and situated production can differ:** Abe also describes a bar employee who generally preferred *jibun* and rejected an overly masculine presentation, but switched to *ore* when angrily addressing a customer. The shift is not adequately explained as discovering the speaker’s “real” pronoun. It performs a particular action toward a particular person.

**This directly affects the media hypothesis:** A participant could adopt a manga-associated form without adopting its conventional gender meaning. Conversely, a participant might retain *watashi* while changing how it expresses affiliation, femininity, distance, or professional identity. A form-count study and an interactional study could therefore reach different but compatible conclusions.

### Documentary speech provides numerical observations

**Bendall offers a concrete queer-language proxy:** Lisa Bendall’s 2021 thesis analyzes selected speakers in Graham Kolbeins’s 2019 documentary *Queer Japan*. The thirty-two analyzed subjects are mostly aged thirty to forty, with some older; their clips range from about twenty seconds to three or four minutes. These are observed queer Japanese expressions in public-facing documentary interaction, rather than the proposed 18–29 population. [28]

| First-person classification | Subject records | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| No first-person form | 23 | Omission in the analyzed segments |
| *Watashi* | 6 | Includes one subtitle associated with signing |
| *Boku* | 3 | Overt self-reference in the coded material |
| *Ore* | 0 | Separate from a quoted customer’s voice |
| *Atashi* | 0 | No subject classified under this form |
| Total | 32 | Edited documentary subject records |

**Omission dominates the observed material:** The calculated omission share is 23 ÷ 32 × 100 = **71.9% of subject records**. Among the nine records containing a coded first-person form, *watashi* accounts for six and *boku* for three: calculated conditional shares of **66.7%** and **33.3%**. These are classifications of subjects in excerpts, not a complete token distribution across their lives.

**Modality changes the interpretation:** One *watashi* record comes from Japanese subtitles accompanying Japanese Sign Language. Removing that translated record from the pronoun-bearing subset leaves five *watashi* records and three *boku* records; the calculated *watashi* share becomes 5 ÷ 8 = **62.5%**. The preference within this small subset still favors *watashi*, but translated wording should remain distinguishable from a participant’s own spoken production.

**Quoted speech has a different referent:** One interviewee recounts an encounter and attributes *ore* to the person being quoted. That occurrence gives evidence about how another voice is represented, not necessarily the interviewee’s habitual self-reference. This is precisely the distinction needed when participants quote manga characters: production by the participant is not automatically production in the participant’s own voice.

**Particles are present but sparsely classified:** Bendall reports particle use by nine of thirty-two subjects, calculated as **28.1%**. The listed endings include *ne* three times, *kana* twice, and *yone* and *sa* once each, alongside two *wa*-bearing strings described as *no wa* and *de wa*. The latter should be retained as constructional strings when comparing this coding with studies of standalone sentence-final *wa*. [28]

**The functional picture is ordinary and varied:** Alignment-seeking *ne*, wondering *kana*, and mixed formality are compatible with general conversational resources. Bendall finds that women’s greater formality appears in this material, but a simple pattern of masculine-speaking lesbians and feminine-speaking gay men does not. The evidence favors heterogeneous expression over a distinct, stable queer-group style.

### Other queer studies identify alternative mechanisms

**Selfhood and acceptance shape expression:** Xue’s case study, published on 31 March 2022, analyzes a semi-structured interview with one trans man using thematic analysis. It connects Japanese self-expression with relationships, social information, self-acceptance, acceptance by others, stress, and resilience. The case supports an identity-and-interaction mechanism without establishing a particular shōjo pathway. [29]

**Community performance also matters:** A study published online in 2021 and in the *Journal of Asian Pacific Communication* in 2023 examines amateur gay volleyballers associated with Ni-chōme in Tokyo. It interprets tactical *onē-kotoba*—a stylized effeminate register—in relation to discretion, disclosure, and community interaction. This is a situated practice, not evidence that gay men generally reproduce media stereotypes. [30]

**The register should not define queerness:** *Onē-kotoba* is highly visible in mediated portrayals, but its community uses and evaluations are more complicated. Treating it as the expected speech of gay participants would build the media stereotype into the research instrument. Its presence, absence, or playful use instead requires interpretation in the actual interaction.

**The participant judgment favors contextual agency:** Existing queer-language observations show speakers selecting, switching, omitting, and reinterpreting resources. They support investigating media as one possible source of those resources, while keeping speakers’ own explanations central.

## The three-way comparison: manga, everyday use, and queer expression

### Pronouns: resemblance depends on the comparison axis

**The strongest completed comparison is functional:** Manga often makes pronouns useful for rapid characterization; everyday Japanese frequently leaves reference implicit; documentary speakers mostly omit first-person forms and favor *watashi* when an overt form is coded. These findings compare different dimensions, so they should be displayed separately rather than collapsed into one similarity score.

| Pronoun dimension | Manga evidence | Everyday-Japanese evidence | Queer-expression evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overt-form density | Counts per dialogue utterance | Frequent avoidance | 23/32 documentary subjects omit |
| Self-reference | *Ore*, *atashi*, *watashi*, *boku* vary by context | *Atashi* common within overt female references | Six *watashi*, three *boku* records |
| Addressee reference | *Omae*, *anta*, *anata* mark relationships | Names, titles, and omission matter | Interpret each recorded addressee separately |
| Social function | Characterization, stance, public/private scenes | Relationship and formality | Interview presentation and negotiated identity |
| Closest supported reading | Heterogeneous fictional repertoire | Context-sensitive reference | Documentary proxy fits contextual usage |

**This is not a national frequency ranking:** The table identifies shared analytical axes, but each numerical observation retains its own denominator. Its strongest conclusion is that the documentary proxy resembles ordinary context-sensitive Japanese more closely than a stereotype in which queer speakers consistently select conspicuously cross-gender forms.

**The comparison does challenge one prediction:** If shōjo consumption produced a general shift toward heavily explicit, character-like self-reference, the documentary proxy would offer little encouragement: omission dominates. That observation concerns a mostly older, public-interview sample, however, and should not be promoted into a conclusion about the reading habits or speech of eighteen- to twenty-nine-year-olds.

**A different media effect remains plausible:** A participant could show ordinary overall pronoun density but reserve a manga-associated form for trusted friends, online expression, or playful quotation. Such selective uptake would be invisible in an aggregate interview count. The relevant resemblance would concern function and setting rather than overall frequency.

### Particles: match construction, function, and delivery

**Particle resemblance is even more multidimensional:** A written *wa* can signal stylized feminine characterization, an assertive personal reaction, or a different dialectal or prosodic realization. Likewise, interrogative and declarative *no* should not occupy the same comparison cell.

| Particle dimension | Shōjo evidence | Everyday-Japanese evidence | Queer-expression evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Wa* | 249, 145, 44 tokens across Aizawa’s titles | 43 male and 25 female tokens | Two reported *wa*-bearing strings |
| Declarative *no* | 113, 61, 91 tokens | 47 male and 37 female tokens | Interpret separately from questions |
| Alignment | *Ne* and combinations in the inventory | Listener involvement; camaraderie distinctions | *Ne* is the most reported ending |
| Context | Character age, role, confrontation, presentation | Emotion, explanation, teasing, information | Public interviews and narrated encounters |
| Delivery | Written representation | Pitch, contour, and duration | Retain audio and interactional sequence |

**The best partial judgment rejects automatic femininity:** Shōjo and everyday evidence both show gender-associated endings serving several functions. The documentary inventory also contains largely ordinary interactional resources. Consequently, simply finding *wa*, *no*, or *ne* among queer readers would not distinguish manga influence from everyday language use.

**A common comparison needs common units:** For pronouns, the useful units include occurrences per 100 comparable utterances and choice shares among explicit references. For particles, they include occurrences per 100 eligible clause endings and distributions by discourse function. Each comparison should also distinguish a form’s absence from the absence of an appropriate opportunity to use it.

**Personal expression permits another pattern:** Participants need not fall wholly into “manga-like” or “majority-like” categories. They may combine a conventional self-reference form with unconventional particles, or use the same forms with different meanings. A mixed, contextually organized repertoire is a substantive outcome rather than a failed classification.

**The three-way judgment is therefore asymmetric:** The documentary proxy supports ordinary contextual reference and varied interactional endings; classification of the proposed 18–29 population remains conditional rather than assigned by analogy.

## How media could influence expression—and why resemblance is insufficient

### Recognition is the strongest demonstrated mechanism

**Manga clearly communicates social associations:** Kinsui’s account of *yakuwarigo*, or role language, explains how speech evokes character attributes such as age, gender, occupation, status, and regional identity. Manga and anime help form and circulate these associations. His parallel utterances combining *washi*, *atashi*, or *ore* with other features illustrate how audiences infer different imagined speakers from substantially similar propositional content. [2]

**Recognition has experimental support:** Kinsui reports Hyeseon Jung’s 2007 experiment in which participants matched dialogue to manga images. Overall accuracy was 70.2% for native Japanese speakers and 42.8% for native Korean speakers—a calculated difference of **27.4 percentage points**. In the Japanese-original material, the reported results were:

| Respondent language | Material | Correct matching |
|---|---|---:|
| Japanese | Japanese manga | 89.6% |
| Korean | Japanese manga | 44.4% |
| Korean | Korean manhwa | 41.2% |

**Recognition is different from adoption:** These results support shared knowledge of character–speech associations. They do not compare high- and low-shōjo readers or measure changes in participants’ ordinary production. A person can recognize a highly stereotyped voice precisely because they regard it as fictional and avoid using it personally.

**Uptake therefore has several possible directions:** Recognition may enable imitation, identification, ironic quotation, selective borrowing, or rejection. In a queer context, it could also make a form newly interpretable as a resource for femininity, masculinity, ambiguity, intimacy, or authority. These are possible pathways, not interchangeable measured effects.

### Reader attribution supplies a narrower reception anchor

**Some readers explicitly credit manga:** Unser-Schutz reports Endo’s 2001 survey of 136 female students: twenty-three had used *boku* or *ore* at some point, while seventy-two had observed other girls using them. The calculated shares are **16.9%** and **52.9%**, respectively. Some respondents attributed their own or acquaintances’ use to manga. [12]

**The effect numerator is not twenty-three:** The twenty-three students are those reporting any such pronoun use, not those whose use was established as caused by manga. The survey is valuable because it shows that readers themselves sometimes connect media with expression. It does not convert that connection into an exposure-adjusted probability of adoption.

**Self-attribution also carries social meaning:** Saying “I picked this up from manga” can describe a remembered source, explain an unusual choice, or make that choice socially intelligible. Such an account should be taken seriously without treating it as conclusive causal identification. Its evidentiary strength increases when it names a form, character, period, and actual interaction.

### Influence and reflection can coexist

**The main scholarly disagreement concerns direction:** Ueno presents manga as a possible influence on identity and language, while Unser-Schutz’s 2015 study argues that manga may reflect changes already established elsewhere. These positions need not be mutually exclusive. Media can reproduce an existing conversational innovation and then make it recognizable or attractive to additional audiences. [8][1]

**Pronouns and particles suggest different predictions:** Because manga pronouns are comparatively conventional in the reported corpora, a reading effect might chiefly concern character identification or stylized switching. Because sentence endings vary more broadly, an effect might concern the social meanings attached to an existing particle rather than adoption of a new form.

**The evidentiary bound is explicit:** The sources support recognition of mediated styles, context-sensitive interpretation, and some reader-attributed borrowing. The upper claim justified by these anchors is that shōjo can contribute to a speaker’s expressive repertoire; they do not justify a quantified dose–response, a persistent language change, or a shōjo-specific causal effect among queer Japanese young adults.

## Competing explanations for an exposure–language relationship

**Several pathways can generate resemblance:** The alternatives below concern why the same people might both read particular manga and use particular expressions. They are analytically distinct, even when they operate together.

| Explanation | Supporting anchor | How it could resemble a media effect | Interpretive decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reader selection | Identification and fandom scholarship | Prior preferences shape reading and expression | Establish earlier preferences |
| Peer/community norms | Abe; Fujoshi interaction | Friends transmit both titles and styles | Distinguish network pathways |
| Other media | Manga–anime role-language circulation | Shared characters supply overlapping language | Separate media formats |
| Gender identity | Abe; Japanese diversity guidance | Identity shapes selection and interpretation | Use participant-defined accounts |
| Age and life stage | Ueno; Barke | Reading history and interactional demands co-vary | Compare within age and setting |
| Region and dialect | CEJC metadata; prosody research | Regional forms resemble gendered role language | Retain linguistic geography |
| Education and work | Barke; demographic survey variables | Public roles change formality and networks | Match comparable activities |

### Selection, networks, and other media

**Selection can reverse the presumed direction:** People who already prefer an assertive, delicate, ambiguous, or character-like style may seek manga that resonates with that preference. A relationship between reading frequency and *ore* would then be compatible with language preference influencing media choice. It would not establish the reverse.

**Peer influence can be independent:** Friends may recommend the same manga while also sharing a local style. In that case, friendship helps produce both exposure and speech. Tanaka and Ishida’s socially embedded account of Fujoshi enjoyment makes this a concrete alternative rather than a purely statistical concern. [27]

**Networks can also mediate genuine influence:** If reading introduces someone to a fandom and the fandom then provides opportunities to try expressions, peer interaction is part of the media pathway. Treating every peer variable as something to “control away” could remove the very process being investigated. The crucial distinction is whether the network relationship preceded, followed, or developed alongside the relevant reading.

**Other media complicate attribution:** Manga characters also appear in anime, drama, music, fan fiction, and online discussion. A print reader may learn lexical forms from manga but their delivery from an adaptation or a friend. Dahlberg-Dodd’s 2019 dissertation examines sentence-final expression across manga, popular music, and anime, including audience design in yuri and sociophonetic variation in *wa*. [31]

**Print and audio offer different inputs:** This matters especially for particles because intonation, pitch, and duration contribute to interpretation. A participant’s manga-like spelling in online text and anime-like delivery in speech may reflect different parts of the same media environment. Assigning both to “shōjo reading” would obscure that distinction.

### Identity, age, region, and education

**Identity can influence both sides:** A participant’s gender identity may affect which characters feel relevant and which forms feel comfortable. It may also change over the period of reading and expression. Identity can therefore function as a prior explanatory factor, a moderator of uptake, or part of an evolving interpretive process; it should not be reduced to a fixed binary adjustment variable.

**Age is more than eligibility:** Within ages eighteen to twenty-nine, people can differ in school, university, work, household arrangements, and community participation. Ueno’s character-age distributions demonstrate how strongly age is represented in fictional style, while Barke’s comparison shows that age groups also occupy different conversational settings. A cumulative reading measure can consequently capture age and life experience as well as media exposure.

**Region affects the linguistic interpretation itself:** A form read as gender-transgressive in Tokyo-standard role language may have a different local meaning. CEJC’s birthplace and residence fields are relevant for precisely this reason. Recording broad linguistic geography is more informative than assigning a gender meaning from spelling alone. [19]

**Education is intertwined with activity:** University recruitment would concentrate particular ages and social networks, while workplace interaction may demand different levels of formality. Barke’s student–worker contrast illustrates that compositional issue. The 2024 JACSIS preprint, using data collected from 12 September to 19 October 2022, also treats academic attainment as a demographic dimension; its relevance here is compositional, not a media-language effect. [10]

**The causal decision has one common condition:** For both pronouns and particles, association requires participant-linked reading frequency, duration, dated title history, language engagement, and recorded expression in the proposed Japan-based 18–29 population, with self-described identities and comparable manga and everyday contexts. Causal interpretation additionally requires exposure preceding change, credible low–high or within-person exposure variation, and explicit treatment of selection, peers, other media, identity, age, region, and education. Until those conditions hold together, the direction and strength of the target relationship remain open.

**The resulting judgment is not “no effect”:** It is that several mechanisms fit the existing evidence, and direct imitation is only one of them. A null association would weaken a general dose-based hypothesis; selective, context-bound uptake could still occur without producing a simple aggregate increase.

### A compact design implied by the comparison

**The appropriate design follows the findings:** The following sequence operationalizes the existing question; it does not replace the substantive conclusions with a promise of later research.

| Step | Measure | Analytical use | Validity or ethical guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Define participants | Ages 18–29; Japan residence; self-description | Establish the population | Open identity terms; optional responses |
| 2. Describe exposure | Reading days, time, history, titles, engagement | Separate dose and identification | Avoid collecting identifying account traces |
| 3. Observe expression | Self-reference, address, endings, functions, delivery | Measure situated choices | Consent from recorded interlocutors |
| 4. Match comparisons | Similar activities, relationships, periods, denominators | Assess manga-like or other patterns | Separate quotation and translation |
| 5. Interpret relationships | Temporal order and competing pathways | Distinguish association from causation | Preserve identity and withdrawal boundaries |

**Analysis should separate two pronoun decisions:** First ask whether a speaker makes person reference explicit; then ask which form they choose when they do. For particles, distinguish the opportunity to use an ending, the construction selected, its interactional function, and its delivery. This avoids treating silence, omission, and a different grammatical environment as equivalent observations.

**Forms should not predict their own labels:** Automated manga-speaker attribution deserves particular care. A Meiji University dataset links 147,918 utterances with speakers and reports 86.9% agreement between two annotators; a combined automatic method achieves approximately 70% attribution and uses first-person forms and endings among its cues. Using those same cues to assign a speaker and then infer gendered language could introduce circularity. [32]

**The practical takeaway is specificity:** The most informative result would identify which form, function, relationship, and exposure history are connected—not merely report that manga readers sound more masculine or feminine.

## Protecting participants and interpreting expression

### Privacy is part of valid language research

**Consent should establish disclosure boundaries:** JICA’s 2025 guidance recommends local-language explanations of purpose, data use, storage, risks, anonymization, and citation permissions. It explicitly treats outing someone beyond their chosen disclosure boundaries without consent as a human-rights violation. For this question, recording ordinary conversation also implicates friends, partners, classmates, or colleagues who may not identify with the study’s focal community. [11]

**Privacy must cover recruitment and linkage:** A participant’s manga titles, small fandom, region, occupation, and quoted speech can jointly identify them even after names are replaced. Reading logs should therefore collect the minimum detail necessary, while contact information, consent records, and linguistic data should be stored separately. The Japan Sociological Society’s October 2025 guidelines emphasize both informed consent and careful personal-information management. [33]

**Pseudonyms do not guarantee anonymity:** The British Psychological Society’s 2021 internet-research guidance warns that verbatim online quotations can be searchable and that linked datasets can reveal identities. Paraphrase may protect interview narratives, but linguistic examples sometimes depend on exact wording. In those cases, restricted access, participant-approved excerpts, or minimal de-identified examples are preferable to silently altering the grammatical feature under analysis. [34]

**Safety also improves interpretation:** JICA argues that psychological safety supports more open and diverse responses. A participant who anticipates being judged for sounding insufficiently masculine, feminine, queer, or authentic may monitor their speech or avoid discussing relevant experiences. Respectful conditions therefore contribute to the quality of the evidence, not merely its ethical acceptability.

### Avoid deficit descriptions and identity inference

**Variation is not a disorder:** The outcome should be described as stylistic selection, stance, affiliation, accommodation, or self-presentation—not grammatical confusion caused by queerness. The Montréal Ethical Principles emphasize participants’ own terminology and explicitly reject pathologizing gender and sexual diversity. [35]

**Researchers should not grade authenticity:** A trans man using *watashi* is not necessarily failing to speak masculinely, and a lesbian using *ore* is not necessarily imitating men. Likewise, a gay man’s use of *wa* should not determine how his sexuality or gender is coded. Participant explanations and interactional evidence should guide interpretation without requiring everyone to articulate a stable theory of their own speech.

**Group comparisons require proportionate claims:** A purposive sample can illuminate how media-associated forms enter expression, especially in a particular fandom or community. It should not be generalized into a national percentage of queer Japanese speakers. The Japan Sociological Society recognizes the usefulness of purposive and online-panel sampling for hard-to-reach populations while distinguishing that usefulness from population representativeness. [33]

**The measurement boundaries are specific:** Manga counts describe fictional dialogue; conversation counts describe particular speakers and settings; documentary counts describe edited subject records; readership shares describe a publisher’s audience. The numerical derivations here are labeled calculations or bounds, not target-population effect estimates. The eight-title abstract’s printed line percentages do not consistently align with its stated dialogue totals, so explicit count-based densities are used; the documentary coding separates subtitles and quoted voices. Older material retains its source dates, undated documents remain undated, and the evidence combines representation, reception, everyday usage, and queer expression rather than jointly observing the target exposure and outcomes.

**The final answer is selective influence:** Shōjo manga plausibly contributes recognizable voices and social meanings that queer Japanese young adults can adopt, reinterpret, perform, or resist. Existing evidence more strongly supports that repertoire-based account than a direct imitation model. For pronouns and sentence-ending particles alike, contextual resemblance is meaningful—but it is not, by itself, evidence that reading caused the expression.
