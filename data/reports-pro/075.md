# Plasma Metal-Ion Modulation in Cardiovascular Disease: Clinical Evidence Through 13 September 2026

Through 13 September 2026, no strategy that intentionally raises or lowers plasma or serum metal concentrations is established as a general preventive or therapeutic treatment for cardiovascular disease. For primary prevention, randomized evidence is unconvincing for routine magnesium, zinc, selenium, or copper supplementation; calcium supplementation may increase vascular events. The clearest indication-specific treatment signal is intravenous iron in iron-deficient heart failure: a six-trial individual-participant meta-analysis found fewer recurrent heart-failure hospitalizations plus cardiovascular deaths, with a risk ratio of 0.72 at 12 months and 0.81 over complete follow-up, but without a statistically demonstrated mortality reduction [1].

EDTA chelation after myocardial infarction remains discordant: TACT reported a modest composite benefit, whereas TACT2 found cardiovascular events in 35.6% of EDTA-treated participants versus 35.7% of placebo participants despite a 61% reduction in blood lead [2]. Phlebotomy, iron chelation, and exposure remediation remain appropriate for specific disorders such as polycythemia vera, hereditary hemochromatosis, transfusional iron overload, or confirmed toxic-metal exposure, but their cardiovascular-event efficacy is not established for unselected patients.

## 1. Clinical question, definitions, and evidence boundaries

### What counts as plasma-metal modulation?

**The relevant intervention is intentional change.** This synthesis concerns an intervention designed to raise, lower, replace, bind, mobilize, or otherwise alter an essential or toxic metal in circulation or in a closely related body compartment, with the expectation of preventing or treating cardiovascular disease. It includes oral supplementation, intravenous replacement, dietary modification, chelation, phlebotomy, erythrocytapheresis, and environmental exposure control.

A low or high measured concentration is not itself an intervention. Nor does a prognostic association demonstrate that changing the concentration will change cardiovascular outcomes. A circulating value may reflect dietary intake, inflammation, renal clearance, tissue redistribution, acute illness, medication use, or reverse causation rather than a modifiable disease mechanism.

**Essential and toxic metals differ fundamentally.** The essential ions within the requested clinical scope are magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc, selenium, and copper. They are required for normal physiology, but both deficiency and excess can be harmful. The toxic metals or metalloids of primary interest are lead, cadmium, mercury, and arsenic; at environmentally relevant exposure levels they have no known beneficial physiologic role [3][4].

The distinction determines the therapeutic direction. Repletion is biologically plausible when a genuine deficiency limits erythropoiesis, electrical stability, antioxidant enzyme function, or tissue metabolism. Lowering is biologically plausible when there is iron overload, excessive hematocrit, or ongoing exposure to lead, cadmium, mercury, or arsenic. Raising an already adequate essential metal, however, may have no benefit or may create a new toxicity.

### Plasma, serum, whole blood, and urine are different measurements

**The compartment changes the meaning of the result.** Plasma and serum measure a circulating fraction that may be protein-bound, influenced by inflammation, and rapidly regulated. Whole blood includes red-cell and plasma components and is particularly important for lead. Urine reflects excretion and, depending on the metal, recent exposure or mobilizable body burden. Ferritin, transferrin saturation, and hematocrit are clinically useful indirect markers rather than direct plasma concentrations.

| Compartment or marker | Examples in the evidence | What it mainly reflects | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plasma or serum | Magnesium, zinc, selenium, copper, serum iron | Circulating or protein-bound fraction | Sensitive to timing, inflammation, binding proteins, and homeostasis |
| Whole blood | Lead and some toxic-metal studies | Plasma plus red-cell distribution | Useful for blood lead exposure assessment; not equivalent to plasma |
| Urine | Cadmium, arsenic species, chelation mobilization | Excretion or body burden, often creatinine-normalized | Useful for exposure characterization; a rise after chelation shows mobilization |
| Ferritin and TSAT | Heart-failure iron status, hemochromatosis | Storage and circulating iron availability | Disease-specific treatment markers, not universal plasma targets |
| Hematocrit and hemoglobin | Polycythemia vera and phlebotomy | Red-cell mass and oxygen-carrying capacity | Procedural targets for hematologic disease |

**Measurements are not interchangeable.** The zinc review notes that serum zinc varies with collection time, albumin, acute-phase responses, and kidney disease [5]. Copper is even more difficult to interpret: no single biomarker accurately and reliably measures copper status, while plasma copper and ceruloplasmin vary with estrogen status, pregnancy, infection, inflammation, and cancer [6]. The PREDIMED selenium study used fasting serum and inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry, but its carefully controlled research assay does not create a universal clinical treatment threshold [7].

### Prevention and treatment answer different questions

**Primary prevention asks whether changing status prevents a first event.** Relevant endpoints include first myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, cardiovascular death, incident heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, or atrial fibrillation. Most evidence for magnesium, selenium, zinc, copper, and toxic metals in this setting is observational, and supplementation trials often measured blood pressure, lipids, glucose, oxidative stress, or metal concentrations instead of cardiovascular events.

The contrast is visible in magnesium and selenium. A prospective meta-analysis of 16 studies involving 313,041 people found that each 0.2 mmol/L higher circulating magnesium was associated with lower CVD risk, RR 0.70 (95% CI 0.56–0.88), but the authors described an observational association and called for trials rather than a treatment recommendation [8]. Similarly, higher serum selenium was associated with lower first CVD incidence in a PREDIMED nested case-control study, OR 0.47 (95% CI 0.27–0.81) for the highest versus lowest quartile, but selenium was observed rather than assigned [7].

**Treatment asks whether a diagnosed abnormality changes outcomes in established disease.** This includes intravenous iron for iron-deficient heart failure, intravenous magnesium for acute myocardial infarction, phlebotomy for polycythemia vera or hereditary hemochromatosis, and chelation for confirmed toxic-metal poisoning. Treatment evidence can be stronger even when primary-prevention evidence is null, because the abnormality may be more severe, the disease pathway more specific, and the therapeutic window more apparent.

Intravenous iron illustrates this distinction. Iron deficiency is common in heart failure and is associated with poor function and prognosis, but the strongest randomized evidence concerns selected patients with heart failure and iron deficiency, not people without heart failure who happen to have low-normal serum iron. Conversely, TACT and TACT2 enrolled patients with prior myocardial infarction and diabetes, yet their different results do not support a general metal-lowering strategy.

### Acute and chronic changes may not have the same effect

**Time course changes both mechanism and risk.** Acute interventions include intravenous magnesium during AMI, a calcium bolus, an EDTA infusion, or 14 days of NBMI in mercury-exposed miners. Chronic interventions include oral supplementation, repeated IV iron, phlebotomy until iron targets are reached, and environmental source remediation.

An acute rise may affect electrical conduction, vascular tone, coagulation, or toxic-metal mobilization without correcting tissue stores. A chronic change may alter erythropoiesis, calcification biology, oxidative stress, or cumulative exposure, but it also creates more opportunity for gastrointestinal, renal, hepatic, electrolyte, or adherence problems. Therefore, evidence from an acute infusion cannot be generalized to chronic supplementation, and a short-term biomarker change cannot be treated as a durable cardiovascular intervention effect.

### Association is not causal efficacy

**A concentration gradient is a hypothesis, not a prescription.** People with higher magnesium, selenium, or zinc may differ in diet, socioeconomic conditions, kidney function, inflammation, medication use, physical activity, or disease severity. Conversely, established disease can alter concentrations through malnutrition, altered distribution, renal loss, or the acute-phase response. The environmental-metal review emphasizes that many studies are cross-sectional and that reverse causation and incomplete exposure assessment limit causal claims [3].

**Randomization tests the intervention.** The randomized magnesium trial in CKD raised plasma magnesium but did not slow coronary artery calcification. Selenium-only randomized trials raised blood selenium but did not reduce cardiovascular events. Zinc trials corrected serum zinc or changed lipids without testing clinical events. Copper trials changed copper enzyme activity without producing a consistent lipid benefit. These contrasts show why correcting a measured concentration and improving cardiovascular outcomes must be evaluated separately.

## 2. Proposed intervention types and biological rationale

### Supplementation and replacement

**Oral supplementation raises intake gradually.** Oral magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc, selenium, or copper can correct a nutritional deficiency, increase circulating availability, or affect enzyme systems. The route is accessible and relatively inexpensive, but absorption is regulated and varies by formulation, gastrointestinal function, inflammation, renal function, protein status, and interacting foods or medicines.

The proposed cardiovascular mechanisms differ by metal:

- Magnesium may influence vascular tone, nitric oxide and prostacyclin signaling, platelet activity, electrical stability, and calciprotein-particle formation.
- Calcium is essential for contraction and signaling, but an oral bolus can transiently raise serum calcium and may affect blood pressure, coagulation, or calcification-related physiology.
- Iron supports hemoglobin, myoglobin, mitochondrial enzymes, and oxygen transport; functional deficiency can limit tissue iron even when total stores are not empty.
- Zinc supports more than 300 enzymes, antioxidant defense, immune function, ion-channel biology, and vascular smooth-muscle signaling.
- Selenium supports glutathione peroxidases and other selenoproteins, but the relationship between status and risk may be U-shaped.
- Copper supports mitochondrial respiration, antioxidant defense, connective-tissue integrity, and iron handling; both copper deficiency and overload can be harmful.

**Intravenous iron is replacement rather than generic supplementation.** Ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose, and iron sucrose bypass gut absorption and increase available iron in selected patients with heart failure or CKD. This route can correct functional or absolute deficiency more reliably than oral iron, but it delivers a nonphysiologic load directly into the bloodstream and can produce hypophosphatemia or other formulation-specific harms [9].

### Dietary modification

**Whole-diet changes alter several exposures at once.** A Mediterranean dietary pattern changes fruit, vegetable, cereal, meat, fish, olive-oil, wine, and dairy consumption, thereby changing several mineral intakes and toxic-metal exposures simultaneously. In a randomized five-week diet-switch study of 27 healthy adults, two weeks of Mediterranean diet increased urinary cobalt by 211%, iodine by 70%, manganese by 102%, and plasma selenium by 14%; organic versus conventional food produced no significant difference in the measured metal biomarkers [10].

The direction is therefore mixed rather than uniformly beneficial. The same diet increased urinary nickel by 42%, and fish consumption was associated with urinary cadmium and lead. The study did not measure MI, stroke, HF hospitalization, arrhythmia, or cardiovascular death. A Mediterranean diet may prevent cardiovascular disease through many pathways, but its metal-biomarker changes cannot be assigned a separate cardiovascular effect from this trial.

**Dietary calcium is not equivalent to calcium tablets.** Food calcium is absorbed within a meal and does not necessarily produce the same serum-calcium peak as a 500–1,000 mg supplement bolus. Likewise, dietary copper intake and serum copper are distinct exposures: a cross-sectional NHANES analysis found lower MI odds with higher dietary copper, whereas observational studies often associate high serum copper with higher cardiovascular risk [11]. These findings should not be combined into a single copper-modulation recommendation.

### Chelation and binding

**Chelators bind metals for excretion.** EDTA is an intravenous, relatively nonspecific chelator that binds metals with valences from +2 to +6, including calcium and magnesium, and permits urinary or biliary elimination. The proposed cardiovascular mechanism is that removing metal-catalyzed oxidative chemistry might reduce reactive oxygen species, inflammation, and atherosclerotic injury; this remains a mechanistic hypothesis [12].

DMSA and DMPS use sulfhydryl groups to bind selected toxic metals. DMSA is widely used orally for lead and selected mercury or arsenic exposures, while DMPS is more water-soluble and used particularly for inorganic mercury and arsenic where available. NBMI has been studied for mercury. Iron chelators such as deferoxamine, deferasirox, and deferiprone target excess iron rather than environmental lead or mercury.

**Chelation changes more than the intended toxic metal.** In a dose-ranging EDTA pharmacokinetic study of 10 older volunteers, 3 g EDTA increased six-hour urinary excretion of iron by 3,900%, zinc by 3,400%, manganese by 58,000%, and copper by 678%; urinary calcium increased by 107% in women. Blood was collected before, but not after, infusion, so the study demonstrated urinary mobilization rather than a durable therapeutic plasma target [13].

### Phlebotomy and erythrocytapheresis

**Blood removal reduces red-cell iron and hematocrit.** A 500 mL blood unit removes approximately 200–250 mg iron. Therapeutic phlebotomy is first-line for polycythemia vera and hereditary hemochromatosis; erythrocytapheresis separates red cells and returns the remaining blood, allowing more red-cell iron removal per procedure in selected patients.

These procedures do not constitute general plasma-metal lowering. Their clinical rationale is disease-specific: lowering hematocrit reduces thrombosis risk in polycythemia vera, while lowering iron stores prevents or limits organ injury in hereditary hemochromatosis. Cardiovascular benefit in people without erythrocytosis or iron overload has not been demonstrated.

### Toxic-metal exposure remediation

**Stopping exposure is the primary intervention.** Removing lead paint, replacing contaminated pipes, ending occupational exposure, reducing contaminated food or water exposure, and stopping smoking can reduce ongoing lead, cadmium, mercury, or arsenic input. These measures act chronically and upstream, before a toxic burden must be mobilized pharmacologically.

The environmental-metal literature supports concern about exposure: in 8,989 U.S. adults followed for an average of 4.8 years, cardiovascular-mortality hazard ratios comparing the 80th with the 20th percentile were 1.69 for blood cadmium and 1.74 for urine cadmium [14]. Such findings support exposure prevention, but they do not show that reducing the biomarker with chelation prevents cardiovascular events.

**Chelation is reserved for confirmed toxicity.** Toxicology guidance prioritizes exposure removal, nutritional support, and supportive care, reserving chelation for symptomatic patients with laboratory-confirmed toxic levels. Guidance supports consideration of chelation for children with blood lead concentrations of at least 45 μg/dL; routine cadmium chelation is generally discouraged because benefit is limited and toxicity may increase [4].

## 3. Clinical evidence for metal-ion supplementation

### Magnesium

**Magnesium has the clearest observational signal but not a demonstrated chronic event benefit.** The 2013 prospective meta-analysis included 16 studies, 313,041 people, and 11,995 CVD events. Each 0.2 mmol/L higher circulating magnesium was associated with lower CVD risk, RR 0.70 (95% CI 0.56–0.88). Dietary magnesium 200 mg/day higher was not significantly associated with total CVD, RR 0.89 (95% CI 0.75–1.05), although ischemic heart disease and fatal ischemic heart disease associations were lower. These were prospective observational estimates, not supplementation effects.

The review described usual circulating magnesium distributions of approximately 0.75–0.96 mmol/L, with a broader laboratory range of 0.65–1.10 mmol/L. It identified clinical hypomagnesemia below 0.65 mmol/L and summarized small controlled dietary-restriction studies in healthy postmenopausal women in which restricting intake to 101–130 mg/day induced atrial and supraventricular beats relieved by magnesium. That evidence supports correcting severe deficiency or electrical instability; it does not establish chronic supplementation for people within usual concentrations.

**The MAGiCAL-CKD trial directly tested a status change.** This multicenter, double-blind randomized trial enrolled 148 predialysis CKD participants with eGFR 15–45 mL/min/1.73 m² and low magnesium or high phosphate. Participants received oral magnesium hydroxide 15 mmol twice daily, equivalent to 30 mmol elemental magnesium/day, or placebo for 12 months. Plasma magnesium was 0.94 ± 0.14 mmol/L with magnesium versus 0.82 ± 0.12 mmol/L with placebo at week 52, a between-group difference of 0.13 mmol/L (95% CI 0.08–0.17; P<0.001).

The primary endpoint was coronary artery calcification. Adjusted CAC change was 0.9% between groups (95% CI −10.2% to 13.4%; P=0.438), so the trial did not show slower calcification despite a measured plasma increase [15]. An open-label competing trial titrated serum magnesium toward 1.04–1.25 mmol/L and reported slower CAC progression, but achieved serum magnesium was 0.88 ± 0.13 mmol/L. The contrast may reflect titration, blinding, formulation, population, or chance; it does not establish a cardiovascular target range.

**Acute IV magnesium does not treat AMI mortality.** A Cochrane review of 26 randomized trials found a fixed-effect early-mortality OR of 0.99 (95% CI 0.94–1.04), despite a different random-effects estimate of 0.66 (95% CI 0.53–0.82) under marked heterogeneity. The large 58,050-participant trial that triggered the controversy found no benefit, and stratification by treatment timing removed a consistent mortality effect.

IV magnesium reduced ventricular fibrillation, OR 0.88 (95% CI 0.81–0.96), but increased profound hypotension, OR 1.13 (95% CI 1.09–1.19), and bradycardia, OR 1.49 (95% CI 1.26–1.77). The review judged mortality benefit unlikely in early or late treatment, with thrombolysis, or at doses of at least 75 mmol [16].

**Feasibility was high under trial supervision but tolerability was limited.** In MAGiCAL-CKD, pill counts showed that 97% of participants took more than 95% of assigned medication. However, loose stool or diarrhea occurred in 35 of 75 magnesium participants (47%) versus 9 of 73 placebo participants (12%); eight participants reduced the dose and three discontinued because of gastrointestinal effects.

The magnesium group also had 23 serious adverse events versus 13 with placebo and six major cardiovascular events versus none, including three sudden cardiac deaths, two strokes, and one incident heart-failure case. The trial was not powered for these outcomes, and investigators did not attribute them to magnesium, so the signal is hypothesis-generating rather than proof of harm. No participant exceeded 1.50 mmol/L, and the investigators noted that arrhythmia, hypotension, or syncope associated with toxic hypermagnesemia generally occurs above 2.0 mmol/L.

**Clinical judgment for magnesium.** Oral magnesium is feasible for documented deficiency or a noncardiovascular indication, and acute replacement can be appropriate when severe hypomagnesemia contributes to arrhythmia risk. The evidence does not support routine chronic magnesium supplementation for primary CVD prevention, CKD calcification prevention, or AMI mortality treatment. In CKD, renal clearance limits escalation; a cardiovascular-outcome-linked plasma target has not been established.

### Calcium

**Calcium supplementation has a possible vascular-harm signal, not a cardiovascular benefit.** A 2021 meta-analysis of double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trials found higher CVD risk with calcium supplementation, RR 1.15 (95% CI 1.06–1.25), and higher coronary heart disease risk, RR 1.16 (95% CI 1.05–1.28). The pooled absolute risk differences were 8.6 CVD events and 8.8 coronary events per 1,000 people. The signal was most apparent in trials using approximately 1,000 mg/day supplemental calcium and in healthy postmenopausal women, although cardiovascular outcomes were generally secondary endpoints [17].

A separate trial-level meta-analysis of three placebo-controlled trials involving 20,090 participants over 6.2 years found MI RR 1.21 (95% CI 1.01–1.44), stroke RR 1.20 (95% CI 1.00–1.43), and MI-or-stroke RR 1.16 (95% CI 1.02–1.32), with no increase in all-cause mortality, RR 1.01 (95% CI 0.90–1.12) [18].

**The calcium evidence remains contested.** A patient-level analysis of 8,151 participants found weak evidence of increased MI, HR 1.31 (95% CI 1.02–1.67), but not a statistically significant composite of MI, stroke, and death, HR 1.18 (95% CI 1.00–1.39). In contrast, the Lewis trial-level analysis of 63,563 women found no significant effect on MI, RR 1.08 (95% CI 0.93–1.25), angina or acute coronary syndrome, RR 1.09 (95% CI 0.95–1.24), or chronic coronary heart disease, RR 0.92 (95% CI 0.73–1.15) [19].

Differences in trial eligibility, open-label versus placebo-controlled designs, background personal calcium use, endpoint validation, and secondary-endpoint status explain part of the disagreement. The prudent conclusion is not that every calcium tablet causes an MI, but that calcium supplementation has no established cardiovascular benefit and a plausible, repeatedly observed vascular-risk signal.

**The intervention was oral calcium, not dietary calcium.** The Women’s Health Initiative used 1,000 mg/day calcium plus 400 IU/day vitamin D. A 500–1,000 mg supplement, especially when fasting, can raise circulating calcium toward the upper-normal range for at least eight hours. Reviews describe acute increases in blood pressure, coagulability, and calcification-related physiology, while also emphasizing that a direct causal link to coronary calcification has not been demonstrated [20].

**Safety limits practical use.** Calcium supplementation increased gastrointestinal complaints by 43% compared with placebo. In a postmenopausal study using 1,200 mg/day, 9% developed hypercalcemia and 31% hypercalciuria; Women’s Health Initiative calcium-plus-vitamin-D allocation increased renal calculi by 17%. A history of renal calculi is therefore a major contraindication to routine calcium supplementation in the cited clinical review.

**Clinical judgment for calcium.** Calcium should not be used to prevent or treat cardiovascular disease. If prescribed for bone health or documented deficiency, the clinician should distinguish dietary intake from tablets, review renal-stone and hypercalcemia risk, and avoid interpreting a transient serum-calcium rise as vascular protection.

### Iron

**Iron replacement provides the strongest treatment-level evidence.** Iron deficiency in heart failure can be absolute, reflecting inadequate stores or blood loss, or functional, in which hepcidin restricts access to stored iron. Clinical trials commonly use ferritin below 100 ng/mL, or ferritin 100–299 ng/mL with transferrin saturation below 20%, although bone-marrow comparisons suggest that TSAT below 19.8% and serum iron below 13 μg/dL may identify deficiency more accurately than the conventional criteria [9].

The distinction matters because ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and can rise with inflammation. In one observational HF cohort of 906 patients, 43% met iron-deficiency criteria using serum iron below 13 μg/dL, and 44% of those resolved their deficiency at one year even though only 4% received iron. When the broader FAIR-HF criteria were used, 63% were classified as deficient and only 21% resolved; associations with mortality changed. A low iron marker may therefore be both a target and a marker of disease severity.

**Oral iron has not improved the main functional endpoint.** IRONOUT HF randomized 225 patients with HFrEF and iron deficiency to oral iron polysaccharide 150 mg twice daily or placebo for 16 weeks. Peak oxygen uptake, six-minute walk distance, NT-proBNP, and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire scores did not improve, although serum iron, TSAT, and soluble transferrin receptor changed [21].

Oral iron remains inexpensive and physiologically regulated through intestinal absorption, but nausea, diarrhea, constipation, bloating, and metallic taste limit adherence. Proton-pump inhibitors, gut edema, hepcidin, and other factors can reduce absorption. There has not been a completed head-to-head cardiovascular-outcome trial establishing oral iron as equivalent to IV iron in HF.

**Moderate-sized IV trials improved function and symptoms.** FAIR-HF randomized 459 patients with symptomatic HF, iron deficiency, and LVEF criteria to ferric carboxymaltose or saline for 24 weeks. Ferric carboxymaltose was given as 200 mg weekly until repletion and then every four weeks. Patient global assessment and NYHA class improved, as did six-minute walk distance, quality of life, and other secondary measures, without a mortality difference.

CONFIRM-HF randomized 304 patients to ferric carboxymaltose or placebo for 52 weeks. Initial dosing was 500–2,000 mg, followed by 500 mg maintenance doses at weeks 12, 24, and 36 if deficiency persisted. The net six-minute-walk difference was 33 ± 11 m, and HF hospitalization was reduced as a secondary endpoint. EFFECT-HF randomized 172 patients to ferric carboxymaltose or standard care for 24 weeks; NYHA class and patient global assessment improved, but peak oxygen uptake did not differ significantly. These trials suggest symptomatic and functional benefit, but they were not decisive hard-outcome trials.

**Large trials produced mixed hard-outcome results.** AFFIRM-AHF enrolled 1,132 patients after acute HF hospitalization and followed them for 52 weeks. The total HF-hospitalization plus cardiovascular-death endpoint was neutral, RR 0.79 (95% CI 0.62–1.01; P=0.059), although total HF hospitalizations fell, RR 0.74 (95% CI 0.58–0.94). IRONMAN randomized 1,137 patients to ferric derisomaltose or usual care over a median 2.7 years; recurrent HF hospitalization plus cardiovascular death was neutral, RR 0.82 (95% CI 0.66–1.02; P=0.070), while a secondary composite of cardiovascular death or admission for stroke, MI, or HF was lower, RR 0.83 (95% CI 0.69–1.00). HEART-FID randomized 3,065 patients to ferric carboxymaltose or placebo for a median 1.9 years; its hierarchical death, HF-hospitalization, and six-minute-walk endpoint was not improved, with a win ratio of 1.10 (99% CI 0.99–1.23).

The 2024 JACC review therefore described the three large trials as failing their primary hard-outcome endpoints. This conflicts with the 2025 individual-participant meta-analysis, which pooled FAIR-HF, CONFIRM-HF, AFFIRM-AHF, IRONMAN, HEART-FID, and FAIR-HF2. Across 7,175 participants followed for a median of 6–32 months, IV iron reduced recurrent HF hospitalization plus cardiovascular death to RR 0.72 (95% CI 0.55–0.89) at 12 months and RR 0.81 (95% CI 0.63–0.97) over complete follow-up. Recurrent HF hospitalization alone was reduced to RR 0.69 (95% CI 0.48–0.88) at 12 months and RR 0.78 (95% CI 0.55–0.98) over complete follow-up.

Cardiovascular mortality did not reach statistical significance: HR 0.80 (95% CI 0.61–1.03) at 12 months and HR 0.87 (95% CI 0.73–1.04) over complete follow-up. All-cause mortality was likewise nonsignificant, HR 0.82 (95% CI 0.65–1.03) and HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.80–1.07). Thus, the most defensible causal judgment is fewer recurrent HF hospitalizations, with a possible composite benefit, rather than established mortality reduction.

**The benefit appears related to sustained disease-specific repletion.** The meta-analysis found no statistically significant interaction by age, ischemic versus nonischemic cause, NYHA class, eGFR, hemoglobin, ferritin, or TSAT. A sex interaction was reported: women had RR 0.98 (95% CI 0.75–1.26), although the interaction may reflect chance or inconsistent trial composition. Treatment effects were greatest during the first year, when average IV-iron dosing was approximately 2,000 mg, compared with 300–900 mg/year in years two and three. Later dosing and adherence were lower, suggesting that maintenance strategy may affect durability.

**IV iron is feasible but not risk-free.** Ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose, and iron sucrose permit repeated outpatient administration, with schedules ranging from weekly induction to doses every four weeks, four months, or six months. Monitoring generally includes ferritin, TSAT, hemoglobin, renal function, and phosphate after formulations associated with hypophosphatemia.

Serious hypersensitivity was uncommon in the cited programs: 0.3% with ferric derisomaltose versus 0.2% with iron sucrose in FERWON, and 0.8% with ferric derisomaltose versus 1.7% with ferric carboxymaltose in PHOSPHARE. Ferric carboxymaltose produces more hypophosphatemia through FGF-23-mediated phosphate wasting than ferric derisomaltose. A meta-analysis of 154 trials found higher infection risk with IV iron than oral or no iron, RR 1.16 (95% CI 1.03–1.29). The JACC review also advises caution in possible acute coronary syndrome because of endothelial effects associated with IV iron.

**Clinical judgment for iron.** IV iron is the strongest candidate for targeted cardiovascular treatment, particularly in symptomatic HFrEF or HFmrEF with iron deficiency and in selected patients with CKD. Its benefit is best described as fewer HF hospitalizations and improved symptoms or function, not proven broad prevention or mortality reduction. The indication depends on baseline iron status, disease phenotype, formulation, dose, monitoring, and reassessment after HF therapy has been optimized.

### Zinc

**Zinc deficiency is associated with cardiovascular risk, but repletion has not demonstrated event prevention.** Zinc supplementation trials have used oral zinc sulfate, gluconate, or amino-chelate preparations at 9.8–75 mg elemental zinc/day for four weeks to 12 months. Polaprezinc, a zinc–L-carnosine chelate, was studied at 75, 150, and 300 mg for 12 weeks. A stable CAD trial used zinc 30 mg/day together with selenium 150 μg/day for four months.

The target populations have included taste-disorder-related hypozincemia below 70 μg/dL, CKD and hemodialysis populations in which deficiency has been reported in 40%–78%, metabolic-risk groups, and patients with stable CAD. These are deficiency or surrogate populations, not established cardiovascular indications.

**Randomized trials show biochemical and surrogate effects.** A 27-study meta-analysis involving 1,042 zinc-treated and 974 control participants found improvements in fasting glucose, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol, but no improvement in blood pressure and no cardiovascular-event endpoint. Another meta-analysis of 24 studies involving 14,515 participants found average zinc supplementation of 39.3 mg/day reduced LDL cholesterol by 4.78 mg/dL, total cholesterol by 10.72 mg/dL, and triglycerides by 8.73 mg/dL. These are risk-factor changes, not demonstrated reductions in MI, stroke, HF, or mortality [22].

An individual-patient-data review of four randomized trials in Japanese participants with taste-disorder hypozincemia found that polaprezinc increased serum zinc by 9.08 μg/dL compared with placebo over the eight-week primary endpoint (95% CI 5.46–12.70; P<0.001). The participants’ mean baseline serum zinc was 71.0–77.8 μg/dL, and the primary deficiency threshold was below 70 μg/dL. At 300 mg polaprezinc, the proportion reaching at least 80 μg/dL increased by 63.1% (95% CI 38.2–87.9). This demonstrates correction of a biochemical deficiency, not cardiovascular efficacy [23].

**Observational AF evidence identifies a plausible trial subgroup.** A 2026 retrospective TriNetX cohort matched 61,732 adults with serum zinc below 70 μg/dL to 61,732 with zinc 70–120 μg/dL. Zinc deficiency was associated with incident AF during months one to six, HR 1.62 (95% CI 1.39–1.90), and months six to 24, HR 1.42 (95% CI 1.29–1.57). Severe deficiency below 50 μg/dL was associated with late AF HR 2.04 (95% CI 1.67–2.49), while mild-to-moderate deficiency of 50–70 μg/dL was associated with late AF HR 1.26 (95% CI 1.14–1.41). Ischemic stroke hazards were smaller, HR 1.19 early and 1.12 late; ventricular fibrillation or flutter was not significantly associated [24].

The cohort adjusted and matched for many variables, but it remained observational. Serum zinc was measured once, the reason for testing was not available, and residual confounding by diet, inflammation, kidney disease, or nutritional status remains plausible. The dose-response pattern makes severe deficiency a reasonable enrichment criterion for a trial, not a basis for prescribing zinc to prevent AF.

**Measurement and safety constrain titration.** Serum zinc has diurnal variation and depends on collection timing. In CKD, hypoalbuminemia, phosphate binders, altered absorption, uremic toxicity, and urinary or dialysis losses complicate interpretation. There is no cardiovascular-outcome-linked target concentration.

The adult zinc tolerable upper intake level is 40 mg/day. Approximately 60 mg/day for up to 10 weeks has reduced copper-containing enzyme markers, and chronic excess can cause copper deficiency, altered iron physiology, gastrointestinal symptoms, and changes in lipid metabolism. Polaprezinc trials reported gastrointestinal adverse reactions in 14.9% at 300 mg versus 3.0% with placebo over 12 weeks. Zinc should therefore be assessed together with copper status when prolonged or high-dose therapy is considered.

**Clinical judgment for zinc.** Correcting documented zinc deficiency may be appropriate for nutritional, taste, or CKD-related indications, but cardiovascular-event efficacy remains unproven. Severe deficiency, CKD or dialysis, and possible AF-risk enrichment are rational populations for randomized repletion trials. Routine zinc supplementation for primary prevention or established CAD is not supported.

### Selenium

**Selenium has a nonlinear observational rationale but null selenium-only event trials.** Selenium is incorporated into glutathione peroxidases and other selenoproteins, which provides a plausible antioxidant mechanism. Population-reference serum concentrations are often described as 70–150 μg/L, with concentrations below 100 μg/L proposed as potentially suboptimal, although neither range is a validated cardiovascular treatment target.

A meta-analysis of 16 prospective observational studies found a nonlinear relationship between blood selenium and CVD risk across 30–165 μg/L, with an inverse association within approximately 55–145 μg/L. In a PREDIMED nested case-control study of 207 incident cases and 436 controls, the highest versus lowest serum-selenium quartile was associated with lower first CVD risk, OR 0.47 (95% CI 0.27–0.81) over a mean follow-up of 4.8 years. Serum selenium was not randomized, and higher levels were associated with fish and protein intake and lower age and body mass index [7].

**Randomized selenium changes did not translate into cardiovascular benefit.** Selenium-only RCTs have used selenite or high-selenium yeast at median doses of 100–200 μg/day. A meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found that a median 200 μg/day increased blood selenium by 56.4 μg/L (95% CI 40.9–72.0), but a median 100 μg/day had no significant effect on CVD, RR 0.91 (95% CI 0.74–1.10) [25].

The Cochrane review of 12 selenium-only RCTs included 19,715 randomized participants, mostly well-nourished adults in primary-prevention settings. It found no significant effect on CVD mortality, RR 0.97 (95% CI 0.79–1.20), nonfatal CVD, RR 0.96 (95% CI 0.89–1.04), or all CVD events, RR 1.03 (95% CI 0.95–1.11) [26].

**The low-status selenium signal is non-isolating.** In a secondary post-hoc analysis of 668 elderly Swedish residents with mean baseline selenium of 67.1 μg/L, 200 μg/day selenium plus 200 mg/day coenzyme Q10 was associated with lower cardiovascular mortality among participants below 65 μg/L: 12.1% (12/99) versus 24.1% (48/199), adjusted HR 0.50 (95% CI 0.26–0.95), over a median 5.2 years. In the 65–85 μg/L group, mortality was 6.0% (6/100) versus 14.0% (27/193), adjusted HR 0.38 (95% CI 0.15–0.92).

The treatment combined selenium with coenzyme Q10, and the subgroup analysis was post hoc. In participants above 85 μg/L, the detailed results showed no significant effect, HR 0.90 (95% CI 0.25–3.27). The signal therefore supports a hypothesis about low-status combination therapy rather than selenium monotherapy.

**Safety argues against indiscriminate elevation.** Selenium supplementation produced a possible increase in type 2 diabetes, RR 1.06 (95% CI 0.97–1.15), and increased alopecia, RR 1.28 (95% CI 1.01–1.62), and grade 1–2 dermatitis, RR 1.17 (95% CI 1.00–1.35), in the cited randomized evidence. The possible U-shaped relationship means that raising an adequate selenium concentration may move a patient away from, rather than toward, the lowest-risk range.

**Clinical judgment for selenium.** Selenium replacement is reasonable for documented deficiency or a separate established indication, but selenium-only supplementation is not supported for general cardiovascular prevention or treatment. A low-status, single-agent, hard-endpoint trial could justify a subgroup indication; current combination-treatment findings do not establish one.

### Copper

**Copper biology supports balance, not indiscriminate correction.** Copper is involved in mitochondrial respiration, antioxidant defense, connective tissue, and iron metabolism. Reviews describe U-shaped associations between serum copper and cardiovascular mortality, with both low and high concentrations associated with risk [27].

Copper supplementation trials have used oral copper glycinate 2 mg/day for eight weeks. In a randomized trial of healthy adults and postmenopausal women with plasma cholesterol of 190–270 mg/dL, copper increased erythrocyte superoxide dismutase and ceruloplasmin but did not consistently improve C-reactive protein, homocysteine, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, or HDL cholesterol. Oxidized LDL fell inconsistently, and no cardiovascular events were measured [28].

**The randomized lipid evidence is null.** A systematic review of five RCTs involving 176 participants found no significant effect of copper supplementation on total cholesterol, SMD −0.05 (95% CI −0.52–0.43), LDL cholesterol, SMD 0.22 (95% CI −0.46–0.89), or HDL cholesterol, SMD 0.18 (95% CI −0.14–0.49). None of the trials measured MI, stroke, HF, arrhythmia, or cardiovascular death [29].

**Observational serum and dietary findings point in opposite directions.** A 2023 meta-analysis of observational studies associated high serum copper with MI, pooled OR approximately 1.31, and cardiovascular mortality, pooled OR approximately 1.60, comparing high with low categories. In contrast, a 2022 NHANES analysis of 14,876 adults and 655 reported MI cases found lower MI odds in the highest versus lowest dietary-copper quartile, adjusted OR 0.69 before matching and 0.70 after matching. Dietary intake, serum concentration, and total-body copper are different exposures and should not be treated as interchangeable.

**Measurement and toxicity limit clinical modulation.** Normal serum copper is reported as 10–25 μmol/L, but copper status is not routinely assessed and no single biomarker is reliable. Ceruloplasmin carries more than 95% of plasma copper in healthy people, but inflammation, estrogen, pregnancy, and cancer alter its concentration. Chronic high copper exposure can cause gastrointestinal symptoms and liver damage; excessive zinc can induce copper deficiency.

**Clinical judgment for copper.** Copper supplementation should be reserved for documented deficiency or a disease-specific indication. Serum-copper reduction for cardiovascular prevention has no established clinical regimen, and routine copper supplementation or chelation should not be proposed for unselected CVD.

## 4. Clinical evidence for metal reduction, chelation, and exposure remediation

### EDTA chelation after myocardial infarction

**TACT produced a modest secondary-prevention signal.** TACT randomized 1,708 adults aged at least 50 years with a prior MI to 40 intravenous disodium-EDTA or placebo infusions, with median follow-up of 55 months. The primary composite occurred in 222 of 839 EDTA recipients (26%) versus 261 of 869 placebo recipients (30%), HR 0.82 (95% CI 0.69–0.99; P=0.035). All-cause death was not reduced, HR 0.93 (95% CI 0.70–1.25; P=0.64) [12].

A prespecified diabetes subgroup of 633 participants had a larger apparent effect: 25% versus 38%, HR 0.59 (95% CI 0.44–0.79). This generated interest in a target-enriched post-MI strategy, but the result required replication.

**TACT did not isolate a metal-specific mechanism.** The factorial oral regimen contained calcium 500 mg, magnesium 500 mg, zinc 20 mg, selenium 200 μg, and copper 2 mg, alongside EDTA or placebo. The double-active comparison produced HR 0.74 (95% CI 0.57–0.95), but the bundled oral mineral intervention cannot be separated into individual-ion effects, and the high-dose vitamins and minerals alone did not reduce cardiovascular events. Pill discontinuation and dropout further weakened confidence in the factorial result.

**TACT2 failed to reproduce the cardiovascular benefit.** TACT2 enrolled 1,000 adults aged at least 50 years with diabetes and prior MI between 2016 and 2023; 959 received infusions. After 40 EDTA or placebo treatments, cardiovascular events occurred in 35.6% of the EDTA group and 35.7% of the placebo group. Blood lead fell by 61% with EDTA, while urine cadmium increased immediately after treatment [2].

The divergence could reflect differences in lead exposure, disease severity, baseline risk, adherence, or mechanisms unrelated to toxic-metal removal. A cardiovascular event reduction cannot be inferred from successful lead mobilization.

**The pooled EDTA evidence remains weak.** A Cochrane review of five randomized trials involving 1,993 participants, including coronary and peripheral vascular disease, found no clear differences in mortality, MI, stroke, ankle-brachial index, walking distance, or quality of life. For coronary disease, all-cause mortality was RR 0.97 (95% CI 0.73–1.28; 1,792 participants), MI was RR 0.81 (95% CI 0.57–1.14; 1,792 participants), and stroke was RR 0.88 (95% CI 0.40–1.92; 1,867 participants). Certainty ranged from low to very low [30].

**Delivery burden and harm are clinically important.** A coronary-disease EDTA course commonly requires 20–40 weekly infusions lasting several hours, often with high-dose vitamin and mineral pills. EDTA chelation for coronary disease is not FDA-approved. Important risks include hypocalcemia, kidney damage, and loss of essential metals. Serious adverse-event rates were 11.9% versus 14.6% in TACT and 16.8% versus 16.6% in TACT2, but trial tolerability does not establish benefit or justify unsupervised use.

**Clinical judgment for EDTA.** EDTA should not be used for routine primary or secondary cardiovascular prevention. A future benefit claim would require a prespecified metal-burden subgroup, standardized blood or tissue measurements, a durable burden reduction, and fewer MI, stroke, HF, or cardiovascular-death events in an adequately powered randomized trial.

### Phlebotomy and erythrocytapheresis

**Polycythemia vera is the clearest hematocrit-reduction indication.** Therapeutic phlebotomy targeting hematocrit below 45% has been associated with lower cardiovascular morbidity and fewer major thrombotic events in polycythemia vera. Here the relevant abnormality is excessive red-cell mass, not an isolated plasma iron concentration, and the endpoint is thrombotic cardiovascular morbidity.

Phlebotomy usually removes 500 mL every one to two weeks during induction, followed by maintenance sessions every two to four months or according to hematologic status. A 250 mL volume may be used for people with small body mass, anemia, or cardiac or pulmonary disease. Hemoglobin should be checked at each visit; intervals are lengthened below 120 g/L and treatment temporarily stopped below 110 g/L in the EASL haemochromatosis guidance [31].

**Hereditary hemochromatosis evidence is clinically persuasive but cardiovascularly sparse.** EASL recommends ferritin below 50 μg/L during induction and 50–100 μg/L during maintenance, with 500 mL removal every one to two weeks initially. These targets prevent iron accumulation and organ injury, but they are not validated cardiovascular-prevention targets.

A systematic review identified 24 HH studies involving 5,994 patients, of which only three were RCTs; 19 of 21 nonrandomized intervention studies had serious risk of bias. Four studies involving 4,033 patients commented on cardiovascular disease, but only two reported treatment outcomes. In a questionnaire study of 2,851 patients, heart-fluttering symptoms improved in 42 of 679 participants (6.2%) and worsened in 69 (10.1%). A death-certificate analysis of 1,085 treated patients reported cardiovascular mortality below that of the general population, standardized mortality ratio 0.27 (95% CI 0.1–0.5), but this was retrospective and the symptom and mortality data do not establish causal cardiovascular benefit [32].

**Erythrocytapheresis reaches biochemical targets faster.** In a post hoc comparison of HFE C282Y-homozygous patients with iron overload, erythrocytapheresis reached persistent TSAT below 70% in a median 5.5 weeks and 2.0 procedures, compared with 19.0 weeks and 16.0 procedures with whole-blood phlebotomy. The sample was small, with 18 erythrocytapheresis and 15 phlebotomy participants, and no cardiovascular endpoint was measured [33].

Phlebotomy commonly causes tiredness, fainting, appetite loss, and needle intolerance in 19%–52% of HH patients. Up to 25% of erythrocytapheresis collections have complications, mainly mild citrate toxicity, hypotension-related reactions, or vein rupture. Hemoglobin, ferritin, TSAT, vital signs, hydration, and symptoms determine whether continued reduction is safe.

**Iron chelation has a narrower role.** EASL reserves deferoxamine, deferasirox, and deferiprone for selected patients who cannot undergo phlebotomy, including inaccessible veins, needle phobia, or life-threatening cardiac iron overload. Evidence in HH is very limited; a phase II deferasirox study included 10 patients. In transfusion-dependent thalassemia, a systematic review of 11 studies and 12 reports, including three open-label RCTs, found that combined oral deferiprone and deferasirox generally reduced ferritin and sometimes improved cardiac MRI T2*, but no study measured cardiovascular events. Follow-up was six to 36 months, adherence ranged from 66.7% to 100% in four studies, creatinine increased in 6%–25%, and adverse effects included gastrointestinal symptoms, transaminase elevation, proteinuria, neutropenia, and occasional treatment discontinuation [34].

**Clinical judgment for blood removal.** Phlebotomy and erythrocytapheresis are feasible and clinically justified when a defined hematologic or iron-overload disorder is present. Their cardiovascular value is strongest for thrombotic-risk control in polycythemia vera and indirect for hemochromatosis. They should not be used as general cardiovascular prevention in people without erythrocytosis or iron overload.

### Toxic-metal remediation and chelation

**Exposure associations are strong enough to justify prevention, not to prove treatment efficacy.** Chronic cadmium exposure is associated with cardiovascular mortality, and environmental reviews link arsenic and lead with hypertension, stroke, CHD, and cardiovascular mortality. A Shenzhen case-control study of 1,277 pairs found ischemic-stroke OR 1.88 (95% CI 1.25–2.81) for plasma arsenic above 2.40 μg/L versus below 0.66 μg/L. These findings support source control and biomonitoring in exposed populations.

**Lead reduction has not translated into an EDTA event benefit.** TACT2 is the strongest clinical example: blood lead fell 61%, but cardiovascular events were identical between EDTA and placebo. A retrospective CAD study of 14 patients receiving a median 51 EDTA infusions reported urinary lead 36% lower than baseline, with no control group and no MI, stroke, or mortality endpoint. The finding shows biomarker change, not clinical efficacy.

**Mercury biomarker reduction has also been short-term.** A randomized phase-IIb trial enrolled 36 Hispanic male Ecuadorian gold miners with urinary mercury above 15 μg/L. NBMI at 100 or 300 mg/day for 14 days produced a dose-normalized 30% reduction in urinary mercury. Post hoc regression estimated plasma-mercury reductions of approximately 2 μg/L at day 15 and 4 μg/L at day 45, but the plasma estimates depended on the model, mercury was not speciated, and no cardiovascular endpoint was measured [35].

**Exposure removal is safer than indiscriminate binding.** Lead paint, leaded gasoline, contaminated pipes, occupational sources, and smoking are actionable sources. Cadmium exposure may arise from tobacco, occupational settings, food, or contaminated soil; arsenic assessment requires urine speciation to distinguish inorganic arsenic from seafood-derived arsenicals; cadmium assessment commonly uses creatinine-normalized urine cadmium and tubular-effect markers. The intervention should target the source and chemical form rather than simply lowering a single blood value.

**Clinical judgment for toxic metals.** Source remediation is the preferred chronic intervention. Chelation belongs in specialist toxicology care when symptoms, exposure history, and metal-specific laboratory results align. There is no controlled cardiovascular-outcome evidence supporting chelation of asymptomatic people solely to reduce future MI or stroke risk.

## 5. Comparative efficacy across cardiovascular prevention and treatment settings

### Primary prevention: associations exceed intervention evidence

**The prevention evidence is strongest for exposure avoidance and weakest for supplementation.** Environmental studies consistently provide a public-health rationale for reducing lead, cadmium, mercury, and arsenic exposure. Yet controlled trials linking source remediation or chelation to fewer cardiovascular events are sparse. The most direct controlled toxic-metal study, TACT2, reduced lead but not cardiovascular events.

For essential metals, higher circulating magnesium, selenium, zinc, or lower copper associations may identify healthier diets, better organ function, or lower inflammation rather than causal treatment effects. The magnesium association of RR 0.70 per 0.2 mmol/L, PREDIMED selenium OR 0.47 for the highest versus lowest quartile, and observational zinc-AF HR 1.62 early are important hypotheses. None demonstrates that raising the measured concentration will prevent an event.

**Calcium is the principal prevention caution.** Unlike magnesium, selenium, zinc, or copper, calcium supplementation has a possible randomized vascular-harm signal. The most consistent clinical implication is therefore not supplementation but restraint: use calcium for a bone or deficiency indication, distinguish tablets from dietary calcium, and assess renal-stone and hypercalcemia risk.

**Diet remains a broader intervention than metal manipulation.** The Mediterranean-diet biomarker trial changed several urinary minerals and plasma selenium over two weeks but measured no CVD endpoint. The broader cardiovascular evidence for Mediterranean dietary patterns cannot be attributed to these metal changes without a factorial trial that isolates metal status from fiber, polyphenols, fatty acids, alcohol, sodium, weight, and other dietary components.

### Established disease: targeted treatment can be different

**Iron-deficient heart failure has the strongest causal signal.** Six randomized trials in 7,175 patients found fewer recurrent HF hospitalizations plus cardiovascular death with IV iron, especially during the first year. The effect was mainly hospitalization-related, with mortality confidence intervals crossing one. This is a conditional treatment indication tied to heart failure, iron status, route, formulation, and follow-up.

**Acute magnesium treatment has not improved AMI survival.** IV magnesium can reduce ventricular fibrillation but increases hypotension and bradycardia, and the Cochrane review does not support continued routine use for AMI. Magnesium remains relevant when severe hypomagnesemia or a specific arrhythmic indication is present, but that is correction of an acute electrolyte abnormality rather than a general metal-modulation strategy.

**EDTA is less actionable than IV iron.** TACT’s post-MI composite benefit, its diabetes subgroup, the factorial mineral signal, the negative pooled Cochrane review, and TACT2’s event-null replication produce a discordant evidence base. The treatment is lengthy, invasive, not FDA-approved for coronary disease, and biologically nonspecific. It ranks below IV iron because the target metal mechanism is uncertain and replication failed.

**Blood removal is highly disease-specific.** Hematocrit below 45% in polycythemia vera is linked to lower cardiovascular morbidity, making phlebotomy part of a hematologic treatment pathway. In hereditary hemochromatosis, phlebotomy clearly reduces iron burden and prevents organ injury, but cardiovascular endpoint evidence is sparse and confounded. In transfusional iron overload, chelators can improve ferritin, liver iron, and cardiac MRI surrogates, but those studies do not prove fewer cardiovascular events.

### Cardiovascular subtypes do not respond identically

**Coronary disease and MI.** Calcium supplementation may increase MI risk in some analyses; IV magnesium has no mortality benefit; zinc and selenium trials in stable CAD have been short and surrogate-based; TACT and TACT2 address post-MI secondary prevention but conflict. These interventions should not be grouped under a single “metal-lowering” or “metal-repletion” effect.

**Heart failure.** Iron deficiency is common and IV iron has the strongest evidence, particularly in HFrEF and recently decompensated HF. Oral iron was neutral in IRONOUT HF. Magnesium supplementation did not slow CAC in CKD, and zinc or selenium supplementation has not demonstrated HF-event benefit. HFpEF remains underrepresented in IV-iron trials, and CKD modifies both metal handling and treatment safety.

**Stroke.** Calcium-plus-vitamin-D trials reported a possible stroke increase, while observational zinc, selenium, copper, arsenic, lead, and cadmium studies are inconsistent or confounded. Zinc deficiency in the 2026 cohort was associated with modestly higher ischemic-stroke hazards, but the association was not an intervention effect. No metal supplementation has demonstrated stroke prevention.

**Arrhythmia.** Severe hypomagnesemia can promote atrial and supraventricular beats, and IV magnesium may reduce ventricular fibrillation in AMI at the cost of hypotension and bradycardia. Zinc deficiency was associated with incident AF in a large retrospective cohort but not ventricular fibrillation or flutter. Hemochromatosis-related palpitations and arrhythmias are clinically plausible but poorly measured in iron-reduction studies.

**Hypertension and peripheral vascular disease.** Magnesium supplementation lowers systolic pressure by approximately 1.25 mmHg and diastolic pressure by 1.40 mmHg in an umbrella meta-analysis of 8,610 participants, but heterogeneity was very high and the endpoint was blood pressure rather than CVD events. EDTA trials in peripheral vascular disease did not clearly improve walking distance or ankle-brachial index. Lead and arsenic exposure are associated with hypertension, making exposure reduction sensible, but cardiovascular event prevention has not been directly demonstrated.

### Endpoint hierarchy changes the conclusion

**A biomarker is not a clinical event.** The evidence spans metal concentrations, urinary excretion, ferritin, TSAT, hematocrit, CAC, T50 calcification propensity, blood pressure, lipids, glucose, peak VO₂, six-minute walk distance, NYHA class, quality of life, HF hospitalization, MI, stroke, cardiovascular death, and all-cause mortality.

A treatment that raises plasma magnesium but does not slow CAC has not shown vascular benefit. A treatment that raises selenium but leaves CVD RR near 1.0 has demonstrated target engagement without event prevention. A treatment that lowers lead but leaves TACT2 cardiovascular events unchanged has demonstrated biomarker mobilization without clinical benefit. IV iron is different because its strongest evidence includes recurrent HF hospitalization and cardiovascular death, although the mortality component remains uncertain.

**The mechanism must match the compartment and endpoint.** A serum concentration may not represent intracellular availability. A urinary excretion increase may indicate mobilization without durable tissue reduction. Ferritin may be an acute-phase marker rather than a pure store measure. Hematocrit is a proxy for thrombosis risk in polycythemia vera, not a general iron target. A causal claim becomes credible only when the intervention changes the relevant compartment, the change persists, the endpoint is clinically meaningful, and safety remains acceptable.

## 6. Feasibility, monitoring, safety, and clinical implementation

### Measurement methods and target selection

**Clinical assays are disease-specific rather than universal.** Reported measurement methods include plasma or serum magnesium, atomic absorption for zinc and selenium, ICP-MS for selenium and copper, ferritin and TSAT for HF iron status, blood lead, creatinine-normalized urine cadmium, and urine arsenic speciation. Each method answers a different question.

| Target | Main clinical measurement | Disease-specific range or threshold | What remains unresolved |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | Plasma or serum magnesium | Usual circulating distribution 0.75–0.96 mmol/L; hypomagnesemia below 0.65 mmol/L | Cardiovascular benefit from a higher chronic target |
| Calcium | Serum total or ionized calcium | Trial supplements raised intake by 1,000–1,200 mg/day; no CVD target | Whether transient calcium peaks cause vascular events |
| Iron | Ferritin, TSAT, serum iron, hemoglobin | HF ferritin <100 ng/mL or 100–299 ng/mL with TSAT <20%; HH ferritin targets | Best HF definition of tissue iron deficiency and maintenance target |
| Zinc | Serum or plasma zinc, with albumin and timing | Deficiency often below 70 μg/dL; severe deficiency below 50 μg/dL in AF cohort | Cardiovascular-outcome target and tissue-status assay |
| Selenium | Serum or plasma selenium by ICP-MS or atomic absorption | Population reference 70–150 μg/L; proposed low-status bands below 65–100 μg/L | Causal treatment threshold and safe upper cardiovascular range |
| Copper | Serum copper, ceruloplasmin, sometimes ICP-MS | Serum copper often 10–25 μmol/L | Reliable status biomarker and cardiovascular target |
| Lead | Whole-blood lead | Toxicology-specific thresholds; 45 μg/dL supports pediatric chelation consideration | Adult cardiovascular treatment threshold |
| Cadmium | Urine cadmium normalized to creatinine; blood cadmium | Exposure-specific reference distributions | Benefit threshold for reduction and role of chelation |
| Mercury | Blood, plasma, urine, and chemical speciation | Exposure- and species-specific interpretation | Cardiovascular threshold and durable treatment target |
| Arsenic | Urine speciation for inorganic arsenic, MMA, DMA | Species- and exposure-specific interpretation | Cardiovascular target after source removal |

**No universal cardiovascular target has been linked to better outcomes.** The available evidence supports disease-specific operating ranges for IV iron, hematocrit in polycythemia vera, ferritin in hemochromatosis, and selected toxicology thresholds. It does not provide a single cardiovascular-outcome-linked plasma or serum target for magnesium, calcium, zinc, selenium, copper, lead, cadmium, mercury, or arsenic.

A general target range and titration algorithm would require standardized compartment-specific assays, baseline-status stratification, dose-response trials, target-guided treatment, and hard cardiovascular endpoints. Until those conditions are met, a measured value should be interpreted alongside the disease, organ function, exposure context, and treatment objective.

### Dose titration and administration

**Fixed doses dominate supplementation evidence.** Magnesium, calcium, zinc, selenium, and copper trials generally used fixed oral doses rather than titration to a cardiovascular target. The MAGiCAL-CKD magnesium trial used 30 mmol elemental magnesium/day for 12 months; the competing CKD trial titrated toward 1.04–1.25 mmol/L, but the achieved concentration was lower and the study was open-label. Selenium trials used 100–300 μg/day, zinc trials 9.8–75 mg/day, and calcium trials approximately 1,000 mg/day, without validated cardiovascular titration rules.

**Disease-specific titration is more developed for iron and blood removal.** HF IV-iron protocols use ferritin, TSAT, hemoglobin, formulation, body size, and reassessment to determine induction and maintenance dosing. Examples include ferric carboxymaltose 200 mg weekly until repletion, 500–2,000 mg initially with 500 mg maintenance, or ferric derisomaltose on days 0 and 28 and then every four months if deficiency persists. Phlebotomy uses hematocrit, ferritin, TSAT, hemoglobin, volume, and symptoms to set intervals.

**Chelation requires specialist dosing.** EDTA courses commonly involve 20–40 weekly infusions. Iron chelators require renal, hepatic, hematologic, and dose monitoring; oral deferasirox and deferiprone regimens in transfusional iron overload have different schedules and toxicities. Toxic-metal chelation depends on metal species, exposure timing, symptoms, and validated biomonitoring rather than a general “detoxification” target.

### Laboratory monitoring

**Monitoring must follow the intervention’s mechanism and harm profile.**

- IV iron: ferritin, TSAT, hemoglobin, serum iron when appropriate, renal function, phosphate after formulations associated with FGF-23-mediated phosphate loss, hypersensitivity, infection, and clinical HF outcomes.
- Oral magnesium: serial magnesium, renal function, gastrointestinal tolerance, blood pressure, heart rate, and symptoms of accumulation in CKD.
- Calcium: serum calcium, renal function, urinary calcium or stone history when clinically indicated, and the noncardiovascular indication for treatment.
- Zinc: serum zinc under standardized collection conditions, albumin and inflammatory context, copper status during prolonged treatment, gastrointestinal effects, and adherence.
- Selenium: serum or plasma selenium using a consistent assay and timing, dose exposure, glucose status, dermatologic effects, and signs of excess.
- Copper: serum copper and ceruloplasmin interpreted with inflammation, liver function, pregnancy, estrogen status, or cancer context; zinc exposure should be reviewed.
- EDTA: creatinine, calcium, magnesium, zinc, blood pressure, infusion reactions, and essential-metal depletion.
- Phlebotomy: hemoglobin at every visit, ferritin and TSAT, volume status, vital signs, fatigue, fainting, and iron-depletion trajectory.
- Toxic-metal remediation: blood lead, urine cadmium with creatinine normalization, urine arsenic speciation, blood or urine mercury with species where relevant, renal tubular markers, and exposure-source reassessment.

### Adherence and implementation

**Oral access does not guarantee sustained use.** MAGiCAL-CKD achieved 97% pill adherence under trial conditions, but 47% experienced loose stool or diarrhea and 11% reduced dose. In the Swedish selenium-plus-coenzyme-Q10 study, 129 of 441 participants (29.1%) discontinued, mainly because of tablet or capsule burden. TACT also had substantial pill discontinuation and dropout, which weakened the certainty of its bundled mineral result.

**IV iron shifts burden from pills to services.** Infusion visits, venous access, monitoring, formulation selection, and repeated dosing are required. The 2025 meta-analysis found approximately 2,000 mg IV iron in the first year but only 300–900 mg/year in years two and three, reflecting lower later dosing and adherence. This difference matters because an initially successful repletion strategy may not maintain tissue availability.

**Procedural reduction requires repeated contact.** Phlebotomy generally requires 500 mL removal every one to two weeks during induction, followed by maintenance sessions. Smaller volumes may be needed in cardiac or pulmonary disease. Erythrocytapheresis reduces the number of procedures but requires specialized equipment, trained staff, vascular access, longer visits, and management of citrate and hemodynamic effects.

**EDTA has the highest delivery burden among the cardiovascular strategies.** Twenty to 40 weekly infusions lasting several hours, laboratory monitoring, and the absence of FDA approval for coronary disease make routine implementation difficult. Over-the-counter chelation products are not a substitute for toxicology or cardiology care and can cause harm.

### Safety of increasing metal concentrations

**Magnesium requires renal caution.** Oral magnesium commonly produces diarrhea and loose stool. Reduced kidney function limits clearance and may permit hypermagnesemia, with hypotension, syncope, bradycardia, or arrhythmia at high concentrations. Acute IV magnesium may reduce ventricular fibrillation while increasing profound hypotension and bradycardia. A CKD trial did not test sustained concentrations above 1.50 mmol/L.

**Calcium may create vascular and renal risk.** Calcium supplements can produce transient serum-calcium and blood-pressure rises, hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, gastrointestinal complaints, and renal calculi. The vascular risk signal is not uniform across analyses, but it is sufficient to rule out calcium as a preventive cardiovascular supplement.

**Iron bypasses physiologic regulation.** IV iron may cause hypersensitivity, infection risk, endothelial effects, hypophosphatemia, and tissue exposure to reactive iron. Ferric carboxymaltose causes more hypophosphatemia than ferric derisomaltose in the cited studies. Repeated long-term exposure has less evidence than short-term safety, and iron should not be given to people without a justified indication.

**Zinc can induce copper deficiency.** High-dose zinc causes gastrointestinal symptoms and can reduce copper-containing enzyme markers at approximately 60 mg/day. The adult upper limit is 40 mg/day. Prolonged zinc therapy should therefore include attention to copper, hemoglobin, iron physiology, and medication or phosphate-binder interactions.

**Selenium has a narrow therapeutic margin.** Potential harms include alopecia, dermatitis, and a possible increase in type 2 diabetes. Because observational evidence suggests a U-shaped concentration-risk relationship, increasing selenium above adequate status may create harm rather than benefit.

**Copper excess damages the liver.** Chronic high copper exposure can produce gastrointestinal symptoms and liver injury. Copper deficiency may impair mitochondrial and connective-tissue function, but serum copper is confounded and neither raising nor lowering it has a validated cardiovascular target.

### Safety of lowering or removing metal burden

**EDTA can remove essential ions.** Hypocalcemia and kidney injury are the most important serious risks. EDTA also mobilizes magnesium, zinc, iron, manganese, and copper, so successful urinary excretion may create deficiency or electrolyte consequences. Trial safety rates do not eliminate the need for renal and calcium monitoring.

**Phlebotomy can cause hypovolemia and anemia.** Tiredness, fainting, appetite loss, and needle intolerance are common. Hemoglobin below 11 g/dL increases hypovolemia or anemia symptoms and makes iron depletion less efficient. Cardiac or pulmonary disease may require smaller removal volumes and longer intervals.

**Erythrocytapheresis has procedure-specific hazards.** Citrate toxicity, hypotension, vein rupture, vascular-access difficulty, and cost limit use. Its faster biochemical effect does not establish superior cardiovascular outcomes.

**Iron chelators require organ monitoring.** Deferasirox can increase creatinine, proteinuria, gastrointestinal symptoms, and transaminases; deferiprone can cause neutropenia or agranulocytosis and also affects liver enzymes and gastrointestinal tolerance. These risks are particularly important in patients with renal or hepatic disease and in those receiving multiple cardiometabolic medications.

**Toxic-metal chelation should not replace source removal.** Chelation can mobilize metals into transiently higher circulating or urinary concentrations, remove essential ions, and produce renal or hepatic toxicity. Cadmium chelation is generally discouraged in routine practice; mercury and arsenic treatment depends on species and timing; lead treatment depends on symptoms, age, and blood lead concentration.

### A status-first clinical pathway

| Step | Question | Proceed when supported | Stop or qualify when absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify the compartment | Is the result plasma, serum, whole blood, urine, ferritin/TSAT, or hematocrit? | Use a compartment-appropriate assay and repeat questionable results | Do not equate serum, plasma, urine, and whole blood |
| 2. Establish the indication | Is there iron-deficient HF, polycythemia, iron overload, severe deficiency, or confirmed toxic exposure? | Treat within the disease-specific pathway | Do not use an abnormal value alone as a CVD indication |
| 3. Select route and target | Is there a validated dose or disease-specific target? | Use IV iron, phlebotomy, or toxicology-directed therapy when indicated | Avoid extrapolating a surrogate target to general CVD |
| 4. Monitor benefit and harm | Are clinical endpoints, metal markers, organ function, interactions, and adherence being followed? | Continue only when benefit and safety remain favorable | Adjust or stop for toxicity, nonresponse, or misclassification |
| 5. Reassess the evidence | Has randomized hard-outcome benefit been replicated in the relevant subgroup? | Consider broader use only after replication | Retain an indication-specific or investigational judgment |

## 7. Overall clinical judgment and research priorities

### Overall judgment

**The answer for primary prevention is no for routine metal manipulation.** Higher circulating magnesium, selenium, zinc, or lower copper may predict lower cardiovascular risk in selected cohorts, but observational gradients do not demonstrate that supplementation changes events. Randomized selenium-only trials were null; magnesium increased plasma concentration without slowing CAC; zinc changed surrogate markers without event data; copper trials were small and lipid-neutral. Calcium supplementation has a possible MI and stroke harm signal. Toxic-metal exposure reduction is rational, but cardiovascular-event trials are lacking.

**The answer for treatment is conditional.** IV iron in iron-deficient HFrEF or selected HF patients is the strongest indication-specific strategy, with evidence for fewer recurrent HF hospitalizations and a composite reduction that has not translated into statistically demonstrated mortality benefit. Phlebotomy is standard for polycythemia vera and hereditary hemochromatosis, but cardiovascular benefit is disease-specific and better established for hematocrit-related thrombosis than for iron-mediated cardiomyopathy. Chelation is appropriate for selected toxicology or iron-overload indications, not routine coronary prevention.

**The relevant clinical principle is status first, disease second, route third.** A low plasma value alone is not enough. The clinician should establish the metal, compartment, cause of abnormality, cardiovascular phenotype, renal and hepatic function, route, and monitoring plan. The intended change should be proportional to the abnormality and linked to a clinically meaningful endpoint.

### Evidence ranking by clinical actionability

**First: IV iron for iron-deficient heart failure.** This strategy has the most coherent relationship between a defined abnormality, a disease-specific mechanism, randomized treatment evidence, clinical endpoints, and guideline support. Its benefit is conditional on correct diagnosis, formulation, dosing, and monitoring.

**Second: phlebotomy or erythrocytapheresis for polycythemia or iron overload.** These interventions reliably change hematocrit or iron burden and are clinically feasible within specialist pathways. Cardiovascular evidence is strongest for hematocrit control in polycythemia vera; hemochromatosis cardiovascular evidence is sparse and mostly observational.

**Third: confirmed toxic-metal exposure remediation.** Removing lead, cadmium, mercury, or arsenic sources is biologically and public-health rational, particularly because exposure is associated with hypertension, stroke, MI, and cardiovascular mortality. The benefit is prevention-oriented and source-directed; controlled event evidence for chelation remains weak.

**Fourth: correction of severe essential-metal deficiency.** Magnesium, zinc, selenium, copper, and calcium should be corrected when a clinically meaningful deficiency or separate indication exists. The evidence for cardiovascular event reduction is not established except for the specific IV-iron heart-failure context. Severe zinc deficiency and low selenium status are reasonable trial-enrichment groups, not routine CVD-treatment indications.

**Fifth: EDTA for coronary disease and unselected mineral supplementation.** TACT, TACT2, and the Cochrane review do not provide consistent support for EDTA. Routine calcium, magnesium, zinc, selenium, or copper supplementation in an unselected cardiovascular population lacks a favorable evidence-risk balance; calcium has the clearest potential for harm.

### Plausible beneficiary subgroups

**Iron-deficient HFrEF is the most credible subgroup.** Candidate participants should have heart failure with reduced or mildly reduced ejection fraction, symptoms or recent decompensation, and low ferritin, TSAT, or serum iron using standardized criteria. Patients with CKD may be particularly relevant because functional deficiency and impaired absorption coexist, but renal function must be integrated into safety monitoring.

**Severe magnesium deficiency may justify correction.** Patients with magnesium below 0.65 mmol/L, arrhythmia risk, gastrointestinal losses, or medication-related depletion may benefit from replacement for electrical stability. A chronic cardiovascular-prevention effect among people with usual-range magnesium remains unsupported.

**Severe zinc deficiency merits an intervention trial.** Serum zinc below 50 μg/dL, CKD or dialysis, malnutrition, or recurrent deficiency could define an enriched population. The 2026 AF cohort provides a risk gradient, with late AF HR 2.04 for severe deficiency and 1.26 for 50–70 μg/dL, but supplementation must be tested rather than inferred.

**Low selenium status may modify response.** The Swedish selenium-plus-coenzyme-Q10 signal was concentrated below 85 μg/L, particularly below 65 μg/L, while selenium-only trials were null. A future study should test selenium alone, stratified by baseline status, rather than treat the combination result as proof of selenium efficacy.

**Disease-specific reduction groups remain distinct.** Polycythemia vera with elevated hematocrit, hereditary hemochromatosis with high TSAT and ferritin, transfusion-dependent thalassemia with cardiac iron, and symptomatic patients with confirmed toxic-metal exposure have clear reasons to alter metal burden. Their outcomes should not be extrapolated to patients with ordinary coronary risk.

### Evidence gaps and future trial requirements

**Trials should enroll by baseline metal status.** A study of supplementation in replete participants can dilute a deficiency-specific benefit and expose people to toxicity. Future magnesium, zinc, selenium, copper, and calcium trials should prespecify deficiency or low-status strata and analyze treatment interaction by baseline concentration.

**Trials should standardize the measured compartment.** Plasma, serum, whole blood, urine, ferritin, TSAT, hematocrit, and tissue imaging should not be treated as equivalent. Assay platform, collection time, fasting state, inflammatory status, albumin, renal function, and sample handling should be prespecified and harmonized.

**Trials should compare fixed dosing with target-guided dosing.** Fixed-dose supplementation demonstrates delivery but not whether a target was reached. Target-guided designs should compare a fixed regimen with titration to a prespecified, physiologically justified range, while monitoring tissue or functional markers where circulating values are inadequate.

**Trials should use single-agent comparisons where attribution matters.** TACT combined EDTA with calcium, magnesium, zinc, selenium, and copper pills, making metal-specific attribution impossible. Future chelation trials should separate chelation from oral minerals and include a placebo-controlled, factorial design when multiple components are necessary.

**Trials should prioritize hard cardiovascular endpoints.** Primary outcomes should include MI, ischemic stroke, HF hospitalization, cardiovascular death, incident or recurrent AF, ventricular arrhythmia, and all-cause mortality according to the disease setting. CAC, T50, blood pressure, lipids, glucose, peak VO₂, six-minute walk distance, and metal concentration should be secondary or mechanistic endpoints rather than substitutes for clinical events.

**Trials should follow participants long enough to detect both benefit and harm.** Acute studies should capture hypotension, bradycardia, electrolyte shifts, arrhythmia, and early events. Chronic studies should follow renal function, hepatic function, infection, vascular events, malignancy-related signals where relevant, bone effects, adherence, and cumulative exposure over multiple years.

**Trials should replicate disagreement directly.** TACT versus TACT2 requires prespecified comparison of lead burden, disease severity, diabetes, renal function, treatment adherence, and essential-metal mobilization. The IV-iron field requires trials using more accurate iron-deficiency criteria, target-guided maintenance, and adequate representation of HFpEF and CKD.

**Future work should measure implementation, not only efficacy.** Real-world adherence, infusion access, venous access, pill burden, laboratory frequency, cost, formulation switching, and discontinuation should be recorded. A treatment that works only under intensive trial supervision may not produce the same population benefit.

**Evidence boundary.** “Plasma metal” in this article includes serum when the source used serum, while whole blood, urine, ferritin, TSAT, and hematocrit are reported as distinct measures. RR, OR, and HR retain their source-specific denominators and follow-up periods; CAC and blood-pressure findings are surrogate outcomes. Most non-iron evidence is observational, surrogate-based, short, underpowered, or confounded by bundled interventions; values such as the magnesium 0.13 mmol/L increase, selenium 56.4 μg/L increase, calcium RR estimates, IV-iron RRs, and TACT2 event proportions are source-reported measurements or estimates rather than newly calculated results.

A broader cardiovascular role for plasma-metal modulation would require replicated randomized benefit in a prespecified baseline-status subgroup, a standardized assay and compartment, a durable and safe target change, target-guided dosing, and clinically meaningful reductions in MI, stroke, HF hospitalization, arrhythmia, or cardiovascular death. Until those conditions are met, metal modulation should remain status-specific care rather than a general cardiovascular therapy.
