# Smart Home Product Development, 2026–2035: The Products Most Likely to Shape the Industry

**Integrated energy systems lead the outlook:** The strongest smart-home product opportunity is a coordinated system that manages heating and cooling, solar generation, batteries, electric-vehicle charging, hot water, and electricity tariffs. Interoperable AI security, natural-language home control, and increasingly autonomous cleaning appliances form the next major wave. Aging-in-place systems and smart-water products should become increasingly important through 2035, with adoption shaped more strongly by local infrastructure, affordability, regulation, and trust.

**Useful autonomy is the common direction:** Successful products will complete valuable household tasks with less supervision, while preserving understandable controls, privacy, and reliable basic operation. General-purpose humanoids and robotic kitchens belong on a higher-uncertainty watchlist; task-specific automation already has a stronger commercial foundation.

## The industry baseline: substantial adoption, several distinct markets

### Market size depends on what is being counted

**The clearest hardware baseline is $159.5 billion:** Statista projects worldwide smart-home hardware revenue of US$159.5 billion in 2026. Its definition covers physical, network-connected products sold to private consumers to monitor, control, or regulate household functions; it excludes software, subscriptions, platforms, installation, maintenance, smartphones, tablets, and business-to-business sales. Smart meters also sit outside this particular market boundary. [7]

**The wider industry includes supporting services:** Product development also depends on software platforms, monitoring subscriptions, installers, utilities, and connected-home services. This report therefore examines the wider residential ecosystem while retaining each financial estimate’s original scope. Solar systems, EVs, water infrastructure, and healthcare are considered where connected controls coordinate them—not counted wholesale as smart-home revenue.

The relatively restrained hardware outlook makes replacement cycles, software differentiation, and customer retention strategically important. The broader forecasts support substantial investment interest, but their difference from hardware growth does not, by itself, quantify how much future revenue will come from services.

**Six categories organize the hardware market:** Statista’s taxonomy provides a practical starting point for understanding what households buy. A television counts within Statista’s definition when it performs smart-home control or integration functions, rather than streaming alone. This distinction helps explain why a large entertainment-device market should not automatically be interpreted as a similarly large installed base of automated homes. [7]

These measures describe an internet-connected population and specified product coverage, rather than every US household. [10][3]

## The forces determining which products will scale

### Retrofit economics favor modular development

**Existing homes remain the larger route:** Persistence Market Research assigns retrofit installations 67.3% of its 2025 solutions market, while Grand View Research reports 60.8% under its broader definition. Both point toward incremental upgrades as a central sales channel. Persistence also forecasts faster new-construction growth of approximately 14–18% annually and estimates that builder integration can reduce installation costs by 40–60%. [8][5]

**The two routes reward different designs:** Retrofit buyers benefit from wireless installation, compatibility with existing equipment, and the ability to begin with one useful product. Builders can embed cabling, gateways, electrical capacity, and coordinated controls before walls and parking infrastructure are finished. The resulting product judgment is to prioritize modular retrofit compatibility for volume, while offering more deeply integrated packages through construction and renovation channels.

**Housing type changes the opportunity:** A detached owner-occupied home can combine rooftop solar, a battery, an EV charger, and independently controlled heating. An apartment may require shared charging, landlord permission, common-area systems, and tenant-specific billing. These differences explain why a single premium whole-home package will travel less easily across countries and housing segments than a modular platform. [1]

### Consumers purchase outcomes more readily than intelligence

**Demand exists for simpler automation:** Samsung’s 2025 survey of 1,200 smart-home owners in the UK, Germany, and France found that 66% considered an AI-enabled home appealing, 44% associated it with streamlined tasks, and 45% wanted greater phone or voice control. The population was already device-owning, making the results most useful as evidence of upgrade interest. [18]

**Practical value determines monetization:** Parks Associates tested seven intelligent services and identified revenue potential across cybersecurity, maintenance monitoring, context-aware security, energy optimization, and other offerings. Conversational assistants and agentic experiences were weaker at commanding premiums than services associated with protection, prevention, savings, or support. The implication is that AI should improve a purchased outcome, rather than become the entire value proposition. [17]

**Setup friction is commercially consequential:** In the Parks–CSA findings, 52% of owners who installed a device themselves reported difficulty. Households with children also had higher product ownership—63%, compared with 37% among households without children—suggesting a substantial family segment with practical coordination needs. Products that reduce commissioning, account management, and troubleshooting burdens can therefore create value before introducing more advanced autonomy. [3]

### Regulation, electrification, and climate change reinforce useful automation

**Structural demand extends beyond convenience:** Electricity-system flexibility creates demand for controllable household loads; aging creates demand for accessible environments; water stress creates demand for monitoring and intervention. Meanwhile, security rules increasingly require manufacturers to disclose support practices and address elementary vulnerabilities. These forces favor products whose capabilities remain useful throughout a long equipment life. [19][20]

**The commercial transition has trade-offs:** Subscriptions can finance updates, monitoring, and continuing optimization, but they also raise lifetime costs and expose households to service changes. Open compatibility can broaden sales, but it increases the importance of dependable integration and support. A product becomes more attractive when its ongoing charges fund a visible service and its essential functions remain understandable and dependable.

**The scaling judgment is outcome-led:** Modular installation, measurable benefits, simple operation, and credible lifetime support offer a stronger route to broad adoption than adding connectivity or conversational features alone.

## Interoperability, AI, and trust will reshape product architecture

### Matter expands the possible system, but does not replace every integration

**Matter is moving into consequential functions:** Matter 1.4, announced in November 2024, emphasized ecosystem synchronization, network infrastructure, and energy management. Matter 1.5 extended the specification to cameras, closures, richer energy information, EV charging capabilities, and soil-related irrigation functions. The transition broadens interoperability from basic household switching toward security and resource coordination. [21][16]

**Standardized information enables better decisions:** Matter 1.5 introduces a defined format for real-time and forecast tariffs, grid-carbon information, metering history, solar data, power limits, EV state of charge, and bidirectional charging. An energy controller can use those inputs to schedule equipment against household preferences and system conditions. The product opportunity is therefore not merely connecting more devices, but making their operating choices mutually intelligible. [16]

| Layer | Relevant standards or technologies | Function in the future home |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-brand device behavior | Matter | Shared device capabilities and control |
| Local network transport | Thread, Wi-Fi, Ethernet | Moving information within the home |
| Existing device networks | Zigbee, Z-Wave | Connecting substantial legacy product ecosystems |
| Energy-system coordination | OpenADR, IEEE 2030.5, EEBus | Exchanging energy and external control information |
| Vehicle–charger communication | ISO 15118-20 | Supporting advanced EV charging interoperability |

The energy-standard roles and continuing need for multiple standards are described in the 2025 residential HEMS scan [22]; vehicle–charger requirements are discussed by the IEA [23].

**Compatibility remains a layered problem:** A standard can define how a camera streams video without standardizing every manufacturer’s subscription, analytics model, or storage service. Similarly, an energy device can expose measurements while retaining proprietary optimization or utility enrollment. Matter 1.5 expressly allows brands to retain their applications and services. [16]

**The installed base creates a transition:** Parks Associates identifies controlling devices through a preferred interface as the leading benefit that could motivate a platform switch. Legacy products mean that this transition will take years, while the HEMS scan found 53% of 51 products relying on bespoke brand- or model-specific integrations. Product developers should consequently support standards alongside migration paths for existing equipment. [17][22]

### Natural-language control is becoming an automation interface

**Google demonstrates interpretation and routine creation:** Gemini-powered Google Home features let users describe an automation in ordinary language and search camera history through contextual questions. Google’s example moves from a generic “animal detected” label toward understanding that a dog is digging in a garden. Its “Help me create” feature illustrates converting a request such as locking doors and switching off lights at bedtime into a routine. [12]

**Amazon demonstrates service orchestration:** Alexa+ combines large language models with systems, capabilities, APIs, and instructions that Amazon describes as “experts.” The purpose is to coordinate actions across devices and services, rather than require users to remember exact commands. Amazon’s oven-repair example goes further, describing a future assistant that discovers a provider, authenticates, and arranges a repair. [11]

**The development sequence matters:** Natural-language search and routine drafting require less authority than purchasing services, changing access permissions, or controlling safety-relevant equipment. The strongest 2026–2030 forecast is therefore progressive delegation: explanation and routine creation first, bounded cross-device action next, and selective unsupervised service execution where permissions and accountability are clear.

**Permissions become part of usability:** A fluent assistant still needs to identify the correct device, person, location, and action. A request involving “the downstairs door” must map to a specific lock and authorized household member. The recommended product design is to show proposed routines, distinguish reversible adjustments from consequential actions, preserve manual override, and maintain an action history.

**Commercial reach exceeds standalone premiums:** Market.us models AI-native orchestration and subscription bundles as a short-term opportunity with a +1.8% potential CAGR impact. Parks’ findings, however, favor outcome-linked monetization over conversational intelligence alone. Together, these support AI control as an important enabling layer whose strongest economics may come through security, maintenance, energy, and accessibility services. [24][17]

### Edge and cloud processing serve different jobs

**Hybrid architecture offers the strongest fit:** Processing close to a sensor—on the device or a local home controller—reduces dependence on an external internet connection and can keep sensitive information within the home. Cloud processing provides resources for complex models, training, long-term storage, and service coordination. Pilotti and colleagues’ 2026 review explains this trade-off in terms of network latency, bandwidth, privacy, computing capacity, and power. [25]

| Architecture | Principal strength | Principal trade-off | Best-fit functions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Edge or local-first | Fast response and local data handling | Limited compute, memory, and power | Detection, basic routines, immediate control |
| Cloud-first | Large models, storage, remote integration | Internet dependence and remote data handling | Rich interpretation and service orchestration |
| Hybrid | Local continuity plus cloud capability | More complex engineering and support | Whole-home energy, security, appliances |

**Products already show this direction:** In the 51-product HEMS scan, 69% used a hybrid approach and 23% were predominantly cloud-based. Hybrid products could retain local rules while accessing external forecasts, prices, or aggregation services. This makes the division of functions a practical design issue rather than an abstract technology preference. [22]

**Local processing changes competitive positioning:** A security camera that recognizes routine events locally can reduce the need to upload continuous video. A thermostat can continue basic control while advanced weather optimization pauses. A robot can navigate without waiting for remote inference. These are useful forms of resilience, although the actual offline behavior remains a product-specific design choice.

### Privacy and cybersecurity become visible product features

**Security requirements are becoming purchase signals:** ETSI EN 303 645 establishes consumer-IoT provisions covering passwords, vulnerability reporting, updates, secure communications, software integrity, personal-data protection, outage resilience, deletion, and usable installation. These provisions turn lifecycle support into part of product quality. [26]

| Jurisdiction or framework | Relevant date | Product-facing mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | April 29, 2024 | Password rules, vulnerability reporting, update-period disclosure |
| Australia | March 4, 2026 | Minimum requirements for most newly manufactured household smart devices |
| US Cyber Trust Mark | Framework adopted March 2024 | Voluntary label and QR-linked security information |
| ETSI EN 303 645 | September 2024 version | Consumer-IoT security and data-protection baseline |

These mechanisms are documented by the UK government [20], Australian Department of Home Affairs [27], FCC [28], and ETSI [26].

**Regulatory scope affects product strategy:** Australia’s manufacturing-date rule differs from a requirement applying to every previously sold device. The UK regime excludes categories including EV charge points, medical devices, and smart-meter products, while the FCC framework focuses on eligible wireless consumer IoT. A company spanning security, energy, and health therefore needs category-specific compliance rather than one universal badge. [27][20][28]

**Privacy requires more than local computing:** Occupancy patterns, video, voice, location, and appliance activity can reveal intimate household routines. Recommended features include data minimization, clear consent, separate household and guest permissions, deletion controls, limited retention, and transparent sharing. Local storage can reduce exposure, but secure firmware, authentication, and remote-access controls remain necessary. [26]

**The architectural judgment favors bounded intelligence:** Cross-brand compatibility, local continuity, transparent permissions, and secure lifetime support should become more important differentiators as AI gains authority over household actions.

## Energy products: from smart thermostats to grid-interactive homes

### Learning climate control is the strongest entry product

**Thermostats offer an established value proposition:** IMARC estimates the global smart-thermostat market at US$4.94 billion in 2025 and forecasts US$18.21 billion in 2034. Those endpoints imply approximately 15.6% annualized growth, calculated as `(18.21 ÷ 4.94)^(1/9) − 1`. Its residential segment represented 58.9% of the 2025 market, distinguishing household demand from commercial and industrial applications. [29]

**Savings come from better operating decisions:** Learning controllers combine occupancy, schedules, weather, tariffs, and a building’s thermal response. They can avoid conditioning empty rooms, preheat or precool before expensive periods, and manage heat-pump recovery without unnecessarily activating resistance backup. These controls change when and how equipment operates; they do not independently improve the thermodynamic efficiency of the underlying furnace or heat pump. [30]

**Realistic savings support a credible sale:** Energy Solutions Intelligence’s synthesis puts certified-thermostat average savings near 8%, with independent field-study summaries commonly around 10–15% of HVAC energy. Manufacturer figures are higher in selected cases: Nest reports approximately 10–12% heating and 15% cooling savings, while Ecobee advertises up to 26%. The appropriate product proposition is therefore measurable, household-specific improvement rather than a universal maximum. [30]

**A worked payback is approximately 1.6 years:** Using midpoints of the cited ranges, a US$205 thermostat divided by savings of US$1,050 annual HVAC spending × 12.5% gives US$131.25 a year and about 1.6 years’ hardware-only payback. Across the full cited US$130–280 device range and US$90–180 annual savings range, the calculated hardware-only bounds are approximately 0.7–3.1 years. Professional installation, adapters, and remote sensors increase the investment; eligible utility rebates can reduce it. [30]

**System fit matters more than branding:** Central heating, ductless air conditioning, electric baseboards, and uneven room temperatures require different controls. ### Data governance is part of care quality

**Monitoring can support or constrain autonomy:** A 2025 ethics review found privacy discussed in 18 of 23 included studies. Concerns included personal space, freedom from observation, data collection, storage, sharing, and pressure to accept tracking in exchange for independent living. The commercial product should therefore protect the resident’s agency, rather than treat caregiver convenience as the only objective. [47]

**Recommended controls should be granular:** Residents should be able to understand which events are inferred, who receives them, and what triggers escalation. **A staged health strategy is stronger:** Accessible environmental control and bounded caregiver alerts are nearer-term opportunities. More consequential health interpretation should progress with prospective validation, dependable response pathways, and appropriate accountability. Affordability, reimbursement, maintenance, and digital inclusion will influence whether these systems reach beyond affluent early adopters.

**Domestic robotics has a supported growth trajectory:** Mordor Intelligence estimates domestic-service-robot revenue at US$14.62 billion in 2025 and US$16.90 billion in 2026, forecasting US$34.93 billion in 2031—approximately 15.6% annualized growth from 2026. **Humanoid economics remain scenario-dependent:** Roland Berger’s projection of up to US$750 billion by 2035 covers industrial and consumer humanoids. [56]

**The physical task is the harder step:** A language model can describe putting dishes away without reliably grasping unfamiliar objects, navigating clutter, or moving safely around a child. Roland Berger describes hierarchical systems combining high-level planning with low-level motor control, while identifying durability, supply chains, standards, and liability as continuing constraints. Multipurpose household adoption through 2035 turns on deployed reliability, accepted pricing, safety certification, runtime, maintenance, and willingness to pay. [56]

**Consumer preferences favor bounded assistance:** Cakmak and colleagues’ 2025 survey of 76 US and UK participants found a general preference for special-purpose robots, associated with safety, privacy, comfort, and space. Respondents were more receptive to imagined low-interaction tasks such as laundry folding than safety-critical assistance such as carrying someone down stairs. Acceptability under hypothetical favorable conditions is a different commercial signal from willingness to purchase. [57]

**Subscriptions change financing, not technical readiness:** Mordor forecasts household Robotics-as-a-Service growing at 19% annually through 2031 and describes an under-twelve-month payback scenario for bundled offerings. That proposition depends on utilization, maintenance, and the labor or services actually replaced. The broader domestic-robot forecast also projects AI-assisted platforms at 20.1% growth, supporting continued investment in useful perception and navigation. [53]

**The robotics judgment favors specialization:** AI replacement appliances and mature cleaners should lead during 2026–2031, while kitchen, manipulation, and humanoid systems remain selective 2030–2035 opportunities conditional on stronger physical reliability and household economics.

## Comparing the opportunities and making product decisions

### Readiness and value explain the priority order

**The ranking favors achievable household outcomes:** Energy ranks first because it combines savings, electrification, comfort, resilience, and grid value. Security ranks second because it already commands substantial spending and has clear protection use cases. Natural-language control ranks above cleaning in strategic reach, not demonstrated physical autonomy: it can improve the experience of nearly every other category, while cleaners have stronger task-specific shipment evidence.

| Candidate | Technical and ecosystem readiness | Likely adoption pattern | Geographic fit | Economic hurdle | Defensible differentiation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Integrated energy | High basic control; mixed full orchestration | Modular upgrades, then coordination | US, Europe, electrifying APAC, Australia | Installation and tariff economics | Multi-asset, locally resilient optimization |
| AI security | Mature hardware; evolving cross-brand AI | Broad upgrades and subscriptions | North America leads; APAC expands | Lifetime fees and response quality | Useful local detection and privacy |
| AI home control | Routine tools advancing; agents less mature | Embedded platform feature | Ecosystem and language dependent | Premium willingness to pay | Permissioned cross-brand action |
| Appliances and cleaners | High cleaning maturity; mixed appliance AI | Replacement-led and task-specific | Broad; property type determines fit | Price, maintenance, space | Better task completion with less supervision |
| Assisted living | Established components; demanding care integration | Targeted accessibility and care bundles | Stronger evidence in Europe and North America | Affordability and care funding | Accessible, low-obtrusion assistance |
| Smart water | Mature metering; more involved shutoff and reuse | Utility-, property-, and scarcity-led | Water-stressed and damage-exposed settings | Plumbing and financing | Detection linked to automatic intervention |

**Readiness is not the same as importance:** Health and water rank below energy and security because their broad commercial deployment involves more demanding care, plumbing, infrastructure, or financing arrangements. In a drought-exposed region, water can rationally become the first priority. For a mobility-impaired resident, accessible controls can offer greater immediate value than energy optimization.

**The strongest products connect existing assets:** Much of the opportunity lies in improving equipment already present or due for replacement: thermostats, appliances, meters, chargers, cameras, and sensors. This reduces the need to persuade households to adopt an entirely new physical category. Compatibility and support become particularly valuable when products are expected to work across several replacement cycles.

**Commercial models should match the beneficiary:** Utilities and aggregators can value peak reduction; residents value comfort and bills; caregivers value timely assistance; property owners value avoided damage. Product design should make those interests explicit, preserve household choice, and allocate fees and responsibilities accordingly. The HEMS scan specifically warns against third-party control arrangements that extract value without sufficient transparency or consumer control. [22]

**Forecast changes have identifiable triggers:** Energy moves further toward grid services when tariffs, enrollment, and interoperability improve. AI control gains authority when cross-brand actions become dependable and auditable. Health moves toward consequential intervention when prospective performance and acceptance support it. Robotics expands beyond constrained tasks when safety, durability, and household willingness to pay justify the additional complexity.

**Evidence boundaries apply throughout:** Revenue series retain their original hardware, solutions, service, and geographic definitions; shipments, installed devices, connections, households, and research-study counts measure different things. Statista’s reported penetration sequence—82.1% in 2026 and 0.0% in 2031—is excluded from adoption forecasting. Annualizations, the thermostat payback, charging opportunity, pool residual, and kitchen-market bound are labeled calculations; priorities and rollout windows are analytical estimates. Publisher forecasts, manufacturer demonstrations, modeled savings, and dataset results are distinguished from measured household outcomes, with no common residential false-alarm or clinical-performance rate inferred from benchmark accuracy.
