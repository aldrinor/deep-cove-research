# How Birds Find Their Way During Migration

**Precision through several complementary systems:** Birds navigate by combining information about **where they are** with a compass that tells them **which direction to fly**, then correcting their movement as conditions change. Spatial variations in Earth’s magnetic field, odors, landmarks, and remembered routes can provide position-related information; magnetic inclination, the Sun, stars, and sometimes polarized skylight provide directional references. Precise migration emerges from combining these systems across different distances, not from one universal “biological GPS.” [1]

**Disturbances act through different pathways:** Artificial light can attract and disorient migrants, and particular electromagnetic exposures can disrupt magnetic orientation. Winds and storms physically displace birds or change the safest route, while climate change, noise, infrastructure, and habitat loss also affect departure decisions, food availability, stopovers, and the energy required to finish a journey.

**The questions behind successful migration:** This report explains how birds estimate position, establish direction, and correct their routes; how inheritance, experience, and social information interact; and why different disturbances produce different ecological consequences. It draws on experiments and observations across migration systems available through 13 September 2026.

## What precise migratory navigation requires

**Different information for different jobs:** A compass supplies a reference direction, but it does not automatically reveal the bird’s location or the direction of its destination. A navigational “map” supplies information about position relative to a goal, route, or geographic feature. The term need not mean that the bird represents the world as a human-style coordinate grid. [2]

| Navigation task | Main information sources | What they contribute | Behavioral result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Estimate position | Magnetic signatures, odors, landmarks | Location relative to a goal or route | Detect displacement |
| Establish direction | Magnetic, solar, and stellar compasses | A directional reference | Select a heading |
| Control the route | Position updates, landmarks, wind information | Movement relative to the intended course | Correct drift or reroute |
| Locate the final goal | Familiar visual or olfactory features | Recognition at shorter distances | Reach a particular site |

**A correct heading is insufficient:** Imagine a bird following its normal southwest migratory bearing after a storm has carried it far east. Its compass could work perfectly while it travels along the wrong corridor. To return toward its usual route, it must detect that its geographic situation has changed and choose a different bearing. Experiments that displace birds physically, or alter only the positional information they receive, are therefore especially informative about navigation. [3]

**Two strategies solve different problems:** In *vector navigation*, a bird follows a direction for a specified time or distance, sometimes through a sequence of directional changes. In *true navigation*, it can orient toward a distant target after displacement to an unfamiliar place using information available there. An inherited migration program can support the first strategy without necessarily providing the second, although recent evidence shows that some inexperienced birds also possess forms of position control. [4]

**Precision changes with spatial scale:** Crossing a continent, locating a wetland complex, and returning to a particular breeding site are not identical tasks. Broad-scale magnetic or celestial information can maintain progress over long distances, while familiar landscape or odor information can become increasingly useful near intermediate destinations and the final goal. These transitions can repeat during migration whenever a stopover becomes the next target. [3]

**Successful routes need not be straight:** A detour may avoid a desert, follow favorable winds, or connect reliable feeding sites. Conversely, a straight track does not by itself prove that a bird knows its coordinates: several compass rules can produce similar routes. Precision is best understood as reaching suitable destinations while controlling displacement and environmental risk, rather than always following the shortest possible line. [5]

**The central ecological judgment:** Birds achieve precision through repeated, context-dependent adjustments that connect position information, directional references, and decisions about where and when to travel.

## How birds estimate geographic location

### Spatial magnetic information

**Magnetic variation can indicate position:** Earth’s magnetic field changes geographically in several ways. *Intensity* is its strength; *inclination* is the angle at which field lines intersect the horizontal surface; and *declination* is the angle between magnetic north and geographic north. These properties can potentially tell a bird more than which way to face. [2]

| Magnetic property | Broad geographic pattern | Potential positional information |
|---|---|---|
| Intensity | Generally stronger toward polar regions | Progress along a regional gradient |
| Inclination | Steeper toward magnetic poles | Latitude-related position or a route signpost |
| Declination | Varies regionally between magnetic and true north | East–west displacement in some regions |
| Local anomalies | Departures caused by magnetic geology | Distinctive regional signatures |

**Intensity supplies a geographic gradient:** If a bird associates particular magnetic strengths with parts of its route, a changed intensity could indicate that it has progressed northward or southward, reached a turning region, or been displaced. This is a map function because the value is interpreted geographically. The same bird could then use a separate compass function to execute the corrective bearing. [2]

**Inclination has two distinct uses:** A bird might use the *direction and slope* of field lines to distinguish poleward from equatorward travel. That is compass information. It might also associate a *particular inclination angle* with a place or stage along its route. That is position-related information. Keeping these uses separate avoids the common mistake of treating every response to a magnetic field as evidence for a magnetic map.

**Geographic gradients are not perfect coordinates:** Intensity and inclination both often vary broadly north–south, so they do not automatically provide independent equivalents of latitude and longitude. Declination can add east–west information in some regions, but its geographic usefulness varies, and local magnetic anomalies complicate the broad patterns. A complete magnetic-coordinate hypothesis therefore requires more than showing that birds can detect field strength or inclination. [2]

**Reed warblers demonstrate position-sensitive correction:** In experiments summarized in the 2021 magnetic-map review, spring-migrating Eurasian reed warblers at their capture site oriented northeast. Birds transported **1,000 km east** instead oriented northwest, toward their breeding region. Birds remaining at the capture site but exposed to the magnetic field of the eastern location showed a comparable directional change. [2]

**Virtual displacement distinguishes the functions:** In these experiments, the birds’ physical location stayed constant while their magnetic surroundings changed. Their response was not simply to maintain their usual migratory heading relative to magnetic north; they selected a heading appropriate to a different geographic situation. The sequence—altered spatial magnetic information, apparent displacement, and corrective orientation—supports a magnetic contribution to position estimation.

**Realistic cue combinations can matter:** Responses are not identical whenever one magnetic parameter is changed. The same review describes Eurasian reed warblers responding to altered declination in one experiment, two other migratory species not showing that response, and a later reed-warbler experiment producing correction when inclination, intensity, and declination changed together. The relevant signal may therefore be a combination of field properties rather than a single universal magnetic coordinate. [2]

**Magnetic signposts require less information:** A bird need not calculate its exact position to benefit from geomagnetic geography. Central European pied flycatchers normally begin southwestward and later turn southeast, following a route around major barriers. Captive birds exposed to a sequence of route-like magnetic fields made the corresponding directional switch, suggesting an interaction between environmental magnetic information and an internal migration program. [2]

**Signposts and maps predict different abilities:** Recognizing “this is the region where I should turn” is useful but does not establish that a bird could determine the direction home from any arbitrary location. A regional magnetic signature might trigger a change in heading, fuel accumulation, or migratory activity without supplying a complete two-dimensional position. This distinction is particularly important for first-time migrants, whose inherited instructions may contain geographically meaningful triggers. [4]

**The strongest magnetic-map conclusion:** Some birds demonstrably change their orientation in response to spatial magnetic information. The broader claim that migratory birds generally navigate with a simple, globally applicable magnetic coordinate grid remains a hypothesis. A recent synthesis contrasts the displacement evidence with tracking-based analyses that make such a simple gradient strategy unlikely in the systems examined. [3]

### Odors and atmospheric gradients

**Smell can supply geographic information:** An olfactory map is not necessarily a coordinate map. Birds may learn associations between wind directions and airborne odors at a familiar site, then use the mixture encountered after displacement to infer which direction leads back. Relatively stable spatial gradients in atmospheric volatile compounds, including their ratios, provide a plausible physical basis for this information. [3]

**The mechanism is not distant nest-smelling:** The hypothesis does not require a bird hundreds of kilometers away to detect a continuous scent trail from its own nest. Instead, regional chemical patterns could indicate the direction of displacement, or an odor plume could help identify a familiar migratory corridor. Seabirds may also learn an oceanic odor landscape associated with islands, coastlines, and biologically productive waters. [3]

**Shearwater homing shows a sensory contribution:** In a satellite-tracked displacement study summarized by Mouritsen, Cory’s shearwaters released about **800 km from home** wandered after their olfactory nerves were cut. Birds with intact olfactory nerves but severed ophthalmic branches of the trigeminal nerve returned much more directly. This supports the importance of olfactory information for long-distance homing in that system. [1]

**Migrating gulls separate map from compass:** In a 2009 experiment, adult lesser black-backed gulls from Finland were moved **1,250 km southwest** to Heligoland, outside their normal migratory corridor. Gulls deprived of olfactory input retained a southerly directional preference but corrected poorly toward their population’s corridor. Intact birds and birds with the ophthalmic trigeminal branch sectioned oriented toward the expected route. [6]

**The retained heading is revealing:** The olfactory-treated gulls still migrated and maintained a consistent direction. Their difficulty concerned selecting an appropriate corrective route after displacement, not merely producing an oriented flight. This is the behavioral pattern expected when a bird retains directional guidance but loses an important source of position-related information.

**Familiarity changes the available alternatives:** Olfactory-treated gulls displaced southeast within Russia corrected comparably to other treatment groups. Tracking suggested that the release region lay within their population’s migratory corridor, where familiar information could have provided another way to navigate. Thus, the same sensory loss can have different consequences depending on whether the bird is navigating through familiar or unfamiliar geography. [6]

**The resulting olfactory judgment:** Odors can support geographic correction in both homing and actual migration, but their contribution depends on the bird’s ecology, route familiarity, and access to alternative information.

### Visual landmarks and remembered routes

**Landmarks can identify routes and places:** Coastlines, rivers, mountain ridges, and other conspicuous features can serve as leading lines, recognizable locations, or references for detecting sideways movement. A familiar feature may tell a bird where it is; an unfamiliar but continuous coastline may simply offer a useful direction to follow. These are related forms of visual guidance, but they make different demands on memory. [5]

**Shearwaters reveal a change of strategy:** In experiments during June 2012 and 2014, **32 Scopoli’s shearwaters** were displaced about **400 km** from Pianosa Island in Italy. Controls and magnetically disturbed birds oriented homeward over open sea, whereas birds deprived of smell initially deviated. After reaching a coastline, their tracks became more homeward-directed. [7]

**Coastal recovery suggests alternative guidance:** In the GPS subset, **five of six** olfactory-deprived shearwaters preferred coastal routes. The change from inefficient open-sea movement to more directed coastal movement is consistent with coastal topography providing an alternative reference when olfactory guidance was impaired. It is an especially clear example of different cues contributing at different stages of a journey. [7]

**Landmark usefulness spans a range:** A reasonable qualitative estimate is **high usefulness along a visible, familiar coast and low usefulness over featureless open ocean or under poor visibility**, based on the coastal-recovery experiments and open-ocean tracking comparisons. Darkness can weaken particular visual references without eliminating all visual information, while cloud, fog, flight altitude, and the geometry of the landscape determine what remains visible. [8]

**Route memory adds geographic structure:** Remembering a sequence of useful places is different from holding one compass bearing. A bird can learn where to turn, where to refuel, and which regions to avoid, building a route organized around intermediate destinations. Such information can make later migrations more reliable even if the bird does not possess a complete representation of every place between those destinations. [9]

**Repeated site use differs among species:** Satellite tracking deployed in 2014–2016 showed that **16 of 20 bar-tailed godwits** and **8 of 12 great knots** revisited Yellow Sea sites during southward migration that they had used northward. The godwits spent a median **92%** of southbound staging time at repeatedly visited sites, compared with **19%** for great knots. These differences show that even related shorebirds can organize migration around familiar places to different degrees. [10]

**The location-finding takeaway:** Magnetic signatures, odors, landmarks, and memory can all help birds recognize geographic circumstances, but the information may specify a corrective direction or route stage rather than exact coordinates.

## How birds establish and maintain direction

### The magnetic inclination compass

**Field-line inclination provides directional reference:** The best-established avian magnetic compass is an *inclination compass*. It responds to the alignment and inclination of magnetic field lines rather than reading magnetic polarity like a conventional compass needle. This allows birds to distinguish directions along a poleward–equatorward axis. European robins provided foundational evidence, and comparable behavior has been demonstrated in more than 20 other bird species. [11]

**A compass turns information into headings:** Once a bird’s inherited program or position estimate specifies a preferred direction, the magnetic reference helps it maintain that bearing. If experimental coils rotate the surrounding field, an appropriately changed preferred direction shows that the bird is responding to magnetic information. The experiment establishes a directional sensory capability without, by itself, establishing geographic positioning.

**Retinal chemistry offers a leading explanation:** A prominent mechanism involves *radical pairs*: pairs of chemically linked molecules or molecular states with unpaired electrons whose reactions can be influenced by a magnetic field. Light-sensitive cryptochrome proteins in the retina are leading receptor candidates. In this account, light initiates chemical reactions, the field’s orientation influences their outcomes, and the resulting sensory signal contributes to directional orientation. [11]

**Light dependence supports that mechanism:** Laboratory experiments show that magnetic orientation depends on illumination conditions. The reviewed birds oriented under several ultraviolet, blue, turquoise, and green wavelengths but became disoriented under the tested yellow and red monochromatic conditions. These results connect magnetic-compass behavior to light-sensitive processes; they do not mean that every outdoor light source has the same effect. [11]

**Experience changes the operating window:** Magnetic orientation also depends on prior exposure to field strength. In the reviewed experiments, fields more than about **20–25% weaker or stronger** than the familiar local field could initially cause disorientation. European robins normally kept at **47 μT** could subsequently orient at experienced fields of **4 μT** and **150 μT**, demonstrating substantial recalibration rather than an absolutely fixed intensity range. [11]

**Compass and map receptors may differ:** Magnetite—magnetic iron oxide—and pathways involving the trigeminal nerve have also been proposed for detecting magnetic information, particularly intensity-related map input. These ideas need not directly replace a retinal compass: different mechanisms could serve different functions. The interpretation of previously proposed iron-rich receptor structures was challenged when some were identified as macrophages, immune cells rather than magnetosensitive neurons. [12]

**Geography changes compass usefulness:** At the magnetic equator, field lines become horizontal, making the poleward–equatorward distinction ambiguous for an inclination compass. At very high latitudes, steep field lines and unusual lighting conditions create different challenges. East African experiments found migrating marsh warblers strongly dependent on celestial information at sunset after crossing the geomagnetic equator, illustrating why another directional reference can become important. [5]

### The Sun and the internal clock

**Solar orientation requires time compensation:** The Sun changes position during the day, so “keep the Sun on one side” would not maintain a fixed geographic bearing. A time-compensated Sun compass combines the Sun’s position with the bird’s internal daily clock. The clock allows the bird to interpret what the Sun’s observed direction means at that time. [13]

**Clock shifts produce predictable errors:** Homing pigeons whose internal clocks were advanced or delayed departed on bearings shifted from controls in the direction and magnitude expected for a time-compensated Sun compass. The birds interpreted the real Sun using the wrong internal time. This is strong evidence for the mechanism because manipulating one component of the proposed calculation produced the predicted directional change. [13]

**Mirror experiments identify the solar reference:** Foundational experiments with captive European starlings shifted the Sun’s apparent position using mirrors. The birds changed their orientation toward the direction indicated by the altered solar cue, even though that direction was wrong geographically. Together, mirror and clock-shift experiments distinguish use of the Sun from a simple response to the surrounding landscape. [14]

**Daily clocks and seasonal programs differ:** The daily clock compensating for solar movement is conceptually separate from the annual timing program that helps initiate migration or schedule changes in activity. Both involve biological timing, but they answer different questions: “What does the Sun’s position mean now?” versus “When should migration begin or change phase?” [9]

### Stars and polarized skylight

**Stellar rotation supplies geographic reference:** Nocturnal migrants can learn the apparent rotation of the night sky and use its rotation center to establish geographic directions. Foundational work associated with Stephen Emlen showed that the mechanism is not simply an inherited chart of individual stars. Once the reference is learned, a stellar compass can support orientation without the daily time compensation required by the Sun compass. [14]

**Artificial stars demonstrate compass calibration:** Able and Able’s experiments with young Savannah sparrows showed that exposure to a rotating artificial star pattern changed the birds’ preferred magnetic orientation. Experience with clear daytime or nighttime skies could calibrate the magnetic compass during early development. Celestial observations therefore can do more than guide immediate flight: they can establish how another compass should be interpreted. [15]

**Polarization adds information around twilight:** Sunlight scattered through the atmosphere develops a pattern of *polarization*, meaning that light vibrations become preferentially aligned in particular planes. In some experiments, polarized skylight near sunrise or sunset provides a reference for calibrating the relationship between celestial and magnetic directions. Birds subsequently tested with magnetic information alone can retain the experimentally altered calibration. [13]

**Calibration is not a universal compass:** The strongest polarized-light interpretation concerns calibration in particular experimental settings, not a demonstrated all-day directional system used identically by every migrant. Other bird studies have not consistently reproduced regular twilight calibration. Species, region, migration phase, the available view of the sky, and the particular cue-conflict manipulation can influence the outcome. [16]

| Directional reference | Information supplied | Important requirement | Demonstrated role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnetic field | Poleward–equatorward directional axis | Usable inclination and sensory conditions | Maintain a magnetic bearing |
| Sun | Geographic direction from solar position | Internal time compensation | Maintain a daytime bearing |
| Stars | Geographic reference from sky rotation | Learning and visible stellar information | Nocturnal orientation |
| Polarized skylight | Relationship between celestial and magnetic directions | Appropriate twilight exposure | Calibration in some experiments |

**The directional-navigation takeaway:** Birds possess several directional references that can complement and calibrate one another, with their relative usefulness changing across weather conditions, latitudes, and stages of migration.

## How birds correct for wind

### Heading and ground track are different

**Air movement changes geographic movement:** A bird’s *heading* is the direction it points and flies relative to the surrounding air. Its *ground track* is the direction it actually moves over Earth’s surface. Wind adds its own movement to the bird’s air-relative flight, so a stable compass heading can still produce a shifting geographic route. [17]

\(\mathbf{v}_{\text{ground}}=\mathbf{v}_{\text{air}}+\mathbf{v}_{\text{wind}}\)

**Steering into crosswinds reduces drift:** By pointing partly into a crosswind, a bird can cancel some or all of the sideways displacement. *Full compensation* preserves the preferred ground direction; *partial compensation* reduces drift but leaves some residual displacement; and *full drift* allows wind to alter the ground track without corresponding lateral correction. These are movement outcomes, not necessarily different sensory systems. [8]

**Physical capacity sets an upper bound:** At a given airspeed, a bird has only so much sideways flight available to oppose a crosswind. Stronger winds can therefore cause displacement even when the bird detects the problem and attempts to correct it. Changing altitude, waiting to depart, landing, or selecting a different route can sometimes be more effective than trying to preserve the original track. [5]

### Tracking reveals several correction strategies

**Godwits compensate over landmark-poor ocean:** Satellite tracking followed **25 Hudsonian godwits during 2019–2021** on northward flights extending more than **7,000 km**, from Chile toward the northern Gulf of Mexico. Full compensation was the most frequent classified behavior, accounting for **41.1% of observed flight segments**, and was common even far from visible topographic landmarks. [18]

**Frequent correction is not perfect correction:** The same godwits also showed partial compensation, drifting, supported flight, and overcompensation. Their strong use of full compensation therefore should not be read as a claim that every segment followed a fixed line or that wind had no effect. The finding is that active correction persisted throughout a demanding oceanic flight, contrary to the expectation that correction must be severely limited without terrestrial landmarks.

**Coastal migrants correct more strongly:** In autumn 2013–2014, weather radars at three coastal and three inland eastern US sites collected about **1.6 million samples over 55 nights**. Inland migrants compensated for an average **29.0%** of wind effects. Near the Atlantic coast, compensation strengthened during the night, reaching **74.5% in its final tenth**. [19]

**Risk helps explain that contrast:** Inland birds may tolerate some drift while continuing through broadly usable habitat. Near an ocean barrier, the consequences of eastward displacement can become more serious, especially late in the night as fuel reserves decline. Greater correction near the coast is therefore consistent with birds assessing their geographic circumstances and changing how much drift they accept, rather than simply reacting identically to every crosswind. [19]

| Birds and setting | Reported movement response | What the comparison shows |
|---|---|---|
| Hudsonian godwits, transoceanic flight | Full compensation most frequent | Landmarks are not always essential |
| Nocturnal migrants, inland eastern US | 29.0% average compensation | Considerable drift can be tolerated |
| Nocturnal migrants, Atlantic coast | 74.5% compensation late at night | Barrier proximity changes correction |
| Juvenile ospreys and honey buzzards | Approximately full drift | First migrations can be wind-sensitive |
| Adult ospreys and honey buzzards | Reduced drift, reported as 29% | Age is associated with stronger correction |
| Cory’s shearwaters, Gibraltar | Drift in spring; compensation in autumn | Season and flight style matter |

**Age changes wind sensitivity:** Satellite telemetry of ospreys and honey buzzards migrating between northern Europe and Africa found approximately full wind drift in juveniles but much reduced drift in adults. The adult result was reported as **29% wind drift**, a different measure from the compensation percentages in the eastern US radar comparison. The age contrast is consistent with improved detection and control of displacement as navigation develops. [20]

**Flight style changes the trade-off:** At Gibraltar, radar and visual observations analyzed **318 seabird trajectories** during 2006. Northern gannets, Balearic shearwaters, and auks showed compensation under the sampled conditions, while Cory’s shearwaters allowed substantial drift with prevailing spring tailwinds but compensated in autumn. For a gliding bird, accepting some displacement can exploit atmospheric energy and increase progress, so drift need not signify a malfunctioning compass. [17]

**Large radar datasets show active control:** A central Israel study analyzed **857,206 soaring-migrant tracks over 148 days in seven data-collection years between 2005 and 2016**, with honey buzzards as the focal migration species. The migrants maintained a consistent daily mean track near **187.7°** and adjusted airspeed and compensation as tailwinds and crosswinds changed. This demonstrates coordinated control of speed and direction, rather than passive transport by wind. [21]

**Wind correction needs more than a compass:** A compass tells a bird its heading, but not necessarily how quickly it is being carried sideways relative to the ground. Visible landmarks can reveal this through their apparent movement; repeated geographic information could also reveal accumulating displacement. The godwit results show that visual landscape references are not the complete explanation, while leaving magnetic, celestial, atmospheric, and other offshore information as possible contributors. [18]

**The wind-navigation takeaway:** Migrants regulate drift according to ability, experience, flight style, and geographic risk; sometimes the adaptive response is to compensate, and sometimes it is to exploit the wind.

## How inheritance, learning, and social information interact

### First migration and later experience

**Inherited programs provide an initial route:** Many songbirds undertake their first migration without following experienced adults. Their inherited program includes timing, preferred directions, and migration duration or distance-related components. Hand-raised birds expressing seasonally appropriate migratory restlessness and orientation support this explanation, but inheritance does not imply that the entire journey proceeds without environmental feedback. [9]

**Displacement exposes the value of experience:** Between 1948 and 1957, Perdeck’s experiments transported **11,247 common starlings** from the Netherlands to Switzerland. Adult recoveries indicated correction northwest toward the population’s customary wintering region, whereas young birds continued southwest and wintered farther south. The contrast separates following a population-typical direction from correcting toward a geographically familiar destination. [4]

**White-crowned sparrows show a similar contrast:** In another displacement experiment, white-crowned sparrows were transported approximately **3,700 km** from northwestern to northeastern North America. Experienced birds corrected for the longitudinal displacement, while young birds did not show comparable correction. Such experiments establish an age-related difference in navigational performance without requiring that every adult use the same kind of map. [4]

**Young birds are not universally mapless:** A strict “juveniles use only a compass; adults use a map” rule is too simple. A 2025 review describes juvenile common cuckoos correcting after an approximately **1,800-km eastward displacement**, alongside other evidence of position-sensitive behavior in first-time migrants. Its interpretation is that some inexperienced birds may possess inherited signposts or partial position control rather than either a complete map or no geographic information at all. [4]

**Calibration and route learning are different:** Learning how magnetic and celestial directions correspond improves the interpretation of a compass. Learning the location of a wetland, coastline, or breeding area adds geographic information about goals and routes. The Savannah-sparrow calibration experiments directly demonstrate the first process, while repeated journeys and site reuse can provide the experience underlying the second. [15]

**Internal condition changes navigational decisions:** A bird carrying ample fuel may attempt a water crossing that a lean bird avoids. Food availability can suppress migratory activity during refueling, while seasonal timing can increase the pressure to resume travel. Thus, identical directional information can lead to different routes or departure decisions because navigation is embedded in the bird’s physiological condition and migration schedule. [4]

### Social information and collective navigation

**Experienced companions can transmit routes:** In socially migrating species, inexperienced birds may follow knowledgeable individuals and learn the cues associated with the journey. Over repeated migrations, they can become informed navigators themselves, allowing route information to persist beyond the lifespan of any one leader. This complements inherited guidance rather than replacing it in every species. [22]

**Groups can also reduce directional noise:** Collective-navigation theory proposes that averaging individual directional estimates can improve accuracy when errors are unbiased and not perfectly correlated. This “many wrongs” effect differs from following a knowledgeable leader: one pools imperfect estimates, while the other transfers unequal knowledge. A shared bias, such as attraction toward a misleading light, would not necessarily disappear through averaging. [22]

**Flocking alone does not identify information transfer:** In the Gibraltar radar study, **74% of recorded tracks were flocks**, establishing that social flight was common. But the study measured wind compensation rather than whether inexperienced birds learned from particular companions. A flock can share a heading because of social influence, a common environmental cue, similar inherited preferences, or some combination of these. [17]

**Shared routes have several possible causes:** GPS tracking of five goose species in East Asia recorded a common initial northeastward passage toward Bohai Bay wetlands before routes diverged toward different breeding regions. Such structure shows how migration connects geographic waypoints, but it does not alone distinguish inherited route preferences, social transmission, individual memory, or the distribution of suitable habitat. [23]

### How birds combine available information

**Cue availability changes during a journey:** Overcast skies can obscure the Sun and stars; darkness and fog can reduce particular landmark references; ocean crossings remove most terrestrial features. Under these circumstances, magnetic information may become relatively more important. When familiar geography reappears, visual or olfactory information can again contribute strongly. [3]

**Cue integration involves more than substitution:** One system can help interpret another, as celestial exposure calibrates magnetic orientation. A different system can also help recover after a loss of guidance, as the coastal movements of olfactory-deprived shearwaters suggest. These interactions explain why removing one cue sometimes produces severe disruption and sometimes produces a change of strategy rather than complete failure. [7]

**The brain connects information to goals:** A neuroecological review proposes that the hippocampus interacts with structures representing map and compass information to compute and update navigational goals. Other forebrain systems may help weigh conflicting information and respond to unexpected situations. The useful insight is that navigation requires information processing and decision-making, not merely the possession of several independent sensory detectors. [24]

**The developmental-navigation takeaway:** Inheritance supplies important starting instructions, while calibration, individual experience, and—in some species—social learning alter how birds interpret cues and correct their migration.

## What experiments, tracking, and field observations show

**Different methods answer different questions:** Controlled orientation experiments establish how behavior responds when a particular cue changes. Tracking records the geography and timing of real movement. Radar and field observations reveal how large numbers of migrants respond to winds, barriers, light, and other conditions. Their strongest conclusions emerge when these approaches support the same explanation. [3]

| Evidence type | Direct behavioral readout | Representative application |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation cages | Preferred attempted departure direction | Magnetic-field, wavelength, and radiofrequency tests |
| Planetariums and artificial skies | Orientation under altered celestial information | Stellar learning and compass calibration |
| Clock shifts and solar mirrors | Bearing after altered time or apparent Sun position | Time-compensated solar orientation |
| Displacement plus sensory manipulation | Corrective movement after particular inputs are changed | Reed warblers, shearwaters, and gulls |
| GPS, satellite tags, and geolocators | Locations and routes through time | Ocean crossings and repeated site use |
| Radar and field observation | Tracks, headings, density, and flight behavior | Wind compensation and light attraction |

**Orientation cages make cues experimentally accessible:** In an Emlen funnel, migratory restlessness produces marks indicating the bird’s preferred direction. Magnetic coils can change field direction or simulate another location; illumination can be varied; and radiofrequency noise can be introduced. Planetarium and artificial-star experiments provide corresponding ways to manipulate celestial references. [11]

**Magnetic pulses are diagnostic treatments:** Strong magnetic-pulse experiments provide another way to probe magnetic contributions. Reviewed work on Australian silvereyes found changes in experienced birds’ orientation after pulse treatment but not in newly fledged birds, linking the response to developmental or experiential differences. Such pulses are experimental interventions, distinct from ordinary exposure to urban electromagnetic noise. [12]

**High-resolution tracking reveals sustained travel:** A lesser-yellowlegs study tagged **118 adults during 2018–2022**, and **115 tags** supplied full or partial route information. The GPS devices recorded locations with approximately **10-m accuracy**. Ten tracked birds made Atlantic crossings averaging about **4,500 km** and roughly **four days**, demonstrating sustained directional travel across a large ecological barrier. [25]

**Geolocators extend the geographic picture:** Light-level loggers have also revealed long-distance routes in small birds, including northern wheatears migrating between Alaska and eastern Africa. Comparing these routes with predicted compass paths helps assess which directional rules could plausibly generate them. Where several predicted mechanisms fit the same route—as in some bar-tailed-godwit comparisons—the route itself does not uniquely choose between them. [5]

**Sensory experiments give tracks causal leverage:** The shearwater and gull studies are especially useful because they combine movement tracking with controlled changes to candidate sensory inputs. They connect a particular treatment to subsequent correction, route efficiency, or reliance on coastlines. That connection is more informative about cue contribution than either an unmanipulated route or an initial departure bearing alone. [6]

**The evidence takeaway:** Laboratory tests identify cue-sensitive behavior, while tracking and field observations show how that behavior contributes to real journeys across changing landscapes and weather.

## Disturbances that alter migration

**Orientation failure is only one outcome:** A disturbance may alter the sensory information a bird receives, physically displace it, change the costs of a route, or remove the destination it was trying to reach. These effects can interact, but they should not all be described as damage to the compass. A bird may know where to go and still be unable to reach it safely or refuel there.

| Disturbance | Main pathway | Typical consequence | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Artificial light at night | Attraction and altered visual conditions | Circling, slowed flight, disorientation | New York radar and acoustic monitoring |
| Electromagnetic interference | Perturbation of magnetic orientation | Altered compass performance under tested exposures | Robin and blackcap experiments |
| Storms and strong winds | Physical displacement and changing flight constraints | Drift, delay, rerouting, grounding risk | Radar, telemetry, and GPS |
| Climate change | Shifting resource timing, winds, and habitat | Seasonal mismatch and altered route costs | Phenology and route studies |
| Urban noise and infrastructure | Auditory disturbance, barriers, and fragmented habitat | Altered behavior and local movement | Reviews and goose tracking |
| Habitat and stopover loss | Removal of food and resting sites | Longer flights and reduced route options | Flyway monitoring and habitat studies |

### Artificial light at night

**Bright installations can redirect migrating birds:** At New York’s Tribute in Light, radar, acoustic monitoring, and visual observations estimated that approximately **1.1 million birds** were affected across seven analyzed September nights during 2008–2016; the estimate’s 95% confidence interval was **0.6–1.6 million**. Illuminated periods produced concentrations reaching about **20 times surrounding baseline density**, slower flight, circling, and frequent vocalizations. Responses extended to approximately **4 km altitude**. [26]

**Switching lights off changed the behavior:** The observed disturbances dissipated when the beams were extinguished. That on–off response supports a direct contribution from illumination to the aggregation and altered flight. The ecological consequences can include delayed progress and unnecessary flight, as well as increased exposure to nearby hazards; the estimated number affected should not be interpreted as a mortality count. [26]

**Attraction was important in the model:** The simulation best matching the radar observations used a **0.95 disorientation-probability parameter** and a **1,500-m characteristic disorientation distance**. Directed movement toward the light was needed to reproduce the observed concentration; nondirectional wandering alone dispersed the modeled birds. These are fitted model parameters describing this installation, not a universal probability or a hard attraction boundary for all lights. [26]

**Several sensory pathways could contribute:** Artificial light changes the visual environment, may obscure useful celestial contrast, and can interact with light-dependent orientation processes. However, the field aggregation does not by itself identify whether attraction, celestial interference, or altered magnetoreception explains each bird’s response. The observed behavioral endpoint is clear even though its complete sensory explanation involves alternatives. [26]

**Exposure differs across migration systems:** A global study of **298 nocturnally migrating species** found greater artificial-light intensity in passage ranges than breeding ranges. Yet longer migration distances were associated with lower mean light exposure, contrary to the study’s expectation, because many short-distance migrants remained within brightly illuminated temperate regions throughout the year. Risk therefore depends on where birds travel, not simply how far. [27]

**The practical implication follows directly:** Reducing unnecessary illumination during migration addresses a demonstrated source of altered flight behavior. Maintaining dark conditions around valuable stopover habitat also avoids coupling an attractive visual signal with an otherwise hazardous or unsuitable location.

### Electromagnetic interference and geomagnetic disturbances

**Robin experiments demonstrate exposure-dependent disruption:** Engels and colleagues’ fully double-blinded experiments found that European robins lost magnetic orientation in unscreened huts at the University of Oldenburg. Orientation returned in grounded aluminum-screened huts that reduced electromagnetic noise by roughly **two orders of magnitude across 50 kHz–5 MHz**. Removing the grounding or generating broadband noise inside the screened huts again disrupted orientation. [28]

**The experimental reversals matter causally:** Changing the noise environment while restoring and removing the behavioral response strengthens the link between exposure and magnetic-compass performance. Birds tested far from the relevant noise sources oriented without screening, showing that the effect depended on the exposure context. The result concerns a particular sensory function under tested conditions, not a demonstration that every electromagnetic source disrupts every bird.

**Blackcaps provide an important comparison:** A double-blind study retained **15 Eurasian blackcaps** tested during spring 2017 and 2018. They remained magnetically oriented under **0.1–100 kHz broadband noise**, including when the horizontal field was rotated. This contrasted with earlier robin disruption under other tested frequency ranges, including **20–450 kHz**. [29]

**Species and exposure must be separated:** The robin and blackcap studies do not establish a clean species-only explanation because their frequencies, equipment, exposure measurements, and experimental contexts differed. The defensible conclusion is conditional: some electromagnetic exposures can disrupt magnetic orientation, but response cannot be generalized without considering both the bird and the signal. Matched cross-species exposures would determine how much of the contrast reflects sensory biology rather than experimental conditions.

**Natural magnetic disturbances are another pathway:** Geomagnetic storms temporarily alter the field that animals encounter. An analysis of **2,226,936 North American banding records from 152 migratory landbird species during 1960–2019** associated greater geomagnetic disturbance with higher autumn vagrancy. For a typical modeled species, elevated disturbance increased the predicted fraction of rare records from about **2% to 5%**, while spring evidence was much weaker. [30]

**Vagrancy has multiple contributing causes:** The same analysis attributed most variation in vagrancy to other factors, depending on species. Its autumn association is consistent with disturbed magnetic information contributing to geographic errors, but weather, migration strategy, age, and alternative cue use also influence where unusual records appear. Natural geomagnetic disturbance, human-generated radiofrequency noise, and atmospheric storms are therefore distinct phenomena despite their potential to affect the same migration.

### Storms and strong winds

**Strong winds can overwhelm correction:** A radar analysis of mainly nocturnal passerines near Nuremberg during autumn 1987 found partial compensation under particular opposing and sidewind conditions up to approximately **8–10 m/s**. At higher wind speeds, compensatory behavior diminished and birds became fully drifted. This is an observed limit in that migration system, not a universal wind threshold for all flight styles. [31]

**Weather also changes decisions before displacement:** Regional radio-telemetry of **275 songbirds during autumn 2019–2021** found departure more likely under favorable winds, less cloud, and no rain. Overcast conditions or increasing headwinds increased the likelihood of landing, while wind support helped predict offshore versus coastal routing. Birds therefore manage weather through takeoff, route selection, and flight interruption as well as through in-air steering. [32]

**A surprising storm response can be adaptive:** GPS tracking of adult streaked shearwaters breeding in Japan during 2008–2018 showed that some flew toward cyclone eyes when strong winds threatened to carry them toward mainland Japan. Four individuals came within **30 km of a storm’s center**, and some tracked its movement for hours. Their low-cost dynamic-soaring flight allowed a response very different from simply avoiding the strongest winds. [33]

**Geographic context explains the apparent paradox:** For a shearwater between a cyclone and land, flying away from the storm could increase exposure to winds carrying it ashore. Moving toward the storm could instead keep it over water and avoid dangerous wind directions in the storm’s wake. The relevant decision depends on where land lies, the bird’s position within the storm circulation, and its capacity to fly in those winds—not merely on whether it can sense the cyclone.

**The case extends beyond migration alone:** These GPS observations concerned breeding adults exposed to storms, so they clarify a general navigation-and-flight mechanism rather than directly measuring a migratory journey. The study’s interpretation of juvenile vulnerability draws on separate evidence that inexperienced shearwaters often follow inherited bearings and may cross unsuitable land. It illustrates why flight capability and geographic knowledge can jointly determine storm risk. [33]

**Storm displacement need not imply sensory failure:** A bird can retain an accurate compass and still be blown away from its route, delayed until fuel reserves become critical, or forced toward unsuitable landing habitat. Recovery then depends on detecting its new situation, selecting an achievable destination, and having enough energy to reach it. This is where map-like information becomes particularly valuable.

### Climate change

**Changing seasons can outpace familiar schedules:** In a study covering **150 Western-Hemisphere migratory species during 2002–2021**, **109 species** had spring passage at least coarsely synchronized with vegetation green-up. Of those 109, **103—94.50%—were more strongly synchronized with long-term average green-up than with current conditions**. The denominator matters: 94.50% refers to that synchronized subset, not all migratory birds. [34]

**The mismatch concerns ecological timing:** A bird following a schedule shaped by inherited programs, previous experience, or socially transmitted routes can reach the correct place after its expected food peak has moved earlier. This is not the same as choosing the wrong compass direction. It is a weakening relationship between historically useful information and the resources that made the route advantageous.

**Daylength and temperature can diverge:** Photoperiod—the duration of daylight—helps synchronize annual activity in many birds, particularly at middle and high latitudes. Climate warming changes food and vegetation schedules without changing the astronomical daylength calendar. Birds whose departure or breeding schedules are tightly linked to photoperiod can consequently face increasing mismatch, although flexibility varies among species and populations. [35]

**Changing winds can reshape routes:** GPS tracking of **44 mature golden eagles across 73 autumn and 62 spring migrations during 2014–2017** showed that winds influenced which side of Alaska’s Wrangell Mountains the birds used. The relationship differed between seasons. This supplies an observed link from atmospheric conditions to route choice; climate-driven changes in those conditions can therefore alter the costs and geographic attractiveness of established alternatives. [36]

**Future outcomes depend on interacting changes:** Whether a migration becomes easier or harder depends on regional wind direction and timing, the rate at which birds adjust schedules, and whether suitable feeding and resting habitats remain connected. Stronger wind support on one flight segment can be beneficial while downstream crosswinds increase displacement risk. The ecological forecast is therefore route-specific, rather than a simple global prediction of uniformly better or worse navigation. [18]

**Climate evidence supports an ecological pathway:** The clearest findings concern timing, habitat suitability, atmospheric assistance, and route costs. A claim that warming itself directly impairs a magnetic receptor would be a different mechanistic claim from the evidence described here. [37]

### Urban noise and infrastructure

**Noise influences behavior through auditory pathways:** A systematic review of **50 peer-reviewed papers** documented effects of anthropogenic sound on avian behavior, communication, auditory perception, and physiological outcomes. Such changes can affect vigilance, distraction, and the conditions under which birds rest or feed. They provide plausible routes to altered migration performance without requiring direct disruption of magnetic sensing. [38]

**Infrastructure changes the movement landscape:** Highways and buildings can separate suitable habitat patches and act as barriers, while illumination adds a distinct attraction or disorientation pathway. A bird navigating an urban landscape may need to make detours, avoid obstacles, or search farther for usable habitat. These responses can occur even when its geographic orientation remains functional. [39]

**Tracking shows altered local movement:** GPS/GSM tracking of **21 greater white-fronted geese during 2015–2019** found more flying time with higher road density and greater turning angles with more urban land at Chinese stopovers. Farmland-dominated landscapes were associated with more frequent flight, longer movements, and behavioral switching. These observations connect land use to stopover behavior and likely energetic demands, rather than measuring a failed migratory compass. [40]

**The ecological distinction guides interpretation:** A more winding track can reflect disturbance flights, obstacle avoidance, or dispersed feeding opportunities. It should not automatically be labeled confusion. Noise, light, infrastructure, and habitat fragmentation often occur together, so their contributions must be interpreted through the particular behavioral response being measured.

### Habitat loss and stopover disruption

**Correct navigation cannot replace refueling habitat:** Stopovers supply food, recovery, and shelter between flight stages. When a familiar site is lost or degraded, a bird may arrive in the right place yet fail to accumulate enough energy for its next leg. It may then remain longer, search more widely, attempt a longer flight, or use poorer habitat. [41]

**Yellow River Delta changes show the scale:** A study of this East Asian–Australasian Flyway stopover reported a **34.99% decline in modeled habitat area**, a **58.87% decline in the largest-patch index**, and a **90.14% decline in surveyed shorebird abundance from 1995 to 2020**. Mean patch area was associated with abundance, with stronger responses in larger-bodied shorebirds. These are habitat and community changes, not measurements of orientation error. [42]

**Degradation extends across site networks:** BirdLife International reports that **58% of 1,099 monitored Key Biodiversity Areas for migratory birds were in poor or very poor condition during 2016–2025**. This measures the condition of the assessed sites, rather than the fraction of all migration habitat worldwide. Its significance is that migration depends on multiple connected destinations whose deterioration can accumulate along a flyway. [41]

**Tracking identifies geographic exposure:** The East Asian goose study delineated **112 stopovers totaling 2,192,823 hectares** from completed spring routes during 2015–2017. Only **15.63%** of that area overlapped protected areas, leaving **84.37%** outside them. Protection status is not itself a measurement of habitat quality, but the comparison shows how much of a tracked refueling network depended on places beyond formal protected-area boundaries. [23]

**Familiarity can become a disadvantage:** Returning to a known site normally reduces uncertainty about food, resting places, and hazards. If conditions deteriorate, that same fidelity may delay adoption of alternatives. Birds with few major staging sites may face a particularly demanding adjustment because losing one site changes the feasible geometry and energy requirements of much of the journey. [10]

**Food timing makes the connection concrete:** Red knots using Delaware Bay time spring staging around horseshoe-crab egg availability. They can feed for upward of **two weeks** and nearly double their mass before continuing migration. This shows why a functioning compass, an accurately remembered destination, and adequate fuel are complementary requirements: reaching the site is only one part of successful passage. [43]

**Disturbances can compound one another:** A crosswind can push a migrant away from its normal corridor; bright lighting can then divert its flight or landing distribution; and degraded habitat can reduce opportunities to recover. These are interacting pathways through position, behavior, and energy, not necessarily repeated failures of the same sensory organ. Protecting navigational conditions and protecting the habitat network address different parts of that risk.

**The disturbance takeaway:** The strongest explanation distinguishes direct interference with orientation from physical displacement and from changes that make formerly reliable routes, schedules, or destinations less usable.

## The ecological conclusion

**Robust navigation remains conditional:** Birds combine directional compasses with several kinds of geographic information, and their precision depends on when those sources are useful and how they are interpreted. The contrast between a reed warbler correcting after virtual magnetic displacement, a gull losing displacement correction after olfactory treatment, and a godwit compensating over open ocean shows why no single cue explains migration across all birds. [1]

**Evidence supports capabilities more clearly than universality:** Across the studies discussed here, cage bearings measure attempted orientation, tracking measures realized movement, and radar often measures aggregate behavior; approximately 10-m GPS accuracy describes the recorder, not the bird’s own positional accuracy. Light-impact totals are estimates, and disorientation probabilities are fitted model parameters; the high-to-low landmark comparison is qualitative. Receptor identity, a universal magnetic coordinate map, and some proposed neural assignments remain unsettled, while species, age, season, familiarity, and overlapping disturbances affect interpretation. [3]

**Conservation must preserve both information and opportunity:** Reducing demonstrated sources of disorientation, especially unnecessary nighttime lighting, helps birds maintain purposeful movement. Maintaining connected, productive stopover networks gives them somewhere safe to reach and enough energy to continue. Precise migration succeeds when sensory guidance, learned and inherited information, flight capability, and usable habitat continue to work together.
