# Parkinson’s Warning Signs by Stage and Daily Support After DBS

**Warning signs extend beyond tremor.** Early Parkinson’s disease may involve slowness, stiffness, a resting tremor, smaller handwriting, a softer voice, smell loss, constipation, or acting out dreams. Middle-stage concerns include medication wearing-off, involuntary movements, freezing, falls, and increasing difficulty with everyday tasks. Advanced disease may bring substantial dependence, swallowing problems, weight loss, cognitive changes, hallucinations, and recurrent infections. These patterns overlap and vary considerably between people. [1]

**Sudden dangerous changes need action.** Breathing difficulty, active choking, loss of consciousness, stroke-like symptoms, serious injury, suicidal thoughts, or fever with profound stiffness and immobility need emergency assessment. After deep brain stimulation, or DBS, comfort depends on continued medication management, reliable stimulation, rehabilitation, and attention to symptoms that surgery may not improve. [2][3]

**Start with function and urgency.** The stage map below summarizes common patterns. The next section separates emergencies from problems needing prompt advice; subsequent sections explain stage-related changes and practical support after DBS. This guide is written primarily for family members and informal caregivers, to use alongside the patient and their clinical teams.

## Understanding stages without treating them as a timetable

**Stages describe patterns of disability.** For family discussions, early, middle, and advanced disease are useful broad descriptions—not a way to diagnose someone or assign their formal stage at home.

| Stage | Motor warning signs | Nonmotor warning signs | Functional impact | Family response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early or initial | Slowness, resting tremor, stiffness, reduced arm swing | Smell loss, constipation, dream enactment, mood changes, fatigue | Usually independent; tasks require more effort | Arrange assessment; record persistent changes |
| Middle or moderate | Wearing-off, dyskinesia, freezing, impaired balance | Sleep, blood-pressure, bladder, cognitive or behavioral changes | Increasing difficulty with transfers and self-care | Request treatment review and rehabilitation |
| Advanced or late | Severe immobility, recurrent falls, difficult transfers | Dysphagia, dementia, psychosis, fainting, infections | Extensive assistance; possible wheelchair or bed dependence | Coordinate multidisciplinary and palliative support |

The patterns summarized here follow clinical reviews of symptom progression [1] and late-stage Parkinson’s [4].

**Formal staging emphasizes movement.** The Hoehn and Yahr scale commonly groups stages 1–2 as mild, stage 3 as moderate, and stages 4–5 as severe. It mainly describes motor disability and balance. Medication fluctuations, swallowing safety, mood, thinking, and caregiver workload require additional assessment; a motor stage alone does not capture the whole illness. [1]

**Early movement problems can coexist with substantial distress.** In the 2021 COPPADIS analysis of 603 people with Parkinson’s, more than 30% of those classified as Hoehn and Yahr stage I had severe or very severe nonmotor symptom burden. Someone who walks independently may nevertheless need significant help with depression, fatigue, sleep, constipation, or blood-pressure symptoms. Waiting for obvious physical disability can therefore delay useful support. [5]

**Progression is not a fixed sequence.** People differ in which symptoms appear, how quickly they change, and how well treatment controls them. Motor complications may occur while a person remains relatively independent, and some people never reach profound advanced-stage dependence. The practical question is what has changed in the person’s safety, comfort, and abilities—not simply how many years have passed since diagnosis. [4][6]

**Diagnosis requires clinical interpretation.** A neurologist assesses the combination of symptoms, examination findings, treatment response, and possible alternative explanations. Marked balance problems very early in the illness, unusually rapid deterioration, or other atypical features warrant reassessment. Medicines, infections, dehydration, injuries, and other medical conditions can also change function without representing progression of Parkinson’s itself. [7][8]

**The useful family measure is change.** Track what the person can do safely and comfortably, and seek help when that changes rather than waiting for a different stage label.

## When family members should intervene

**Choose urgency by danger and onset.** Immediately threatening symptoms need emergency services; new or rapidly worsening problems need prompt clinical assessment; mild, stable patterns can be documented for planned review. A familiar Parkinson’s diagnosis should never be used to explain away a new medical emergency.

| Action level | Typical pattern | What the family should do |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency now | Airway danger, collapse, stroke-like change, serious injury, unsafe crisis | Call the local emergency number |
| Prompt assessment | New confusion, repeated choking, medication interruption, rapid decline | Contact the appropriate clinician immediately or the same day |
| Routine monitoring | Mild, stable, nonacute symptom patterns | Keep a brief record and arrange review |

These action levels synthesize [2], the 2026 Hospital Safety Guide [9], and an NHS anticipatory-care plan [10].

### Emergency now: call local emergency services

Use the emergency number for the patient’s location; in Queensland, life-threatening symptoms warrant 000, while 13 HEALTH (13 43 25 84) is for non-life-threatening advice.

- **Chest pain or serious breathlessness.** Call immediately for chest pain, extreme breathlessness, or severe difficulty breathing. These symptoms require assessment on their own merits, whether or not the person has Parkinson’s or DBS. [11]

- **Choking with an obstructed airway.** If the person cannot breathe, speak, or cough effectively, call emergency services and follow the dispatcher’s first-aid instructions. Do not give water, food, or tablets to try to clear the obstruction. Aspiration accompanied by breathing distress is also an emergency. [11]

- **Unconsciousness or failure to recover.** Call if the person is unconscious, difficult to wake, or has collapsed and is not recovering normally. A brief faint followed by full recovery still needs urgent assessment, as described below; it should not be dismissed as an ordinary Parkinson’s symptom. [10]

- **Sudden stroke-like neurological changes.** New one-sided weakness, facial drooping, an abrupt major speech change, or another sudden focal neurological deficit needs emergency assessment. Established slow speech or reduced facial expression can complicate recognition, so explain clearly what is new compared with the person’s usual condition. [12]

- **Fever with profound stiffness or immobility.** Fever or overheating combined with severe rigidity, near-complete inability to move, confusion, heavy sweating, or unstable physical condition can signal parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome. This rare emergency may follow medication interruption, infection, surgery, or interruption of established DBS treatment. Do not wait for the next clinic appointment. [2]

- **Serious injury after a fall.** Seek emergency help for a head injury with reduced alertness or neurological change, suspected fracture, severe pain, or an injury that makes safe movement impossible. Parkinson’s increases fall risk, but it does not make a fall harmless or remove the need to assess an injury. [2]

- **Hallucinations creating immediate danger.** Call for emergency help when agitation, delusions, aggression, or other behavior threatens the patient’s or another person’s physical safety. Keep yourself safe and avoid confrontation. Severe psychosis can be a medical or psychiatric emergency, not simply a difficult caregiving situation. [2]

- **Suicidal thoughts or self-harm behavior.** Obtain immediate emergency or crisis assessment; call local emergency services for an attempt, imminent danger, or inability to maintain safety. Stay with the person if it is safe to do so, and notify the neurology or DBS team. Do not postpone help while trying to determine whether Parkinson’s, medication, or stimulation caused the change. [13]

**Severe uncontrolled movements also matter.** Prolonged, exhausting involuntary movements accompanied by fever, dehydration, or physical collapse require emergency assessment. Although many dyskinesias are manageable outpatient problems, an extreme “dyskinetic storm” can cause muscle damage and systemic illness. [2]

### Prompt assessment: contact a clinician immediately or the same day

**Use an urgent route when needed.** Depending on the problem, contact the Parkinson’s nurse, neurologist, DBS team, primary-care clinician, or local urgent-care service. If the usual team cannot provide timely assessment, use the local alternative rather than leaving a rapidly changing problem unaddressed.

- **New confusion or unusual sleepiness.** Confusion, inability to concentrate, hallucinations, agitation, or unusual drowsiness developing over hours or days can indicate delirium. Arrange same-day assessment. Escalate to emergency care for markedly reduced alertness, stroke-like symptoms, severe illness, or unsafe behavior. [9]

- **Medication cannot be taken reliably.** Contact the treating team immediately if prescribed medication is interrupted, tablets cannot be swallowed, vomiting prevents doses being retained, or supplies cannot be obtained. Request a safe medication plan rather than improvising substitutions or catch-up doses. Severe immobility, fever, confusion, or breathing problems move the situation to emergency care. [8]

- **Repeated coughing or choking during meals.** Recurrent throat clearing, choking, a wet-sounding voice, difficulty swallowing pills, or suspected aspiration needs prompt medical and speech-language assessment. Fever, a chesty cough, or breathlessness after swallowing difficulties raises concern for a chest infection; severe breathing difficulty is an emergency. [14]

- **Fainting despite apparent recovery.** A faint needs immediate medical advice and urgent assessment even when the person seems well afterward. Repeated near-fainting, blurred vision, or dizziness on standing also needs review. Blood-pressure changes are one explanation, but dehydration, medicines, anemia, and cardiac problems may contribute. [15]

- **New hallucinations or compulsive behavior.** Report new visual experiences, suspicious beliefs, loss of insight, gambling, spending, binge eating, or sexual disinhibition promptly. Clinicians should assess medication effects and possible medical triggers. Do not abruptly stop a dopamine-related medicine in response. [8]

- **Rapid loss of everyday abilities.** Sudden inability to walk, transfer, eat, communicate, or manage previously familiar tasks requires urgent review. Deterioration over weeks to months also deserves earlier specialist assessment. Infection, dehydration, injury, medication changes, and device problems may be more relevant than ordinary progression. [16]

- **A head impact or unwitnessed fall.** Arrange urgent assessment after a head injury or unwitnessed fall, even if obvious injury is absent. The person may not recall loss of consciousness or explain discomfort reliably. Serious injury, reduced alertness, or new neurological symptoms require emergency care. [16]

- **DBS infection or sudden symptom return.** Contact the DBS team urgently for wound redness, swelling, drainage, fever, exposed hardware, abrupt loss of benefit, severe new dyskinesia, or unexpected behavior. Established stimulation stopping can cause rapid deterioration. Use emergency services if the person is severely unwell. [17][2]

**Constipation has its own escalation points.** One NHS anticipatory-care plan uses three days without bowel activity as a cue to contact the Parkinson’s nurse, neurologist, or primary-care clinician. Severe abdominal pain, distension, vomiting, or marked illness needs urgent assessment rather than simply increasing home constipation treatment, because intestinal obstruction-like complications can occur. [10][2]

In late-stage disease, urinary disturbance is reported in around 90% of people with Parkinson’s disease and incontinence in around 70%. Constipation is common; no bowel activity for three days warrants contact with the Parkinson’s nurse, neurologist, or primary-care clinician.

### Routine monitoring: document stable patterns and arrange review

**Stable does not mean unimportant.** Predictable wearing-off, mild longstanding dyskinesia, fatigue, constipation, soft speech, or gradually increasing difficulty with dressing can be discussed through a planned appointment when no acute danger is present. Contact the team sooner if symptoms interfere with eating, sleep, walking, independence, or caregiver capacity. Specialist advice is recommended before changing treatment for fluctuations or dyskinesia. [8]

**Describe changes against the baseline.** Useful information includes when the change began, whether it followed a dose or programming change, the last successfully taken medication, falls or injuries, fever, oral intake, bowel activity, and DBS battery or therapy status. A short description such as “normally transfers with one person, but today cannot stand” is more informative than “the Parkinson’s seems worse.” The Parkinson’s Foundation’s 2026 emergency toolkit specifically emphasizes establishing the patient’s usual condition and asking the patient and care partner what is most concerning. [12]

**The governing rule is simple.** Abrupt onset, loss of safety, airway danger, injury, or interruption of essential treatment increases urgency; a familiar diagnosis should never delay appropriate help.

## Early or initial-stage warning signs

### Recognize the movement pattern, not just shaking

**Slowness is a central clue.** Bradykinesia means abnormally slow movement, often with movements becoming smaller or less effective. Relatives may notice longer dressing times, difficulty fastening buttons, reduced dexterity, or a general reduction in spontaneous movement. Clinically, parkinsonism is defined by bradykinesia together with resting tremor, rigidity, or both; determining whether that pattern represents Parkinson’s disease requires further assessment. [7]

**Several observable changes deserve attention.** Families may notice the following, particularly when they persist, progress, or occur together:

- **A tremor while resting.** A hand or fingers may shake when relaxed, often more on one side initially. Tremor can be absent in Parkinson’s, and tremor by itself does not establish the diagnosis. [7]

- **Stiffness and reduced movement.** Rigidity can appear as persistent muscular stiffness, reduced ease of movement, or difficulty with familiar tasks. It may coexist with slowness before significant balance impairment develops. [18]

- **A changed walking pattern.** One arm may swing less, steps may become smaller or shuffling, and turning may look less fluid. Relatives sometimes notice these changes before the person recognizes them. [1]

- **Smaller, cramped handwriting.** Micrographia describes handwriting that becomes unusually small, sometimes shrinking further across a line. It is a practical example of reduced movement size rather than a separate diagnostic test. [7]

- **A softer voice or quieter face.** Hypophonia means reduced voice volume; hypomimia means reduced facial expression. These changes can make someone seem less engaged even when their interest and emotions remain present. [7]

**Daily function supplies useful context.** A change matters more when it is persistent and affects the person’s usual activities. Record concrete observations: needing longer to shave, struggling with cutlery, repeatedly being asked to speak louder, or taking smaller steps. These examples help a clinician understand the pattern without asking the family to perform a neurological examination.

**Early falls change the interpretation.** Significant postural instability and unexplained recurrent falls are less typical at the beginning of Parkinson’s than slowness, rigidity, or resting tremor. They deserve assessment for other causes or another parkinsonian disorder. Recognizing that distinction prevents the assumption that any movement problem in an older adult is simply early Parkinson’s. [7]

### Notice nonmotor symptoms without overinterpreting them

**Some clues precede movement changes.** Reduced smell, persistent constipation, dream enactment, mood changes, fatigue, and daytime sleepiness can appear before the motor diagnosis. These symptoms reflect Parkinson’s involvement beyond movement-control pathways, but each also has other possible explanations. Their significance comes from the overall history and pattern. [1]

**Smell loss and constipation are signals.** A persistent unexplained reduction in smell or new, sustained constipation is worth mentioning to a clinician, especially alongside movement changes. Constipation can precede motor manifestations by more than a decade in some people. That association does not mean most people with constipation have, or will develop, Parkinson’s. [19]

Hyposmia or loss of smell is a potential premotor sign and occurs in approximately 70–90% of people with Parkinson’s disease, including newly diagnosed untreated patients; it is not diagnostic by itself.

**Dream enactment deserves specific discussion.** Shouting, punching, kicking, or apparently acting out dreams can suggest REM sleep behavior disorder, or RBD. Normally, most muscles are temporarily inhibited during dream sleep; with RBD, that protection is impaired. It may precede motor Parkinson’s by many years and can injure the patient or bed partner, so both clinical assessment and sleep-safety measures matter. [20]

**Mood and energy changes also count.** Depression, anxiety, unexplained fatigue, and excessive daytime sleepiness should be reported when persistent or disruptive. They should not automatically be treated as reactions to getting older or to receiving a diagnosis. At the same time, clinicians need to consider sleep disorders, medication effects, and other medical or psychological explanations. [1][15]

**Fatigue differs from falling asleep.** Fatigue is a sense of exhaustion or limited energy; excessive daytime sleepiness involves an increased tendency to doze or fall asleep unintentionally. The distinction changes the safety implications, particularly for driving, cooking, and other activities requiring sustained alertness. Family observations can be important because some people underestimate their daytime sleep episodes. [15]

### Arrange evaluation before disability becomes severe

**Persistent signs warrant an appointment.** NICE recommends prompt specialist referral when Parkinson’s is suspected from tremor, stiffness, slowness, balance problems, or gait changes. There is no need to wait for a full cluster or a dramatic tremor. The purpose is to identify the cause, treat troublesome symptoms, and address safety—not to confirm a family-made diagnosis. [8]

**A cluster strengthens the referral case.** The Neurology Institute of Huntsville suggests neurological evaluation for two or more concerning signs, particularly after age 50. This is a practical referral cue, not a formal diagnostic threshold or an age restriction: a younger person, or someone with one important progressive motor change, can also need assessment. [21][8]

**Bring a concise symptom history.** Note when changes started, whether one side is more affected, which tasks have become harder, relevant sleep observations, and the current medication list. With the patient’s agreement, a partner can describe changes the patient may not have noticed. This supports the clinician’s assessment while keeping the patient involved in decisions. [19]

**The early-stage priority is recognition.** Persistent changes in movement, sleep, mood, or bodily function deserve assessment even when the person remains largely independent.

## Middle or moderate-stage warning signs

### Distinguish wearing-off from involuntary movements

**Medication benefit may become less consistent.** Wearing-off means that symptoms return before the next scheduled dose because the previous dose’s useful effect has shortened. Families may see a person walk and speak more easily at one time of day, then become slow, stiff, anxious, uncomfortable, or frozen later. “ON” describes periods of useful medication benefit; “OFF” describes periods when that benefit is insufficient. [1]

**The pattern can take several forms.** A dose may take unusually long to work, termed delayed-ON; sometimes it may appear not to work, termed failed-ON. OFF episodes can also become unpredictable. Delayed stomach emptying and altered medication absorption may contribute to delayed or failed responses, so not every difficult period means the prescription simply needs a higher dose. [1]

**Dyskinesia is a different problem.** Dyskinesias are involuntary, often writhing or flowing movements associated with dopamine-related treatment, commonly near peak levodopa benefit. They may be mild and relatively unobtrusive or interfere with sitting, eating, walking, and safety. They are not the same as resting tremor, and more movement does not necessarily mean better control. [2]

**Disease and treatment interact.** As Parkinson’s progresses, medication response becomes more vulnerable to fluctuations in dopamine delivery. Intermittent oral levodopa produces rises and falls in stimulation, interacting with disease-related changes and brain adaptation. This helps explain why both insufficient movement during OFF periods and excessive involuntary movement during other periods can occur in the same person. [1]

**Changing doses involves a trade-off.** A change that reduces an OFF period may increase troublesome dyskinesia or other side effects; reducing medication to suppress involuntary movements can worsen mobility. Dyskinesia also has different timing patterns, so a family’s assumption that all involuntary movement requires the same adjustment can be misleading. Specialist review is therefore recommended before treatment changes. [8]

**Record timing rather than guessing.** A useful diary distinguishes ON without dyskinesia, ON with dyskinesia, and OFF, alongside actual medication times and the activities affected. For example, “freezing before the lunchtime dose” provides a different clue from “unsteady despite otherwise good movement.” The goal is to help the team identify a reproducible pattern, not to make the family responsible for programming treatment. [1]

### Treat freezing, falls, and task difficulty as functional warnings

**Freezing can abruptly interrupt walking.** The person may feel that their feet are stuck to the floor, particularly when starting, turning, passing through a narrow space, or managing competing demands. If the body continues moving while the feet do not, balance can be lost. Freezing is a movement-control problem, not stubbornness or failure to try. [16]

**Falls often have several contributors.** Impaired postural responses, freezing, severe dyskinesia, low blood pressure, and nighttime trips to the toilet can all contribute. Consequently, the response should examine when and why a fall occurred rather than focus only on installing a walker or changing Parkinson’s medication. Fear after a fall can further restrict movement and independence. [2]

**Intervene without rushing the person.** When freezing occurs, create time and space, support a safe pause, and use any cueing strategy already taught by the physiotherapist. Ask for Parkinson’s-specific gait and balance assessment if freezing is new, increasing, or causing near-falls. External cueing and individualized rehabilitation can address problems that medication alone may not solve. [1]

**Everyday tasks reveal rising care needs.** Difficulty rising from a chair, turning in bed, cutting food, dressing, toileting, or maintaining hygiene signals increasing functional burden. Needing occasional help does not by itself define a formal stage, but it is a reason to assess equipment, medication response, and rehabilitation needs. Occupational therapy can often make a task safer without taking it over entirely. [1][8]

### Ask actively about speech, sleep, and autonomic symptoms

**Communication and swallowing may become harder.** Speech can become quieter or less distinct, and meals may involve slower chewing, drooling, coughing, or difficulty managing pills. Speech-language therapy addresses both communication and swallowing, but the assessments serve different purposes: a clearer voice does not establish that swallowing is safe. [22]

**Sleep disruption affects daytime safety.** Insomnia, dream enactment, difficulty turning in bed, nighttime urination, and daytime sleepiness may coexist. Some reflect Parkinson’s, some medication effects, and some a separate sleep disorder. Sudden sleep episodes require prompt review, and the person should not drive while experiencing excessive sleepiness or sleep attacks. [15][8]

Sleep disturbances affect up to 90% of people with Parkinson’s disease and may include REM sleep behavior disorder, insomnia, daytime sleepiness, and circadian disruption. For dream enactment, first make the sleep environment safe and then discuss treatment with the clinical team.

**Automatic bodily functions can change.** Autonomic symptoms include constipation, urinary urgency, incontinence, and blood-pressure drops on standing. Parkinson’s may impair the automatic narrowing of blood vessels that normally maintains blood pressure when a person stands. Dehydration, infection, dopamine-related medicines, and blood-pressure medicines can worsen the problem. [15]

**Dizziness is not always a balance problem.** Light-headedness, blurred vision, weakness, or near-fainting after standing suggests a different mechanism from freezing or impaired postural control. Clinicians assess orthostatic hypotension using lying and standing blood pressures; the usual definition is a fall of at least 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic within three minutes of standing. Families should seek advice based on symptoms rather than wait to demonstrate that threshold themselves. [18]

### Recognize cognitive, psychiatric, and behavioral changes

**Thinking changes can affect practical judgment.** Slower processing, difficulty organizing tasks, reduced attention, or trouble managing a familiar sequence may appear before dementia. A person can have mild cognitive impairment while retaining considerable independence. New difficulty managing medication, appointments, finances, or cooking should prompt cognitive and functional assessment, not an automatic assumption that all decision-making ability has been lost. [23]

**Hallucinations need a medical explanation.** A person may see people or animals that are not present, initially recognizing that the experience is unreal. Parkinson’s-related changes and medicines can contribute, but infection, dehydration, other medications, and injury can also trigger or aggravate hallucinations. Abrupt onset, distress, loss of insight, or accompanying confusion changes the urgency. [2]

**Compulsive behavior is a treatment warning.** Gambling, excessive shopping, binge eating, hypersexuality, or repetitive risky activity can arise with dopaminergic treatment, especially dopamine agonists. These behaviors may be concealed because of embarrassment or reduced insight. Ask calmly about changes, report them, and seek specialist review rather than treating the issue as a moral failing or stopping medication suddenly. [8]

**The middle-stage priority is pattern recognition.** Link symptoms to medication timing, activities, and safety while ensuring that sudden changes receive medical assessment rather than routine dose troubleshooting.

## Advanced-stage warning signs and care needs

### Dependence changes the nature of risk

**Loss of independence is the central concern.** Advanced disease may involve severe slowness or akinesia—marked difficulty initiating movement—rigidity, frequent freezing, recurrent falls, and difficult transfers. Some people become wheelchair-dependent or bedbound. Families may need help with most personal care, but the extent of assistance should be based on actual abilities rather than diagnosis alone. [4][6]

**Immobility creates additional complications.** Reduced movement can contribute to loss of muscle mass, joint tightening or contractures, pressure injuries, discomfort, and difficult transfers. These complications are not addressed simply by increasing stimulation or improving tremor. They require positioning, appropriate equipment, rehabilitation, skin care, and enough trained assistance to protect both patient and caregiver. [6]

**Repeated falls can overwhelm home arrangements.** Falls despite treatment and sensible adaptations should trigger reassessment of supervision, transfer methods, mobility aids, blood pressure, cognition, and the care setting. Continuing an unsafe arrangement because the family has managed previously can expose both people to injury. Additional home support or facility-based care may become the safer way to preserve comfort and dignity. [24]

### Swallowing problems may be more serious than they appear

**Dysphagia can remain partly hidden.** Dysphagia means difficulty swallowing; aspiration means food, liquid, saliva, or other material entering the airway. A review summarizing a meta-analysis reported swallowing difficulty in approximately 35% of people with Parkinson’s by self-report, compared with approximately 82% using objective clinician-rated measures. These figures describe different detection methods, not a stage-by-stage increase. [14]

**Absence of coughing does not ensure safety.** Parkinson’s can disrupt the speed and coordination of swallowing, and aspiration may occur silently. Watch for coughing or throat clearing during meals, wet-sounding speech, drooling, prolonged eating, difficulty with tablets, unexplained weight loss, or recurrent respiratory infections. A swallowing specialist may recommend an instrumental assessment even when the patient reports little difficulty. [14]

**Aspiration links several care problems.** Unsafe swallowing can cause pneumonia, reduce food and fluid intake, and make medication delivery unreliable. These problems can then worsen mobility and alertness, creating further difficulty eating or taking treatment. A swallowing change therefore deserves coordinated medical, speech-language, pharmacy, and nutritional attention—not only a different food texture. [2]

**Weight loss is an actionable warning.** Monitor unintentional or continuing loss, meals becoming exhausting, and declining intake. ParkinsonNet’s dietetic guideline identifies loss greater than 5% of body weight within one month or greater than 10% within six months as referral cues. Earlier review is appropriate when swallowing, hydration, or medication intake is already compromised; families should not wait to cross a numerical threshold. [25]

### Cognitive, autonomic, skin, and infection problems require active care

**Dementia changes support and communication.** Increasing memory, attention, planning, or visuospatial difficulties may eventually interfere substantially with independent daily life. Psychosis can include hallucinations, fixed false beliefs, agitation, and loss of insight. These problems can increase supervision needs even when some movement remains relatively well controlled. [4]

Dementia is a later complication: it eventually occurs in approximately 40% of people with Parkinson’s disease, while late-stage estimates are about 70–80%; figures vary with cohort and disease stage.

**Acute confusion is a separate warning.** A person with established dementia can still develop delirium from infection, dehydration, medication changes, poor sleep, or unfamiliar surroundings. A sudden decline should not be accepted as an inevitable worsening of dementia. The immediate task is assessment for a trigger and protection of safety. [9]

Late-stage psychosis may include visual hallucinations, delusions, agitation, and loss of insight; it is reported in approximately 50% of late-stage disease. Unsafe agitation is a psychiatric emergency.

**Fainting and continence problems compound dependence.** Orthostatic hypotension may cause collapse during standing or transfers; urinary urgency, nighttime urination, or incontinence can increase falls and skin-care demands. Constipation may become severe, while bladder symptoms can also reflect infection or a separate urological condition. Review should therefore include medications and other medical causes rather than attributing every change to Parkinson’s. [15]

Orthostatic hypotension affects about one-third to approximately 50% of people with Parkinson’s disease, depending on the cohort and disease stage. Dizziness, blurred vision, fainting, injurious falls, and hospitalization are warning signs that need medical review.

**Skin changes deserve early attention.** Inspect areas exposed to sustained pressure, particularly the heels, hips, and lower back. Persistent redness, broken skin, drainage, or increasing pain requires professional advice. Preventive care includes individualized repositioning, pressure-relieving equipment, clean dry skin, and adequate nutrition; the advanced-care guide recommends repositioning at least every two hours for people who are immobile. [24]

**Recurrent infections signal broader vulnerability.** Aspiration pneumonia, chest infections, urinary infections, and repeated hospital admissions should prompt review of swallowing, hydration, mobility, medication delivery, and the overall care plan. Repeatedly treating each infection without revisiting the underlying risks can leave the family managing the same crisis again. [4]

### Introduce palliative support before a crisis

**Palliative care adds practical support.** It focuses on symptom relief, comfort, emotional support, care coordination, and decisions aligned with the person’s priorities. It can accompany neurological treatment and is not limited to the final days of life. NICE recommends considering access at any stage when discussion and support would be helpful. [8]

**Certain changes strengthen the case.** Swallowing problems, a first aspiration pneumonia, recurrent infections, weight loss, marked physical decline, cognitive difficulties, difficult symptoms, and repeated admissions are recognized reasons to involve specialist palliative care. Diminishing benefit from treatment and substantial caregiver strain also make broader supportive planning important. These are care-needs signals, not an individual survival countdown. [4][26]

**Discuss future choices while possible.** Conversations can cover preferred care settings, a trusted decision-maker, emergency treatment preferences, and what should happen if tablets or oral feeding become difficult. Feeding tubes can provide nutrition and medication but do not eliminate aspiration from saliva or reflux. Decisions should follow the patient’s values and a realistic discussion of benefits, burdens, and comfort. [14][27]

**The advanced-stage priority is coordinated comfort.** Preserve meaningful abilities and connection while actively preventing complications and increasing support as needs change.

## Daily-life adjustments after DBS surgery

### Set expectations around the symptoms DBS treats

**DBS improves selected movement problems.** DBS uses implanted electrodes and a pulse generator, usually beneath the skin of the chest or abdomen, to modify abnormal activity in movement-control circuits. It can reduce tremor, stiffness, slowness, OFF time, and dyskinesia in appropriately selected patients. Some medication-resistant tremor can respond particularly well. [3]

**Better ON time is a realistic goal.** For many levodopa-responsive symptoms, DBS extends the time spent functioning near the person’s best medication response rather than creating a completely new level of ability. This distinction matters when planning daily activities: steadier movement can make dressing or outings easier, but it does not necessarily remove the need for assistance. [3]

**DBS does not treat every pathway.** Balance, speech, swallowing, cognition, depression, anxiety, sleep, and autonomic symptoms may persist or worsen. DBS is not a cure and does not stop Parkinson’s progression. Symptoms that are poorly responsive to levodopa are generally less likely to improve with DBS, apart from important exceptions such as some resistant tremor. [3]

**Nonmotor improvement is possible, not dependable.** The evidence is mixed rather than uniformly negative. For example, Dafsari and colleagues’ open prospective multicenter study of 60 patients reported a 42% improvement in aggregate nonmotor symptom burden six months after subthalamic DBS. Other studies found different results across mood, cognition, sleep, and autonomic symptoms; an improvement in a combined score does not promise relief of a particular person’s depression or swallowing difficulty. [28]

**Persistent symptoms need their own treatment.** If tremor improves but falls, poor sleep, anxiety, or choking remain, the appropriate response is a targeted review of those problems. Increasing stimulation independently is not a general solution. Medication, rehabilitation, psychological support, swallowing care, and treatment of other medical conditions remain important after technically successful surgery. [29]

### Protect healing wounds and allow recovery time

**Inspect all surgical areas regularly.** Check the scalp incisions, pulse-generator pocket, and the skin along the connecting wires. Report increasing redness, swelling, tenderness, pain, drainage, fever, or feeling systemically unwell. Skin breakdown exposing a wire or generator requires urgent advice even if it occurs long after the operation. [17]

**Follow the actual discharge instructions.** Keep wounds dry until the implanting team permits washing, follow dressing and stitch-removal arrangements, and avoid rubbing or stressing healing areas. Wound-care schedules differ: Cambridge’s guidance, for example, permits washing after dressing removal two days after surgery, whereas North Bristol emphasizes keeping wounds dry according to its discharge plan. The written instructions for the individual operation should determine the routine. [17][30]

**Recovery does not require complete inactivity.** Arrange help with strenuous household tasks and activities that strain the incisions while allowing appropriately cleared gentle movement. Mayfield Clinic’s guide describes lifting and activity restrictions for six to eight weeks while incisions heal. The restrictions should specify what the person may do, so the family does not have to choose between unsafe exertion and unnecessary immobility. [31]

During the six- to eight-week healing period, follow the implanting team’s lifting and activity restrictions and delay rigorous or impact activities until the team clears them.

**Early improvement can be temporary.** Some people feel better before the stimulator is activated because of the effects of electrode insertion. That early improvement is not a reason to stop medication or assume programming is complete. Postoperative tiredness may also last several weeks, so rest and practical help can remain necessary despite an apparently successful operation. [17]

### Plan for staged programming and medication adjustment

**Programming is part of treatment.** Activation and adjustment commonly require repeated visits, with stimulation settings and medication reviewed together. Families should plan transport, appointment support, and time to observe the effects between visits. Bring the programmer and, when requested, the charger and symptom record. [3][31]

**Activation dates differ between centers.** Mayfield describes an initial programming visit around ten days after surgery and often three or four visits to tune treatment. Cambridge describes activation at six to eight weeks. These examples show why the family should follow the implanting center’s schedule rather than judge recovery against another patient’s experience. [31][17]

**Coordinate every Parkinson’s medication change.** DBS may permit a reduction in medication, but it is not intended to replace medication automatically. The DBS or neurology team should direct changes because stimulation and medicines interact. Continue the prescribed regimen unless the team provides specific instructions, including any supervised preparation for a programming assessment. [3]

**Mood changes can follow treatment adjustments.** Apathy, depression, anxiety, or behavioral changes after surgery may relate to Parkinson’s, medication reduction, stimulation, or the psychological adjustment to surgery. Rapid dopamine-related medication withdrawal has been associated with postoperative apathy in some studies, but does not explain every case. Report changes rather than deciding that the person has become unmotivated or that DBS itself must be responsible. [28]

**Use only authorized controller functions.** Patients may have permission to make limited adjustments within clinician-set ranges. The patient and a willing care partner should be taught exactly which controls they may use, how to recognize therapy status, and whom to contact when the expected response does not occur. Avoid experimenting with settings or turning established therapy off without the clinical plan. [32]

### Keep a practical symptom and side-effect diary

**Record effects that influence daily life.** During adjustment, document actual medication times, ON and OFF periods, dyskinesia, freezing, falls, sleep, mood, swallowing, and relevant side effects. Note the date and time of clinician-directed stimulation changes. A brief, consistently completed record is more useful than a detailed diary that becomes too burdensome to maintain. [1]

**Describe both benefit and burden.** Report not only “walking improved,” but whether the person can reach the bathroom safely, communicate comfortably, or finish a meal. New slurred speech, tingling, muscle contractions, balance difficulty, or behavioral changes after adjustment should be communicated to the DBS team. Some stimulation-related problems improve with further programming. [17]

**Do not let recording delay care.** A diary helps with fluctuating or gradually changing symptoms; it is not a reason to observe an emergency for another day. Abrupt severe deterioration, airway danger, fever with immobility, or an unsafe psychiatric change follows the urgent action rules above.

### Manage the battery before treatment stops

**Battery care depends on the system.** Establish whether the implanted generator is rechargeable, the meaning of its alerts, and the replacement or charging plan.

| System | Family maintenance task | Longer-term planning |
|---|---|---|
| Nonrechargeable | Check status and report replacement alerts | Many systems need replacement after about 3–5 years |
| Rechargeable | Recharge on the prescribed schedule; confirm successful charging | Some guides describe 10–15 years or longer before replacement |

The figures are examples from Mayfield’s DBS guide [31]; actual service life depends on the model, stimulation settings, and use.

**Monthly checks do not replace charging.** A clinical review recommends checking battery level monthly with the programmer. Rechargeable systems also require their scheduled charging routine, which may be much more frequent. Families should know the difference between the implant’s battery status and the charge level of the separate controller, communicator, or charger. [16]

**Charging schedules can differ substantially.** For example, the Boston Scientific Vercise Genus patient manual describes expected routines ranging from five to thirty minutes daily, or thirty minutes to four hours every one to two weeks, depending on stimulation requirements. This is a device example, not a universal schedule. The DBS team should establish the routine that reliably maintains that patient’s treatment. [13]

**Make the routine sustainable at home.** With the patient’s agreement, a care partner can learn charging, status checks, and alert recognition, particularly if hand function, memory, or vision makes device handling difficult. Identify who can help if the usual caregiver is away. Rechargeability reduces some replacement procedures but introduces a recurring task that must remain manageable for the household.

**Treat unexpected cessation as urgent.** In someone established on active DBS, battery exhaustion, accidental switch-off, or hardware failure may cause rapid symptom return and occasionally a dangerous withdrawal syndrome. Follow the team’s taught instructions for checking or restoring therapy and contact them urgently. Severe immobility, swallowing failure, fever, confusion, or respiratory problems requires emergency assessment. [29]

### Recognize wound, hardware, and stimulation problems

**Different problems can look similar.** A sudden worsening does not establish that DBS has failed: infection, dehydration, medication interruption, injury, and other illnesses must also be considered. The pattern helps the expert team decide whether to assess programming, battery status, electrical connections, or lead integrity. [2]

| Problem category | Observable examples | Family response |
|---|---|---|
| Wound or implant infection | Redness, tenderness, drainage, fever | Urgent medical and DBS-team contact |
| Skin or hardware damage | Exposed components, injury over the system | Urgent DBS assessment |
| Interrupted stimulation | Abrupt return of stiffness, tremor, or immobility | Check as trained; contact DBS team urgently |
| Stimulation side effects | New dyskinesia, speech, balance, or behavior change | Report timing and request programming review |

These categories follow postoperative guidance [17] and the review of DBS emergencies [2].

**Expert checks may be necessary.** Clinicians can assess the generator and system impedance—an electrical measure that can help detect connection problems—and obtain imaging if lead migration or fracture is suspected. Families should report observed changes, not manipulate implanted components or repeatedly alter settings while trying to identify the cause. [2]

**Gradual decline needs a broader review.** Over time, speech, gait, postural stability, or cognition may worsen even while DBS continues to control other symptoms. Disease progression, stimulation effects, medication changes, and other medical conditions can overlap. Persistent difficulty is therefore a reason for reassessment, not evidence that all remaining problems should be solved through the stimulator. [29]

### Prepare for medical procedures and travel

**Tell every healthcare provider about DBS.** Keep the device identification card, exact model information, and DBS-team contact details accessible. Bring the patient programmer to clinic and hospital visits. Strong electrical fields can transfer energy through implanted components even when stimulation is off, so simply switching the system off does not make every procedure safe. [33]

| Situation | Required precaution | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| MRI | Confirm exact system eligibility and scanning protocol | Incorrect conditions can cause heating or injury |
| Therapeutic diathermy | Do not receive shortwave, microwave, or therapeutic-ultrasound diathermy | Energy can transfer through implanted components |
| Electrosurgery or electrocautery | Arrange advance DBS and surgical-team planning | Current can affect the device or surrounding tissue |
| Other imaging or procedures | Give staff the device details before treatment | Required precautions differ by system |
| Airport security | Show the implant card and follow screening instructions | Screening may affect stimulation or trigger alarms |

These precautions are supported by UC Davis device safety guidance [33] and perioperative safety recommendations [34].

**MRI-conditional does not mean unrestricted.** Some DBS systems permit MRI only under specified conditions, potentially including eligibility checks and an MRI setting. The imaging center and DBS team must confirm the requirements for the complete implanted system. Do not assume that another patient’s scan, or possession of an “MRI mode,” establishes eligibility. [33]

**Distinguish deep-heat treatment from surgical tools.** Therapeutic shortwave, microwave, and ultrasound diathermy are prohibited because energy can travel through the implanted system and injure tissue. Surgical electrocautery is a separate planning issue: clinicians may need a surgery mode or temporary therapy interruption, equipment precautions, and a check afterward. Families should disclose the implant rather than attempt to prescribe the procedural settings. [13][34]

**Other procedures still need disclosure.** Dentistry, CT, diagnostic ultrasound, radiation treatment, and other interventions may have system-specific precautions; they are not all automatically prohibited. Ask the treating service to coordinate with the DBS team or manufacturer’s instructions. Emergency life-saving treatment, including defibrillation when required, should not be withheld because a DBS device is present; clinicians can take appropriate precautions and assess the system afterward. [33][34]

**Travel requires equipment and continuity.** Carry the identification card, programmer, and charger if applicable, along with current medication information and specialist contacts. Security equipment may cause unexpected stimulation or switch therapy on or off in some systems; follow the device-specific screening advice. Obtain postoperative travel clearance—Cambridge, for example, advises no flying for six weeks after surgery. [17]

### Resume driving and exercise through assessment

**Driving requires more than improved movement.** Resume only after the treating consultant considers the person fit and applicable licensing requirements have been met. Alertness, cognition, vision, unpredictable OFF periods, and medication-related sleep attacks matter as well as tremor. Someone who becomes unexpectedly sleepy should not drive, even if their motor symptoms appear well controlled. [17][8]

**Exercise should return progressively.** Once wounds have healed and the team has cleared activity, rehabilitation can help rebuild confidence, strength, balance, and endurance. Avoid repeated hard blows over the implanted system and use normal protective helmets for activities where they are ordinarily recommended. Activity selection must also account for falls, fainting, and coordination—not just protection of the hardware. [33]

**Water activities need an explicit plan.** Some centers advise particular restrictions because stimulation-related coordination changes may affect safety in water. Do not independently turn DBS off to swim or assume that general exercise clearance covers swimming, diving, or high-risk sports. Ask the DBS and rehabilitation teams for advice suited to the person’s abilities and system. [17]

**The post-DBS priority is reliable treatment.** Protect healing, maintain medication and stimulation, and use specialist follow-up to improve real-life function rather than chasing symptom control through unsupervised adjustments.

## Support strategies that improve comfort and well-being after DBS

### Adapt the home while preserving independence

**Make frequently used routes safer.** Remove loose obstacles and trailing cables, improve lighting, and assess access to the bed, bathroom, kitchen, and favorite chair. Occupational therapy can identify practical adaptations such as suitable seating, rails, bathroom equipment, or changes to the arrangement of everyday items. The best adaptations match the person’s actual movement and cognitive difficulties. [2][8]

**Choose mobility aids through assessment.** A walking aid should match the person’s balance, freezing pattern, hand function, and ability to use it. Wheelchairs and transfer equipment may be appropriate for particular situations even when some walking remains possible. The purpose is safe access and participation, not a symbolic decision that the person has “given up” walking. [2]

**Use physiotherapy for specific tasks.** Parkinson’s-specific physiotherapy can address gait, turning, balance, strength, posture, transfers, and range of motion. Ask for strategies for the situations that actually cause difficulty, such as moving through a doorway or getting out of bed. Exercise can support function, but persistent falls still require assessment rather than reassurance that more exercise alone will solve them. [8][2]

**Learn transfers before they become hazardous.** Request hands-on training for both the patient and caregiver, ideally in the home environment. Transfer boards, sit-to-stand equipment, or a mechanical lift may be needed when assistance becomes physically demanding. A caregiver’s back injury can suddenly make the entire care arrangement unsafe, so equipment should be introduced before injury occurs. [24]

**Help without automatically taking over.** Occupational therapy can simplify dressing, eating, writing, household tasks, and hobbies while preserving the patient’s contribution. Allow sufficient time and discuss what assistance the person wants. Maintaining independence means enabling valued activities safely, not insisting that every task be done alone or completing everything for the person. [1]

**Positioning is part of comfort.** For someone spending prolonged periods in bed or a chair, use the agreed repositioning and pressure-relief plan, inspect skin, and maintain gentle movement as advised. Physiotherapy can teach range-of-motion exercises to reduce joint tightening and discomfort. New pain during movement or care should be reported rather than overcome by force. [6]

### Make eating, drinking, and communication easier

**Request swallowing assessment when signs appear.** A speech-language pathologist can assess swallowing and recommend instrumental testing when needed. A videofluoroscopic swallowing study uses moving X-ray images; flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing, or FEES, uses a small camera to examine swallowing-related function. These assessments help determine the problem rather than relying solely on visible coughing. [14]

**Use an individualized mealtime plan.** Recommendations may include smaller bites or sips, slower pacing, posture changes, or different food and liquid textures. Avoid assuming that thickened drinks or puréed food are automatically the correct answer for everyone. The plan should balance swallowing safety with adequate intake, enjoyment, and the person’s preferences. [14]

**Provide upright, unhurried meals.** Advanced-care guidance recommends sitting as close to upright as possible during meals and remaining upright for at least thirty minutes afterward. Meals may be easier during a predictable ON period, and smaller, more frequent meals can help when eating is tiring. Repeated choking, declining alertness, or inability to manage medication requires clinical advice rather than continued attempts to feed. [24]

**Monitor weight and hydration together.** Track weight trends, meal duration, intake, and difficulties obtaining enough fluid safely. A dietitian can adapt calorie density and meal structure, including when swallowing restrictions are necessary. After DBS, weight gain can also occur, so nutrition review should respond to the person’s actual direction of change rather than assume that undernutrition is the only risk. [14][17]

**Do not reduce protein indiscriminately.** If levodopa response appears related to meals, ask the neurologist and dietitian whether meal timing or protein distribution could help. NICE specifically advises against reducing total daily protein intake. Protecting motor response should not inadvertently worsen nutrition, muscle strength, or enjoyment of food. [8]

**Support speech as well as swallowing.** Speech therapy can address low volume, unclear articulation, and communication effort. When speech becomes difficult, alternative communication methods or equipment may help. Face the person, reduce background noise, allow time, and ask for clarification rather than pretending to understand or routinely finishing their sentences. [8][35]

### Address constipation, bladder problems, blood pressure, and sleep

**Create a sustainable bowel routine.** Record bowel activity and discuss diet, fluid intake, mobility, and medication contributors with the team. A prescribed bowel plan may include regular treatment rather than repeated crisis management. Fiber should be increased according to tolerance and alongside adequate fluid; severe slow transit can make indiscriminate increases uncomfortable. [15]

**Coordinate fluids with the whole condition.** Hydration needs must account for swallowing safety, constipation, urinary symptoms, blood pressure, and heart, liver, or kidney disease. Do not substantially restrict drinking simply to reduce toilet trips, or increase salt and fluid aggressively without clinical advice. Measures that help one symptom may worsen another, so the plan should be shared across the relevant clinicians. [36]

**Reduce the hazards of toileting.** Night lighting, an accessible route, appropriate assistance, and occupational-therapy equipment can reduce the difficulty of responding to urgency or nighttime urination. New urinary pain, fever, or a marked change from the usual pattern needs medical advice. Bladder symptoms can arise from Parkinson’s, but infection and non-neurological conditions must also be considered. [15]

**Respond safely to postural symptoms.** Help the person change position gradually and sit or lie safely if light-headedness occurs. Ask for a lying-and-standing blood-pressure assessment and medication review when symptoms recur. Clinicians may recommend compression, meal adjustments, or other measures, but the underlying cause and coexisting conditions determine which options are suitable. [36]

**Make sleep safer before adding treatment.** For dream enactment, remove nearby hard or sharp hazards and discuss protection of both patient and bed partner with the sleep or Parkinson’s team. For insomnia, consider pain, nighttime OFF periods, urinary symptoms, sleep apnea, and medicine effects. A regular routine and appropriate daytime activity can support sleep, but persistent problems need an explanation rather than simply stronger sedation. [15]

**Protect daytime alertness and participation.** Report dozing during meals, conversations, or activities, especially after medication changes. Treatment may require reviewing both nighttime sleep and daytime medicines. Safer alertness supports not only driving and mobility but conversation, enjoyable activities, and the caregiver’s ability to rest. [15]

### Support cognition, mood, and the person’s sense of self

**Simplify without infantilizing the person.** Consistent routines, less clutter, clear labels, short sentences, and one instruction at a time can reduce cognitive demands. Offer manageable choices and preserve the person’s involvement. Slow movement, quiet speech, or reduced facial expression should not be treated as evidence that the person has nothing to say or cannot decide. [24][8]

**Ask about emotional well-being directly.** Depression, anxiety, apathy, irritability, loss of interest, and changes in impulse control deserve attention after DBS. Ask the patient how they feel rather than relying only on appearance or movement improvement. New or significant changes should be reported promptly; suicidal thoughts follow the immediate crisis pathway. [13]

**Use appropriate psychological and cognitive support.** Cognitive behavioral therapy can help with depression, anxiety, and fear of progression; cognitive training or stimulation may be appropriate for particular cognitive problems. These interventions complement medication and rehabilitation rather than requiring the family to become therapists. The choice should reflect the person’s abilities, goals, and clinical assessment. [36]

**Respond calmly to hallucinations.** Reduce overstimulation, offer reassurance, and avoid arguing aggressively about what the person perceives. Report the experience so the team can assess triggers and need for treatment. Familiar, well-tolerated hallucinations may not require an antipsychotic, whereas distress, loss of insight, or unsafe behavior changes the care needs substantially. [8]

**Maintain enjoyable social roles.** Adapt hobbies, visits, exercise groups, music, or other meaningful activities to current energy, mobility, and communication. The aim is participation that the person values, not an exhausting schedule intended to demonstrate recovery. Exercise and allied-health support can help preserve activity and reduce isolation-related dependence. [19]

### Include caregivers in recovery and support

**Motor improvement does not erase caregiving strain.** After DBS, a partner may still manage charging, medication, appointments, falls, sleep disruption, or behavior changes. The patient may also want greater independence, requiring both people to renegotiate routines. Qualitative caregiver research describes benefits alongside feeling unprepared and uncertainty about changing family roles. [37]

**Caregiver outcomes are not uniformly positive.** In a small study summarized by that review, twenty caregivers remained at one-year follow-up, and more than half rated their subjective well-being negatively. This does not predict another family’s outcome, but it illustrates why successful motor treatment should not be assumed to resolve a partner’s depression, anxiety, or workload. [37]

**Attend appointments with permission.** A care partner can help verify medication schedules, describe day-to-day function, learn controller use, and retain instructions. The patient should remain central to decisions, with opportunities to discuss sensitive issues privately when appropriate. Clear information and partner participation are specifically recommended in the DBS caregiver literature. [37]

**Arrange breaks before exhaustion becomes dangerous.** Share tasks where possible and consider home-care assistance, adult day services, or planned respite. Caregiver support should include their own medical care, sleep, psychological needs, and opportunities for social contact. Regular assessment is important because a family can appear to be coping while one person is becoming physically or emotionally depleted. [27]

**Escalate when the arrangement becomes unsafe.** Repeated injuries, inability to provide required supervision, severe sleep deprivation, or caregiver exhaustion should prompt reassessment and additional help. More support may mean changing staffing, equipment, home services, or the care setting. That decision should reflect both the patient’s preferences and what can be provided safely. [24]

### Prepare for emergencies and measure meaningful outcomes

**Keep an emergency folder and bag.** Include the exact medication names, formulations, doses, and clock times; current clinician contacts; allergies and relevant medical information; DBS identification and model details; the programmer; and applicable advance-care documents. The Parkinson’s Foundation’s 2026 guide recommends current medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles with at least a two-day supply. Hospital use of patient-supplied medication should be coordinated with staff. [9]

**Protect medication timing during hospital care.** Explain that the person’s regimen may not match standard ward medication rounds. The Parkinson’s Foundation’s 2026 emergency toolkit sets a care standard of administering Parkinson’s medication within fifteen minutes of the home schedule. Raise delays or swallowing problems promptly with staff so they can arrange a safe route and schedule; this target is a hospital safety standard, not a reason to improvise doses. [12]

**Ask staff to review new medicines.** Some commonly used dopamine-blocking drugs, including metoclopramide for nausea and haloperidol for agitation, can worsen Parkinson’s symptoms. Ensure clinicians know the diagnosis and ask the pharmacist or Parkinson’s team to review new treatments and interactions. This matters even when admission is for an unrelated problem. [35]

**Track outcomes beyond tremor scores.** At follow-up, discuss falls and near-falls, everyday independence, ON/OFF patterns, swallowing, weight, pain, sleep, mood, cognition, social participation, and caregiver strain. A patient who can pursue a valued activity comfortably may have achieved an important benefit even if some tremor remains. Conversely, improved movement should not conceal worsening swallowing or emotional distress. [5][27]

**Keep the plan shared and specific.** Agree who coordinates care, which symptoms trigger contact, who manages charging and medication, and what support is available outside normal clinic hours. Neurology and DBS teams should work with primary care, rehabilitation, speech-language therapy, dietetics, mental-health services, and palliative care as needed. Treatment decisions should include other illnesses, medication interactions, the patient’s preferences, and the family’s capacity. [8]

**The support priority is meaningful daily life.** A successful plan protects comfort, dignity, participation, and sustainable caregiving—not simply the appearance of better movement.

**Interpret the figures in context.** This international guide reflects the supplied evidence through 13 September 2026 and does not replace individual diagnosis or treatment advice. Stage groupings describe overlapping patterns; quoted percentages reflect particular populations and measurement methods. Recovery, charging, and battery-life figures are source-specific examples, while emergency routes, procedural precautions, and treatment decisions depend on local services and the exact patient and DBS system.

**Notice change and act early.** Families are most helpful when they recognize departures from the person’s usual condition, preserve treatment continuity, and seek the right level of help while keeping the patient’s own priorities at the center of care.
