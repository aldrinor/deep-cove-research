# Can a 500,000-Passenger Airport Generate Significant Regional Socioeconomic Impacts?

**The hypothesis receives qualified support:** an airport handling 500,000 passengers annually can generate measurable employment, income, tourism, business and accessibility benefits for its surrounding region. However, the airport-year evidence below consists of near-threshold observations, rather than a documented case of exactly 500,000 total annual passenger movements. Glasgow Prestwick, with approximately 525,000 reported passengers in calendar year 2023, supported an estimated £26.6 million in Scottish gross value added and 524 full-time-equivalent jobs through its mixed aviation activities. Those figures establish an economic contribution, not a passenger-generated net benefit. [1]

**Passenger volume is not sufficient:** stronger causal studies find positive regional effects from improved air connectivity, but also substantial variation, including null findings. Public audits, airport-finance studies and property-market research show why subsidies, competing airports, noise and other costs can weaken the regional return. The defensible conclusion is therefore “can, under favorable conditions,” not “will, once 500,000 passengers are reached.” [2][3]

**The report resolves six questions:** what the passenger threshold measures; which airports provide the closest comparisons; how benefits arise; what costs and distributional effects accompany them; how much activity can be causally attributed to air service; and what conclusion follows for regional policy.

| Question | Strongest evidence | What it establishes | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can activity be measured? | Prestwick’s accounts and multiplier assessment | Jobs and value added at a nearby scale | Yes |
| Can air service affect regional growth? | European and U.S. policy-shock studies | Positive effects under identified conditions | Conditional support |
| Can the local magnitude matter? | Prestwick’s employment benchmark; small-airport income studies | Potentially material sectoral and household effects | Context-dependent |
| Does 500,000 ensure financial viability? | European profitability evaluations | Persistent fixed-cost and revenue pressures | No |
| Does gross activity establish net benefit? | Subsidy, noise and displacement evidence | Benefits and costs fall on different groups | A separate judgment |

## 1. The hypothesis and the 500,000-passenger threshold

### The question concerns possibility, not a universal threshold

**The wording matters economically:** the proposition being tested is that an airport handling exactly **500,000 passengers per year can generate significant and measurable socioeconomic impacts in its surrounding region**. This is a possibility claim. It does not require every airport of that size to produce the same outcome, nor does it imply that 500,000 is a minimum viable scale.

**Two stronger claims require separation:** “500,000 passengers guarantees significant regional growth” and “500,000 passengers guarantees a positive net social return” go beyond the original hypothesis. Evidence of a subsidy-dependent or economically weak airport challenges those stronger claims. It does not, by itself, refute the possibility that another airport at the same scale provides substantial benefits.

**There is no economic discontinuity here:** the mechanisms identified in the literature operate through useful routes, service frequency, visitor expenditure, procurement, employment and business access. They do not suggest that an otherwise unchanged airport suddenly becomes economically consequential when its annual count moves from just below to just above 500,000. The threshold is a useful scale for comparison, not an established development law. [2]

### Passenger movements are not unique travelers

**The central measurement distinction:** total throughput, boardings and unique people are different quantities. An airport serving 500,000 arrivals-plus-departures passenger movements is ordinarily much smaller than one recording 500,000 boardings alone.

| Passenger measure | What it counts | Relevant source convention | Treatment here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrivals plus departures | Enplaned and deplaned passenger movements | MSP explicitly uses both | Reference total-throughput convention |
| Enplanements | Passenger boardings | FAA calendar-year series | Kept separate from total throughput |
| Terminal passengers | Source-labeled terminal activity | UK CAA tables | Retained in source-native units |
| Handled passengers | Boarded, transit and disembarked passengers | João Paulo II study | Transit-inclusive handled total |
| Unique travelers | Distinct people over the period | Requires deduplication across journeys | Not inferred from movements |

**The reference arithmetic is simple:** under an arrivals-plus-departures convention, annual throughput is \(P=E+D\), where \(E\) denotes enplanements and \(D\) deplanements. If arrivals and departures balance, exactly 500,000 total movements corresponds to an estimated 250,000 enplanements and 250,000 deplanements: \(500,000÷2\). New York State’s airport methodology explicitly uses long-run equality between enplanements and deplanements as a simplifying assumption. This is a conditional conversion, not an observed split for the UK comparators. [4]

**A return trip illustrates the difference:** a person departing from a regional airport and later returning normally contributes two movements at that airport. Repeated journeys by the same person add further movements. Thus even 250,000 return trips would not necessarily represent 250,000 distinct travelers.

**FAA data require particular care:** the FAA defines its main airport passenger series as passenger boardings, or enplanements, collected for January 1–December 31. Its Airport Improvement Program convention also includes certain passengers remaining onboard international flights transiting a U.S. airport for non-traffic purposes. Consequently, “500,000 FAA passengers” should not be silently read as 500,000 total arrivals and departures. [5][6]

**The UK labels remain explicit:** the CAA tables identify “terminal passengers,” while the Exeter and Newquay operator reports use passenger numbers or PAX. Their figures are retained under those labels rather than assigned an unsupported boarding, arrival or unique-person definition. By contrast, MSP explicitly counts enplaned plus deplaned passengers, and the João Paulo II study explicitly adds boarded, transit and disembarked passengers. [7][8][9]

MSP recorded more than 37.5 million total passengers in 2016, including all enplaned and deplaned passengers; more than 21 million were origin-and-destination passengers and more than 7.8 million were connecting passengers. These are throughput categories, not unique travelers.

### “Measurable” and “significant” answer different questions

**Measurability is well supported:** airport employment can be counted, wages and operating expenditures obtained from accounts, and passenger purpose and visitor spending estimated through surveys. Supplier and household-spending effects can then be modeled. These procedures measure an airport-related economic footprint, although the resulting components differ in how directly they are observed.

**Statistical significance concerns uncertainty:** a statistical test asks whether a relationship is distinguishable from a specified null hypothesis under the model’s assumptions. For example, the 2026 João Paulo II study reports \(p=0.046\) for a passenger–employment relationship using six annual observations and a 0.05 reference threshold. That result is statistically significant within its specification; it is not proof of regional causation or a large social benefit. [9]

**Economic significance concerns scale:** 500 supported jobs may be important in a small, weak labor market but modest in a diversified metropolitan economy. Appropriate comparisons include airport-supported employment relative to regional employment, value added relative to regional GDP, wage income per resident, and accessibility gains relative to realistic alternative transport arrangements.

**Net significance concerns alternatives:** an airport can sustain visible jobs while consuming resources that would otherwise support different activity. A regional benefit judgment therefore differs from a description of current spending. It considers what changes relative to an alternative—including access through another airport, improved surface transport, reduced service or closure—and who receives the gains and bears the costs.

### The surrounding region needs a defined boundary

**Geography changes the apparent impact:** the relevant studies use counties, metropolitan areas, administrative regions and distance-based catchments. Prestwick’s core contribution is estimated for Scotland, while its local employment discussion concerns South Ayrshire. Victoria Regional’s principal results concern Victoria County, Texas. MSP uses seven counties, and Philadelphia International uses an 11-county metropolitan area across four states. These are different economic boundaries, not interchangeable definitions of “local.” [1][10][11]

**The practical distinction is retention:** a supplier purchase contributes to a county impact only to the extent that production occurs within that county. A worker’s wages may support household spending outside the airport municipality. A visitor may land at one airport but stay and spend elsewhere. Broadening the boundary generally captures more activity, but answers a different question.

**Section judgment:** a 500,000-passenger test is meaningful only when passenger units, outcome measures, reporting periods and regional boundaries are kept explicit.

### The closest reported-passenger cases

Historical observations between 394,000 and 639,000 provide an extended context. These bands organize the comparison; they do not establish that all airports within them have equivalent services or economic effects.

### Prestwick: the closest detailed impact anchor

**Prestwick supports the possibility claim:** an airport with passenger numbers close to 500,000 can operate an economically consequential aviation business. Its estimated contribution includes 291 direct FTEs, with additional supplier and household-spending effects. However, its operating model is especially important to interpreting that finding.

**Passengers are a minority revenue stream:** in FY2023, the government assessment’s revenue breakdown assigns 78% to fixed-base-operator and fueling activities, 7% to passenger services, 6% to cargo, 6% to property and 3% to aerodrome services. Fixed-base operations include services to military and executive aircraft. The £26.6 million GVA figure therefore describes the airport’s mixed core activities, not an effect attributable solely to approximately 525,000 passengers. [1]

**Its connectivity is also distinctive:** Ryanair is the scheduled passenger carrier, serving ten European leisure destinations in the assessment. The passenger catchment includes Ayrshire and the west of Scotland, with motorway access and a dedicated railway station. Glasgow Airport is approximately 34.1 miles away, making alternative-airport access relevant to any counterfactual. Prestwick also handled 27.5% of Scottish cargo volume in CY2023, compared with approximately 2.1% of Scottish passenger volume. [1]

**Business growth need not track passengers:** between FY2019 and FY2023, reported passenger numbers fell from 662,000 to 459,000, while total revenue increased from approximately £25 million to £58 million. The expansion of fueling and specialist handling explains why passenger throughput alone is an incomplete indicator of the airport’s economic importance. Calendar-year 2023’s approximately 525,000 passengers and FY2023’s 459,000 belong to different reporting periods. [1]

### Exeter: route quality alongside passenger growth

**Exeter offers a passenger-focused comparison:** its operator’s separate, undated release reports 582,347 passengers for a year ending March 31, up 32%, or more than 139,000 passengers. The same release quotes £54 million GVA and approximately 780 locally supported jobs, alongside around 230 people directly employed by the airport operator. The CAA’s 572,000 terminal passengers for calendar year 2025 provides the clearly dated annual traffic anchor. [14][7]

**Connectivity explains more than volume:** Exeter’s daily Amsterdam service averaged approximately 80% seat occupancy and opened access to more than 160 onward destinations through KLM’s hub. TUI based a second aircraft, doubling its own Exeter capacity and adding Ibiza, while Manchester service returned after a five-year absence. The airport reported 28 destinations in ten countries. [14]

**The implication is service-specific:** a hub connection can improve access to many markets without requiring direct regional flights to every destination. That mechanism is relevant to business travel and residents’ access, whereas additional holiday capacity may mainly support leisure travel. Exeter’s figures show a substantial associated local footprint, but the 780 supported jobs and 230 operator employees have different boundaries; their difference should not automatically be classified as indirect or induced employment.

### Newquay: tourism, local procurement and concurrent development

**Newquay supplies below-threshold evidence:** the operator reports 400,311 PAX in FY2024/25 and approximately £100 million annual regional GVA. This supports the proposition that a small passenger airport can be associated with a sizable regional contribution even below 500,000 reported passengers. It also illustrates why study boundaries matter: its reported contribution is broader than Prestwick’s core-operations estimate. [12]

**Its local economy creates both opportunity and vulnerability:** the 2015–2030 masterplan describes tourism dependence and relatively weak economic-activity, skills and wellbeing indicators in Newquay and surrounding parishes. Airport-related visitor demand may therefore matter to local service employment. At the same time, housing and enterprise-zone development are changing the surrounding economy independently: the plan envisages up to 3,750 homes at Nansledan and up to 5,000 jobs from the Enterprise Zone and associated development. Those projections are not observed passenger-generated employment. [15]

**Procurement gives a tangible local channel:** Newquay reports relationships with 38 café and retail businesses and states that 92% of its suppliers are local. That percentage describes suppliers, not necessarily the proportion of procurement expenditure retained locally. The report also records 300 outreach hours and a provisional £5,979,288 social-value contribution, expressly subject to Social Value Portal validation. These indicators should remain separate from GVA. [12]

### Waterloo: a development trigger, not an impact threshold

**Waterloo distinguishes capacity from consequence:** the local newspaper reports 523,210 passengers as “last year” in its 2024 growth context, with Flair, WestJet, Air Canada and Sunwing among the carriers described. Its busiest August recorded 82,531 passengers. The measure remains the newspaper’s reported passenger count, rather than a harmonized arrival-plus-departure total. [13]

**Exactly 500,000 appears in planning:** the Region of Waterloo’s 2017–2022 business plan used 500,000 annual passengers as a Phase 2 terminal-and-runway development trigger. It assigned C$74,370,500 to that planned phase, approximately dated 2019, and C$117.3 million to a subsequent one-million-passenger phase, approximately dated 2025. These are historical planning scenarios and investment amounts, not realized regional GDP or employment effects. [16]

### U.S. boarding comparisons and wider channel cases

**Boarding counts change case selection:** Fairbanks looks close to 500,000 in an FAA table, but its 563,672 enplanements represent departures rather than total throughput. Conversely, Eagle County’s 289,867 enplanements imply a total passenger scale close to the target under a balanced-flow assumption.

| Airport and location | Observed passenger measure | Period | Estimated arrivals-plus-departures equivalent | Evidence role |
|---|---:|---|---:|---|
| Fairbanks International, Fairbanks, Alaska, USA | 563,672 enplanements | CY2025 | 1,127,344, assuming equal arrivals | Boarding-definition comparison |
| Eagle County Regional, Eagle County, Colorado, USA | 289,867 enplanements | CY2024 | 579,734, assuming equal arrivals | Near-scale conditional comparison |

Fairbanks’ figure comes from the FAA’s final CY2025 table [17]; Eagle County’s comes from its March 4, 2025 release [18]. Both estimated equivalents are calculated as twice observed enplanements, using the explicitly stated balancing assumption.

**Eagle County strengthens the comparative case:** its release, citing Colorado’s 2025 economic-impact study, reports US$783.4 million in business revenues, 4,404 supported jobs and US$269.3 million in annual payroll associated with airport activity. New Alaska Airlines and Frontier routes accompanied its traffic growth. These are economic-impact estimates, not a causal net-benefit calculation, but they show how a tourism-oriented airport near the target’s estimated total-passenger scale can have a large associated footprint. [18]

**Wider cases answer narrower questions:** the following airports establish passenger-purpose, statistical or accounting mechanisms. Their larger passenger totals make them supporting channel evidence rather than near-threshold substitutes.

**Section judgment:** the closest cases support a meaningful economic footprint around the target scale, but differences in business model and measurement explain more than small differences in passenger totals.

## 3. Positive impacts: employment, income, production and access

### Direct, indirect and induced effects at Prestwick

**The clearest decomposition is Prestwick’s:** its FY2022/23 core activity supports an estimated 524 FTEs and £26.6 million Scottish GVA. Direct figures originate in airport accounts; indirect and induced figures are calculated using Scottish input-output multipliers.

| Impact category | Employment, FY2022/23 | Scottish GVA |
|---|---:|---:|
| Direct airport activity | 291 FTEs | £14.8 million |
| Indirect supplier activity | 146 FTEs | £7.4 million |
| Induced household spending | 87 FTEs | £4.4 million |
| Total | 524 FTEs | £26.6 million |

The decomposition and definitions come from the Scottish Government’s core-impact assessment [1].

**The mechanism is economically straightforward:** direct activity employs airport staff and creates value through operations. Procurement supports immediate suppliers and their supply chains. Spending by airport and supplier employees supports further demand in shops, housing services and other local industries.

**The multipliers are total relationships:** Prestwick uses Type I and Type II employment multipliers of 1.5 and 1.8. Applying the latter gives \(291×1.8=523.8\), rounded to 524 FTEs. It does not mean adding 1.8 times direct employment to the original 291 jobs. The indirect and induced components are the additional portions of that total. [1]

**Its local benchmark is material:** the assessment describes airport-supported employment as approximately 23% of South Ayrshire’s Transport and Storage sector, which employed around 2,250 people in 2021. The arithmetic, \(524÷2,250≈23.3\%\), demonstrates the scale comparison. It should not be interpreted as a measured 23% net increase in that sector: the numerator includes modeled Scottish supplier and induced employment, while the denominator is a local sectoral headcount. [1]

**The relevant judgment is therefore twofold:** hundreds of FTEs and tens of millions of pounds of value added can be substantively important around a small airport. Nevertheless, the geographic distribution of those jobs and the portion attributable to passenger services must remain separate from the airport-wide Scottish total.

### Victoria Regional: income and value added within one county

**Victoria provides a complete county account:** Glupker and Isely’s October 2022 study estimates annual airport-associated output of US$44.7 million, earnings of US$11.4 million, 171 supported jobs and US$18 million value added in Victoria County, Texas.

| Impact category | Output | Earnings | Supported jobs | Value added |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Direct | US$32.7 million | US$7.7 million | 97 | US$12.0 million |
| Indirect | US$7.2 million | US$2.2 million | 39 | US$3.4 million |
| Induced | US$4.8 million | US$1.5 million | 35 | US$2.6 million |
| Total annual estimate | US$44.7 million | US$11.4 million | 171 | US$18.0 million |

These are IMPLAN estimates for the county, combining airport financial information and business-survey inputs. [10]

**Output and GDP are different:** Victoria’s US$44.7 million output includes transactions and intermediate purchases across businesses. Its US$18 million value-added measure removes intermediate-input duplication and is the concept appropriate for comparison with regional GDP. Earnings are another component, not an additional benefit to add on top of value added.

**Tenants demonstrate the business channel:** the study estimates US$31.2 million annual tenant output, US$7.2 million earnings, 108 supported jobs and US$11.5 million value added. These tenant effects form part of the combined airport total; adding them again would double count. Among 13 businesses analyzed within the tenant exercise, four said the airport influenced their location decision and three attributed some revenue to airport proximity. [10]

**The operating context prevents passenger extrapolation:** Victoria Regional is a 1,700-acre, two-runway mixed-use airport with commercial service, general aviation and military fueling. It recorded 55,025 flight operations in 2021, compared with 36,804 in 2020; approximately 81% were military and 65% local. Those categories overlap rather than sum to 100%. Daily Houston service began in November 2020. This is an operations-based channel case, not a passenger-throughput member of the 500,000 comparison set. [10]

**Fiscal effects are measurable too:** the study estimates US$131,584 annual tax impact for Victoria County and US$194,195 for municipalities, principally the City of Victoria. Their calculated combined amount is US$325,779 annually. These are modeled tax receipts associated with activity, rather than a fiscal surplus after airport expenditures and public support. [10]

### A small-county benchmark puts magnitude in perspective

**Baraboo supplies a population denominator:** Wisconsin DOT’s 2016 study estimates that Baraboo–Wisconsin Dells Regional Airport supported 98 FTE jobs, US$3,611,796 in wage income and US$13,253,523 in local output in Sauk County, Wisconsin. The county population in the report is 62,207, and the City of Baraboo population is 12,023. Its economy includes leisure and hospitality, trade and transportation, and manufacturing. [20]

**The per-resident calculations clarify scale:** dividing the county impacts by 62,207 gives approximately US$213 annual gross output and US$58 wage income per resident; \(98÷62,207×1,000≈1.58\) FTE jobs per 1,000 residents. These are calculated contribution ratios, not household income payments or causal gains for every resident.

**Its categories show where benefits arise:** the 98 FTEs comprise 13 direct airport jobs, 54 jobs associated with airport-user spending and 31 multiplier jobs. The corresponding wage incomes are US$1,007,656, US$1,470,852 and US$1,133,288. The airport-user category measures spending by visitors residing outside the county, making the regional boundary explicit. [20]

**This remains a different airport type:** Baraboo–Wisconsin Dells is a municipally owned general-aviation airport, with 29,930 aircraft operations and 44 based aircraft reported for 2016. Its relevance is that modest aviation facilities can have measurable local activity and public-service functions—not that it validates the target passenger total. The report identifies business travel, emergency medical flights, law enforcement, agricultural spraying and pilot training among its functions. [20]

**The boundary expansion is instructive:** adding effects elsewhere in Wisconsin raises the reported contribution to 104 jobs, US$4,465,394 wage income and US$14,777,404 output. Direct local-and-state public revenue totals US$279,631, including airport storage, landing and lease revenue. Larger totals here arise partly because a larger area captures additional spending, not because the airport’s activity has changed. [20]

### Large airports show channel composition, not small-airport forecasts

**The metropolitan evidence separates accounting measures:** MSP and Philadelphia demonstrate how operational employment, visitor spending, capital investment and earnings can be recorded within an explicit region. Their much larger passenger volumes mean that their per-passenger averages are not adopted as forecasts for a small airport.

| Airport contribution | Jobs | Annual wages or earnings | Annual gross output | Defined region |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| MSP ongoing operations, 2016 | Approximately 54,500 | US$2.8 billion | US$13.1 billion | Seven Twin Cities counties |
| MSP operations, visitors and capital, 2016 | 86,900 | US$3.7 billion | US$15.9 billion | Same seven counties |
| Philadelphia International, 2017 report | 96,260 | US$4.819 billion | US$15.449 billion | Eleven-county Philadelphia MSA |

These are modeled contributions reported by InterVISTAS for MSP [8] and Econsult Solutions for Philadelphia [11].

**MSP also reports value added:** ongoing operations contributed an estimated US$7.2 billion regional GDP, while its combined operations, visitor and capital estimate was US$8.8 billion. The ongoing-operation FTE decomposition is 18,700 direct, 13,100 indirect and 16,500 induced, totaling 48,300 FTEs. This distinguishes full-time-equivalent work from approximately 54,500 job positions. [8]

**Job quality can matter alongside counts:** MSP’s report gives average direct wages of US$67,900 per FTE, compared with US$51,330 statewide and US$49,630 nationally. Around 85% of direct jobs were full-time and 96% permanent. These are useful examples of wage and stability indicators, although a seasonal regional leisure airport may have a very different employment structure. [8]

**Regional retention strengthens the connection:** approximately 94% of MSP’s direct employees lived within the seven-county study region. That region had more than three million residents and nearly 2.2 million jobs in the report’s contextual baseline. Dividing approximately 54,500 supported jobs by approximately 2.2 million gives a contextual employment benchmark of roughly 2.5%, rather than a causal employment increase. [8]

### Tourism depends on visitors, not terminal counts

**Visitors create an additional demand channel:** MSP estimated approximately 5.2 million visitors arriving and departing through the airport in 2016. Their direct spending supported approximately 30,900 jobs, US$0.8 billion wages, US$1.4 billion GDP and US$2.5 billion output. The study excluded indirect and induced visitor-spending effects to reduce overlap with effects already assigned to aviation. [8]

**Philadelphia shows the same mechanism:** its tourism category contributed an estimated US$3.477 billion output, 32,660 jobs and US$1.184 billion earnings within the 11-county region. These are part of, rather than additional to, the airport’s headline regional totals. [11]

**Inverness demonstrates why passenger profiles matter:** its 2018 report analyzes approximately 861,000 one-way trips on 11 scheduled routes in 2017. These comprised 572,000 leisure and 289,000 business trips. Residence counts were 118,000 overseas, 355,000 elsewhere in the UK and 388,000 in the catchment or adjacent areas. Passenger direction was reported as 55% inbound and 45% outbound. [19]

**Trip purpose and residence are complementary:** leisure passengers can be local residents departing for holidays or visitors bringing expenditure into the region. Business passengers can support local firms’ market access without making substantial destination purchases. A passenger total alone distinguishes neither mechanism.

**A tourism model makes the inputs explicit:** the 2022 Crete airport-development study calculates catalytic tourism expenditure using international arrivals, average destination spending and the share arriving by air:

$$CE=(ITA\times AS)\times ITAR.$$

Here \(ITA\) is international tourist arrivals, \(AS\) average destination expenditure and \(ITAR\) the air-traveler share. This estimates air-associated expenditure; establishing additional regional value added requires further attention to substitution, imported inputs and spending that would have occurred anyway. [21]

**The regional implication is conditional:** an airport bringing additional visitors into locally owned accommodation, restaurants and attractions can retain substantial spending. One carrying predominantly outbound residents may generate less inbound expenditure, while still providing valuable travel opportunities and business access. Neither passenger mix should be judged solely by tourism spending.

### Accessibility, investment and wider business effects

**Accessibility has independent socioeconomic value:** Philadelphia’s report estimates that regional residents save 200 million annual travel miles relative to using alternative airports outside the region. Exeter’s Amsterdam connection illustrates a different access gain: reaching a worldwide network through one regional departure. These are travel-cost and opportunity benefits, distinct from employment multipliers. [11][14]

**Business mechanisms extend beyond spending:** air service can facilitate face-to-face communication, coordination, trade, technology diffusion and access to clients. Lower transaction costs may support specialization and firm location. Blonigen and Cristea identify these as mechanisms underlying regional productivity effects, while their empirical study estimates an aggregate regional response rather than a separate value for each pathway. [22]

**Investment creates a separate time profile:** MSP’s 2016 capital program totaled US$121.6 million and was associated with approximately 1,500 supported jobs. Philadelphia’s capital-investment impact category records US$406 million output and 2,680 jobs. Construction activity should not be presented as permanent annual airport employment, and capital expenditure remains a resource cost even when it supports jobs during delivery. [8][11]

**Tax receipts are another distinct outcome:** MSP estimated approximately US$973 million annual taxes associated with direct employment and passenger activity in 2016, comprising approximately US$427 million federal and US$546 million state and local revenue. Its tax appendix excludes indirect and induced multiplier effects from that calculation. Philadelphia estimated US$78 million city and US$295 million state tax revenue, including US$255 million for Pennsylvania. [8][11]

### Passenger–impact relationships should remain marginal

**Incremental estimates support measurability:** ACI–North America’s March 2025 study estimates that each additional 1,000 enplanements is associated, on average, with 1.2 on-airport jobs and US$387,200 on-airport output, plus 4.6 visitor jobs and US$290,500 visitor spending. Its combined graphic reports 5.8 jobs and US$677,700 output per additional 1,000 enplanements. The components add consistently, but they describe a national modeled incremental average. [23]

**Another rule uses different units:** Klophaus’s 2008 analysis of 17 German international airports proposes approximately 500 additional on-site jobs per additional one million annual passengers. This is not a total-employment estimate for an airport handling one million passengers, and halving it would not establish total jobs at a 500,000-passenger airport. [24]

**Small-airport income evidence is positive but variable:** Button, Doh and Yuan’s 2010 study of 66 Virginia airports reports up to a 4% increase in surrounding-area per-capita income when passenger numbers double. The strength of the relationship changes with model specification. It supports possible effects beyond major hubs while reinforcing that passenger totals are not a universal income multiplier. [25]

**Aggregate scale supplies context only:** SEO Amsterdam Economics estimates European airports’ 2019 gross direct, indirect, induced and tourism contribution at approximately €505 billion GDP and 8.1 million jobs—around 2.8% of the European Economic Area economy and 3.6% of its workforce. These figures demonstrate system-wide materiality, but dividing them across airports or passengers would erase the differences central to this assessment. [26]

**Section judgment:** measurable benefits operate through several credible channels, but local retention, passenger purpose, service usefulness and the distinction between gross output and value added determine their practical importance.

## 4. Environmental costs, public finance and distribution

### Financial vulnerability does not settle social value

**The fiscal qualification is substantial:** a European Commission evaluation published in 2026 reports that approximately 54% of sampled regional airports were cost-covering in 2024. Its regional-airport category extends to three million annual passengers, and airports above 700,000 passengers were more likely, on average, to cover costs. A 500,000-passenger airport sits within the financially vulnerable part of that broader category. [27]

**Earlier evidence points the same way:** ACI EUROPE’s 2023 report cites a 2019 assessment placing regional-airport viability around 700,000–1,000,000 annual passengers. Oxera’s 2024 report states that its 2019 study expected five of six sampled airports below one million passengers to remain unprofitable by 2024. The latter is a forecast made in 2019, not an observed failure rate for every small airport. [28][29]

**Efficiency provides an important counterexample:** a 2024 review in *Systems* cites Adler and colleagues’ finding that operationally efficient small airports could potentially cover annual operating costs with as few as 166,000 passengers. That result concerns operating-cost coverage under efficient conditions, rather than full recovery of every capital and social cost. It nevertheless argues against treating any passenger-volume break-even band as a universal rule. [30]

**Fixed costs explain the vulnerability:** runway maintenance, safety, security and other operational requirements must be supported even when flights are infrequent or seasonal. Smaller airports also tend to have fewer commercial-revenue opportunities and may negotiate discounted airline charges to secure service. As passenger volumes rise, the same fixed infrastructure can serve more users; below that point, maintaining useful connectivity can require public support. [31]

**A conditional operating envelope illustrates scale:** applying the smaller-airport figures summarized by Hiney, Efthymiou and Morgenroth—€15–€17 cost per passenger and approximately €4 non-aeronautical revenue per passenger—to exactly 500,000 passengers gives estimated annual costs of €7.5–€8.5 million and non-aeronautical revenue of €2 million. The arithmetic is \(500,000×€15–€17\) and \(500,000×€4\). The remaining €5.5–€6.5 million would need aeronautical or other revenue; it is not an estimated deficit. [31]

**Profitability and welfare are separate:** an unprofitable airport may still provide valuable access to a remote community, while a profitable airport may impose substantial uncompensated harm. The relevant funding question is whether the additional benefits justify the additional resource and public-finance costs, compared with feasible alternatives.

### Waterloo shows the scale of public exposure

**Capital funding is not free revenue:** Waterloo’s 2017 plan assumes that 50% of capital costs at each development trigger would come from non-property-tax sources, including government subsidies or development charges. For the C$74,370,500 Phase 2 scenario, each half is a calculated C$37,185,250. Shifting funding away from property tax changes who pays; it does not eliminate the public or private resource cost. [16]

**Passenger growth can improve operator finances:** the same plan estimates approximately C$1 million additional annual gross airport income from one extra daily domestic Boeing 737-700 service with up to 149 seats. Its listed components—C$130,000 parking and concessions, C$100,000 fuel surcharges, C$161,375 landing fees and approximately C$650,000 departing-passenger fees—sum to approximately C$1.041 million, consistent with the rounded headline. This is an airport-revenue scenario, not regional GDP. [16]

**The comparison changes the appraisal:** recurring airport income can reduce municipal levy pressure, but a capital project’s justification also depends on operating costs, asset life, financing and demand risk. Comparing one year of regional GVA directly with an upfront capital bill would mix unlike quantities.

### Public audit evidence shows weak and strong cases together

**Grand Est provides direct counterevidence:** France’s regional public auditor assessed Strasbourg-Entzheim, Vatry and Metz-Nancy Lorraine over 2016–2023. It found a significant economic contribution at Strasbourg but very limited impacts at the other two, alongside generally modest regional wealth and employment creation.

| Airport and location | Descriptive regional-impact finding | Financial finding, 2016–2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Strasbourg-Entzheim, Grand Est, France | Significant economic contribution | Operating subsidies required |
| Vatry, Grand Est, France | Very limited contribution | Operating subsidies required |
| Metz-Nancy Lorraine, Grand Est, France | Very limited contribution | Operating subsidies required |

These findings come from the Grand Est audit published March 27, 2025 [3].

**The public commitment was material:** combined investment subsidies and operating aid totaled €129.2 million over 2016–2023. Across eight calendar years, that is a calculated average of €16.15 million annually for the three airports together; it does not imply equal funding allocations. [3]

**The audit’s language is descriptive:** “significant” here is an evaluation judgment, not a reported statistical test. The assessment used Insee business data, a survey of 400 Alsatian businesses and 77 semi-structured interviews, while explicitly distinguishing its evaluation from causal identification. Its importance is the observed heterogeneity: public airport support can coexist with either meaningful or modest regional activity.

**Underinvestment can also carry costs:** ACI–NA’s 2025 report identifies US$30.2 billion U.S. airport infrastructure needs against US$17.3 billion funding in 2024, a US$12.9 billion gap. Its scenario of a continuing shortfall projects US$41.6 billion accumulated direct airline and traveler costs over ten years, plus US$6.1 billion less annual output and 53,500 fewer jobs in 2034. These national conditional projections show why avoiding investment is not automatically costless; they are not local forecasts for a 500,000-passenger airport. [23]

### Noise and property effects are measurable regional losses

**Housing studies quantify localized harm:** a 2021 *Land Economics* study combining repeat-sales data with aircraft noise around MSP over 1990–2014 estimates approximately US$25,000 lower sale value for noise-affected properties ineligible for soundproofing assistance. Eligible homes experienced negligible post-soundproofing effects, with estimated abatement returns as high as 40% in Minneapolis. [32]

**Memphis confirms unequal exposure:** a 2019 study of 9,606 properties sold during 2011–2016 in Memphis, Tennessee, estimates a potential average external cost of US$4,795 per decibel of aircraft noise per household. Effects differ across city areas. This is property-market evidence, not an annual cash charge or a transferable price for every airport community. [33]

**Newquay offers a small-airport exposure scenario:** its 2015–2030 masterplan identifies one household above the 57 dB LAeq 16-hour contour in its baseline and projects 16 under modeled summer 2030 operations. It identifies no households at or above the 63 dB contour in either case. These are historical baseline and scenario counts, not a statement that only one household is affected in 2026. [15]

**Passenger volume does not determine exposure:** runway orientation, aircraft type, operating hours, flight paths and settlement density can produce very different noise burdens at airports with equal annual passenger counts. SEO’s European assessment did not find a clear traffic-volume–noise-exposure relationship in the available 2017 data. [26]

**Broader indicators establish social relevance:** the European Aviation Environmental Report 2025 records 3.4 million people exposed to Lden 55 dB aircraft noise at 98 major airports in 2023. Separate Environmental Noise Directive estimates for 2022 identify 649,000 people experiencing high annoyance and 127,000 significant sleep disturbance. These demonstrate measurable health and wellbeing channels without assigning major-airport exposure rates to the target airport. [34]

### Local air quality and climate require separate accounting

**Local pollution is not carbon alone:** aircraft-engine emissions can affect nearby residential exposure to nitrogen dioxide and ultrafine particles. These local air-quality effects depend on operations, meteorology and population location, while flight-related greenhouse gases impose broader climate costs. Combining them into one unspecified “emissions” indicator would obscure different mechanisms. [34]

**Newquay illustrates boundary differences:** its masterplan reports 15,990 tonnes CO₂-equivalent aviation emissions in 2008, based on 14,209 aviation movements, equivalent to 0.3% of Cornwall’s recorded greenhouse-gas emissions. Its 2024/25 report instead measures airport-controlled Scope 1 and 2 emissions, with intensity falling from 1.73 kg CO₂e per passenger in 2019/20 to 0.96 kg in FY2024/25. [15][12]

**Those reductions measure different things:** the calculated per-passenger intensity decline is approximately 44.5%, using \(1-0.96÷1.73\). The report’s separate 65% market-based reduction since 2019/20 and 57.9% reduction since FY2023/24 concern its controlled carbon footprint, not all emissions from passenger flights.

**European totals are scope-specific:** SEO estimates 211 million tonnes CO₂ and 422 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent non-CO₂ effects for flights departing European origins in 2019. The environmental report records 133 million tonnes CO₂ from EU27+EFTA departures in 2023, 10% below its corresponding 2019 baseline. Different geography, years and emissions concepts mean these totals should not be treated as one continuous local series. [26][34]

### Congestion, land use and distribution affect the final balance

**Annual throughput is a weak congestion proxy:** 500,000 movements over a 365-day year averages approximately 1,370 passenger movements daily, calculated as \(500,000÷365\). That is not a vehicle-trip estimate. Congestion depends on peak timing, car occupancy, drop-offs, taxis, public transport, staff travel and other development using the same roads.

**Newquay’s plans recognize separate drivers:** its surface-access strategy has one action plan for up to 400,000 passengers and 1,500 Aerohub employees, and another for up to 600,000 passengers and 5,000 employees. Measures include bus improvements, possible park-and-ride, an internal road, cycling facilities, parking and traffic monitoring. These are planning responses to passenger and employment scenarios, not measured congestion losses caused by reaching 500,000 passengers. [15]

**Land effects can run both ways:** accessibility can raise commercial development potential, while noise and flight-path restrictions can reduce residential amenity or constrain alternative land uses. Property-price discounts may already capitalize part of the noise burden. Adding the full discount to a separate valuation of identical lifetime noise damage would risk counting the same loss twice.

| Affected group | Potential benefit | Potential burden | What determines the balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workers and local suppliers | Jobs, wages and contracts | Seasonality and dependence on carriers | Job quality and local retention |
| Residents and regional firms | Better access and market connections | Fees, travel-related costs and disruption | Useful routes and realistic alternatives |
| Nearby households | Some accessibility and employment gains | Noise, pollution and property effects | Exposure and mitigation |
| Taxpayers and public authorities | Tax receipts and regional access | Operating aid, capital funding and opportunity cost | Additional benefits relative to public cost |

**Section judgment:** positive gross activity can coexist with financial losses and concentrated harms; a defensible regional judgment must distinguish economic contribution, airport profitability and net social value.

## 5. Causal attribution, counterevidence and transferability

### Stronger studies support conditional regional effects

**The causal evidence is positive but heterogeneous:** policy reforms, route shocks and longitudinal regional studies provide stronger attribution than a single-year multiplier account. Their estimates concern changes in connectivity or airport activity relative to a comparison—not the total effect of an airport simply recording 500,000 passengers.

| Study and geography | Data period | Identification or comparison | Main reported result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volkhausen, EU-15 | 1980–2016 | Aviation liberalization; difference-in-differences and IV | Activity +2–6%; employment about +2% | Positive regional effects |
| Blonigen–Cristea, U.S. MSAs | 1969–1991 | 1978 deregulation quasi-experiment | Employment growth +0.31 percentage points | Positive growth response |
| Fu and colleagues, New Zealand | 2001–2016 | Airport-region panels; FE, GMM and CCE | GDP per capita +0.38% per +1% ASK | Positive capacity relationship |
| SEO, European regions | 2004–2019 | Longitudinal spatial econometrics | GDP +0.5% per +10% direct flights | Connectivity matters |
| Cattaneo and colleagues, northern Italy | 2005–2010 | Malpensa de-hubbing natural experiment | Full-sample employment null; sectoral losses | Strong heterogeneity |
| Breidenbach, Germany | Reported in Volkhausen, 2022 | Regional-airport difference-in-differences | No detected regional-growth effect | Counterevidence to universality |

The findings are supported by the EU-15 study [35], U.S. deregulation paper [22], New Zealand panel study [36], SEO report [26] and Malpensa study [37].

**The counterfactual is essential:** prosperous regions attract air service, while air service may help regions become more prosperous. Comparing places with and without airports without addressing that feedback can mistake pre-existing demand and development for airport-generated growth.

**Difference-in-differences addresses part of this problem:** it compares the change in an exposed area with the change in an appropriate comparison area. Its credibility depends on the groups following comparable underlying trends absent the relevant aviation change. Fixed effects and other controls can address persistent differences, but not every coincident local shock.

### European liberalization: positive effects with a meaningful null comparison

**Volkhausen provides relevant regional evidence:** the 2022 study covers EU-15 NUTS-3 regions over 1980–2016 and classifies airports with up to three million annual passengers as regional. It uses a 25-kilometer centroid buffer to capture proximity and spillovers, with 11,781 regional-airport-area observations and 24,732 no-airport observations in its descriptive comparison. [35]

**The reform supplies identifying variation:** the study exploits the 1997 Single European Aviation Market reform, comparing affected and unaffected areas. It reports approximately 2–6% higher local economic activity, 1–3% higher population and around 2% higher employment. Its additional instrumental-variable estimate indicates a 2–3% GDP effect per additional one million passengers. [2]

**The marginal interpretation must be preserved:** half of an estimated per-million marginal relationship is not automatically the effect of operating a 500,000-passenger airport instead of having no airport. The comparison involves existing regional economies and aviation-market changes, not a controlled move from zero infrastructure to the target annual volume.

**German counterevidence deserves equal attention:** Volkhausen reports that Breidenbach’s Germany-only difference-in-differences study found no evidence that regional-airport expansion generated regional growth. Volkhausen suggests limited statistical power as a possible explanation. That interpretation does not convert a null result into a positive one; equally, a statistically imprecise null is not proof that the true effect is exactly zero. [35]

**The resulting judgment is conditional:** broader European evidence supports regional benefits, but existing transport networks, industry structure and catchment overlap can make an additional airport or expansion less consequential. Stronger surface alternatives or an already well-served region would weaken transfer to a particular small airport.

### U.S. deregulation and New Zealand panels

**The U.S. study estimates growth-rate effects:** Blonigen and Cristea’s 2012 NBER working paper uses the 1978 Airline Deregulation Act as a quasi-natural experiment, with 296 metropolitan statistical areas observed in three years spanning 1969–1991. Moving passenger-traffic growth from the 25th to the 75th percentile raised estimated annual population growth by 0.14 percentage points, income growth by 0.20 points and employment growth by 0.31 points. [22]

**Those effects were substantively meaningful:** the reported changes represented approximately 11.7%, 13.2% and 11% of the corresponding average annual growth rates. They were not percentage changes in a single airport’s employment. Effects also varied with metropolitan size and industrial specialization, with medium-sized metropolitan areas benefiting less and wholesale and retail showing significant employment responses. [22]

**Historical confounders remain relevant:** the authors discuss the 1978–1979 oil-price shocks and contemporaneous trucking and railway deregulation. Their design and checks seek to distinguish these influences from aviation changes. The working paper provides stronger attribution than a cross-sectional correlation, but the historical U.S. institutional setting differs from contemporary European or island regional airports.

**New Zealand measures service capacity:** Fu and colleagues analyze 22 airports and their regions over 2001–2016. Their principal activity measure is available seat-kilometers, or ASK: the number of seats offered multiplied by flight distance. A 1% ASK increase is estimated to raise regional GDP per capita by 0.38%, other things equal. ASK can change through aircraft size or route length without an equivalent percentage change in passengers. [36]

**Multiple estimators address feedback:** fixed effects, system generalized method of moments and dynamic common correlated effects produce a significant positive relationship while addressing different aspects of regional heterogeneity, common shocks and growth feedback. New Zealand’s isolation, limited surface alternatives and relatively distinct airport-region structure make its accessibility mechanism particularly relevant to peripheral regions, but less directly transferable to dense multi-airport markets. [36]

**SEO provides complementary European evidence:** its 2004–2019 longitudinal analysis of NUTS-3 regions, using a 150-kilometer airport-region radius, estimates 0.5% higher GDP and 1.6% higher employment for 10% more direct flights. This reinforces the importance of connectivity, while its larger geographic boundary captures effects beyond a narrowly defined airport municipality. [26]

### Malpensa: route quality and industrial exposure change the outcome

**A major service shock reveals heterogeneity:** on March 31, 2008, Alitalia withdrew approximately 180 daily flights and 14 intercontinental routes from Milan Malpensa, Italy, representing around 70% of its airport operations. Total airport passengers fell from approximately 23.8 million in 2007 to 17.5 million in 2009. This is a major-hub connectivity experiment, not a near-threshold passenger case. [37]

**The overall employment estimate was null:** Cattaneo, Percoco and Malighetti’s 2018 study follows Travel-to-Work Areas in Lombardy, Piedmont, Liguria and Emilia-Romagna during 2005–2010. The full-sample employment effect was not statistically different from zero. Urban and heavy-industry areas likewise showed no detected effect. [37]

**Export-oriented areas experienced substantial losses:** the spatial model estimates a 5.9% direct employment contraction and a 14.6% neighboring-area contraction. “Indirect” in this spatial analysis means effects transmitted through neighboring areas; it is not the supplier category in Prestwick’s input-output account. The two percentages should not be added into a single regional employment loss. [37]

**Passenger recovery did not restore identical connectivity:** easyJet traffic almost doubled between 2008 and 2009, contributing to a partial volume recovery. The authors emphasize that low-cost passengers and intercontinental business passengers differ in spending and economic relationships. That does not imply low-cost service is intrinsically low-value; it shows why replacement passenger volume may fail to replace access to the markets important to export-oriented firms. [37]

**This is not simply negative-airport evidence:** employment losses after reduced connectivity support the proposition that useful air service can matter. The simultaneous full-sample null challenges claims that all nearby sectors benefit equally. Both findings are relevant to the target hypothesis.

### João Paulo II: statistical significance without causal identification

**The published association is measurable:** Patrício, Silva and Brito’s 2026 study compares handled passengers with direct employees reported by responding businesses at João Paulo II Airport on São Miguel. Its six observations span liberalization, pre-pandemic growth, the pandemic and recovery.

| Year | Handled passengers, millions | Responding-company direct employees |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2010 | 0.930 | 143 |
| 2015 | 1.266 | 174 |
| 2019 | 2.027 | 237 |
| 2020 | 0.701 | 213 |
| 2023 | 2.471 | 253 |
| 2024 | 2.733 | 267 |

The passenger series comes from Azorean regional statistics, and employment from company questionnaires. [9]

**The six-observation regression is positive:** with passengers \(x\) measured in millions, the study reports the following direct-employment relationship:

$$y=46.63x+135.78.$$

It reports \(R^2=0.67\), \(p=0.046\) and a residual standard error of 30.77 employees. Thus the relationship clears the study’s 0.05 reference threshold, but estimated employment varies considerably around the fitted line. [9]

**A calculated interval shows the uncertainty:** using the six published passenger values gives \(\sum(x-\bar{x})^2=3.546852\). Under classical independent-error ordinary-least-squares assumptions, the estimated 95% confidence interval for the slope is approximately 1.3–92.0 employees per million handled passengers:

$$95\%\ \mathrm{CI}\approx46.63\pm2.776\left(30.77/\sqrt{3.546852}\right)=[1.3,92.0]\ \text{employees per million handled passengers}.$$

This calculation uses the reported residual error, four residual degrees of freedom and the corresponding Student-\(t\) critical value. It is an interval for the respondent-employment association, not a regional net-impact interval.

**Pandemic treatment changes the estimate:** excluding 2020, the results section reports \(y=68.81x+85.06\), \(R^2=0.979\), \(p=0.0013\) and residual error of 8.89 employees. A tighter fit after removing a disruptive year does not establish that the remaining relationship is causal or stable through future crises. [9]

**The raw observations explain the sensitivity:** passengers fell approximately 65.4% from 2019 to 2020, calculated from 2.027 million to 0.701 million, while responding-company employment fell approximately 10.1%, from 237 to 213. Fixed staffing requirements and temporary employment retention are plausible competing explanations for that divergence; the regression does not isolate them.

**The sample defines the outcome:** 19 of 58 businesses and entities responded, approximately 32.8%. Sixteen respondents considered the airport essential to their business, and the study reports that 75% would cease or reduce activity if it closed. These responses support business dependence, but an expressed closure counterfactual is different from observed closure effects. The regression intercept likewise does not prove that the fitted number of jobs would remain if the airport ceased operating. [9]

### Leakage, displacement and selection can change attribution

**Leakage reduces retained local effects:** imported equipment, outside suppliers, nonlocal workers and profits accruing elsewhere all reduce the spending retained inside a chosen catchment. The Crete input-output study explicitly treats imported intermediate inputs as leakage, while ACI–NA defines multiplier recirculation as ending when expenditure leaves the modeled economy. [21][23]

**Displacement changes the relevant comparison:** a passenger gained from a neighboring airport may create little additional activity across the wider region. Local gains can still occur, but they should not automatically be described as new regional demand. Airline mergers and hub reconfiguration similarly redistribute routes and employment across airports. [38]

**Airport choice depends on useful service:** Morimoto’s 2019 study of travel from Kitakyushu city, Japan, estimates that 79.3% of passengers choose Kitakyushu Airport when it directly serves their destination, compared with 0.9% when it does not. This is a modeled airport-choice result rather than a regional growth estimate, but it gives a concrete mechanism: proximity alone is a weak advantage if the needed route is absent. [39]

**Catchment allocation can change conclusions:** a 2023 São Paulo study compares straight-line distance, travel time and airport-attractiveness weighting for municipalities within 200 kilometers. The methods allocate the same regional aviation activity differently across competing airports. This demonstrates why a regional multiplier by itself does not identify each airport’s additional contribution. [40]

**Survey coverage also affects estimates:** SEO’s autumn 2023 airport survey obtained 74 responses, a 13% response rate, including nine small-airport responses. Victoria’s tenant survey obtained 16 responses from 57 tenants. By comparison, MSP’s employer survey obtained 138 responses from 197 firms, covering approximately 90% of employment. Such differences affect confidence in the underlying activity accounts independently of whether the multiplier calculations are correct. [26][10][8]

### Post-COVID comparisons require service and cost context

**Newquay shows the interruption clearly:** its passenger series collapsed during the pandemic and recovered unevenly. The financial-year observation used earlier is kept separate from the following calendar-year series.

| Calendar year | Reported Newquay passengers | Approximate share of reported 2019 baseline |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2019 | 460,000 | 100% |
| 2020 | 67,877 | 14.8% |
| 2021 | 105,554 | 22.9% |
| 2022 | 244,675 | 53.2% |
| 2024 | 416,000 terminal passengers | 90.4% |
| 2025 | 394,000 terminal passengers | 85.7% |

The 2019–2022 figures are CAA data reported by the BBC [41]; the later terminal-passenger figures are from the CAA tables [7]. Baseline percentages are calculations using the rounded 460,000 figure.

**Nearby airports followed different paths:** Prestwick recorded 639,000 terminal passengers in 2019, 535,000 in 2024 and 621,000 in 2025. Exeter increased from 450,000 in CY2024 to 572,000 in CY2025, a calculated 27.1% increase in rounded calendar-year counts. This differs from the operator’s 32% increase for a year ending March 31 because the reporting periods differ. [42][7]

**Passenger recovery is not economic restoration:** Hiney, Efthymiou and Morgenroth report that European airports below ten million annual passengers reached 83–86% of 2019 traffic in 2022. Low-cost carriers’ regional-airport seat share increased from 52% in 2019 to 60% in 2022. Changed airline mix, service frequency, energy costs, staffing and subsidy arrangements mean that the same passenger count can support a different financial and regional outcome. [31]

**New York illustrates the joint shock:** its 131-airport system’s modeled activity fell from US$79.9 billion in 2019 to US$35.6 billion in 2020, retaining approximately 44.6% using those headline totals. Employment fell from 454,500 to 196,200, approximately 43.2% of the baseline, while enplanements fell to 15.2 million, roughly 73% lower. Much of the reduced activity came through lost visitor spending. [43]

**Public support affected measured recovery:** New York airports received nearly US$411.2 million CARES Act relief in 2020; the study associates it with US$814.7 million total activity and almost 5,000 sustained jobs. Thus airport activity during a demand collapse can reflect both connectivity and fiscal intervention. A pandemic before-and-after comparison is not a clean airport experiment because restrictions and the wider economic shock affected tourism and employment simultaneously. [43]

**Financial recovery also varies institutionally:** a 2025 study of 12 Polish airports over 2016–2022 distinguishes relatively resilient hubs from structurally unprofitable smaller regional operators, despite substantial subsidies. It warns that including subsidies in operating revenue can improve standard financial ratios without resolving underlying dependence. Those operator-finance findings should not be mistaken for measurements of regional GDP or social welfare. [44]

**Section judgment:** causal studies support benefits from useful connectivity, while null results, service substitution and pandemic experience reject the idea that passenger volume alone identifies the regional effect.

## 6. Overall verdict and implications for regional decisions

### The evidence supports “can,” not “automatically will”

**The original hypothesis is supported conditionally:** the combined evidence is consistent with a 500,000-passenger airport generating economically meaningful and measurable regional effects. Near-scale airports sustain hundreds of jobs and substantial production, while stronger regional studies identify positive responses to air-service improvements.

**The strongest support is complementary:** local accounts establish that activity exists and can be measured; causal studies establish that changes in air service can affect wider outcomes. Neither evidence category should replace the other. An accounting model offers detailed channels but weak additionality; a regional quasi-experiment offers stronger attribution but less certainty about one airport’s current suppliers, residents and operating model.

| Proposition | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| An airport at this scale can support measurable regional activity | Support | Employment, income, production and visitor channels are documented |
| Its effects can be substantively important locally | Conditional support | Magnitude depends on regional size, specialization and retained spending |
| Exactly 500,000 passengers is a sufficient development threshold | Reject | Connectivity, operating models and causal results vary |
| A gross contribution establishes positive net social benefit | Reject | Public costs, externalities and alternatives require separate treatment |
| Airport profitability determines regional social value | Reject | Commercial and social returns measure different outcomes |

**The observed magnitude is informative:** the three nearby UK contribution statements span £26.6 million to approximately £100 million annual GVA, with Exeter’s £54 million between them. That is a range of named study contributions in pounds, not a confidence interval or forecast for an otherwise unspecified 500,000-passenger airport. Prestwick’s estimate covers mixed core aviation activity; Newquay’s statement concerns a broader regional contribution.

**The local denominator determines materiality:** the same employment contribution can represent a major share of a small specialist sector or a modest fraction of a metropolitan economy. Prestwick’s sectoral benchmark and Sauk County’s per-resident calculations demonstrate workable comparisons. They also show why an impressive absolute total does not answer the regional-significance question by itself.

### What determines the exact-threshold conclusion

**The decisive conditions are identifiable:** for an airport handling exactly 500,000 defined total annual passenger movements, statistical significance turns on the estimated airport-attributable change and its uncertainty interval; substantive significance turns on that change relative to the same catchment’s population, employment and production. The net conclusion turns on retained benefits relative to operating and capital resource costs, public-finance opportunity costs, displacement and environmental harm. Congestion specifically turns on airport-attributable vehicle flows and travel-time changes, not the annual passenger total.

**Support is strongest in distinctive catchments:** peripheral or island regions with limited alternatives, useful year-round connections, meaningful inbound visitor demand, internationally engaged firms and locally retained wages and procurement have credible mechanisms for substantial benefits. An efficient operating model and effective mitigation strengthen the case further. European and New Zealand evidence particularly supports the importance of accessibility and regional context. [27][36]

**Support weakens with duplication and dependence:** overlapping catchments, replacement rather than additional demand, poor route usefulness, highly seasonal service and persistent subsidy requirements can reduce the retained regional return. Concentrated noise or pollution burdens can further weaken the distributional judgment, even where aggregate business activity is positive.

**The funding implication is proportionate:** passenger throughput can establish the scale of an airport, but it is not a stand-alone investment justification. A demonstrated local economic footprint supports taking the airport seriously as regional infrastructure. A claim that a particular expansion or subsidy improves welfare requires the additional comparison with feasible alternatives and the incidence of benefits and costs.

**The evidence has distinct measurement bases:** dated evidence is considered through September 13, 2026, with undated releases retaining their own reporting labels. Monetary values remain in original currencies and study years; output, GVA, wages, jobs and FTEs remain separate. Multiplier totals are gross modeled contributions. Balanced-flow passenger equivalents, per-resident ratios, financial illustrations and the João Paulo slope interval are calculations under stated assumptions; that study’s alternative equation follows its results section. Rounded source totals are not treated as precision forecasts, and the comparisons are not pooled into one exact-threshold net-impact estimate.

**The final judgment is qualified support:** 500,000 annual passengers is fully compatible with significant regional socioeconomic impacts. What makes those impacts significant is the connectivity and economic activity the airport enables—and what the surrounding region retains after costs—not the passenger milestone itself.
