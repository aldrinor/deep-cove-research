# Chub Mackerel (Scomber japonicus) in the Pacific Rim, 2000–2026: Prices, Body Size, Ocean Conditions and Fishery Economics

The core finding: since 2018, chub mackerel prices in the Northwest Pacific have been driven less by how much fish is landed than by how big the fish are and by how much Norwegian Atlantic mackerel is available. Density-dependent stunting after the huge 2013 year class, the 2017–2025 Kuroshio Large Meander and a northward-shifted Kuroshio Extension together broke the old link between volume and value. In Japan and Korea, large fatty fish now sell at record prices while most of the catch is small fish that goes to feed or to Africa.

## TL;DR
- **Prices are at record highs, but this reflects scarcity of *quality*, not just of volume.** Toyosu's average wholesale price for fresh saba hit ¥864/kg in December 2025 (¥651 a year earlier). Japan's national average landing price jumped to ¥199/kg in May 2026 (+116% year on year) even though 80–90% of January–May 2026 landings weighed under 200 g. Norwegian mackerel (<600 g) export prices rose from NOK 20/kg in 2023 to NOK 47.16/kg in October 2025 (versus NOK 21.84 a year earlier, per SeafoodSource citing NSC data), and NSC says they "passed NOK 50 per kg" during 2025. In Korea, imported Norwegian fish reached a record $5.1/kg in March 2026.
- **Body size is now the main economic variable, and it is governed by density and ocean conditions.** Pacific-stock asymptotic length fell from 44.6 to 37.1 cm after the 2013 year class. The NPFC 2025 assessment puts spawning stock biomass (SSB) at 16% of its FY2018 peak, with fishing mortality equivalent to 16% SPR. In Korea, demand for small mackerel is highly elastic (−3.444) while demand for large mackerel is inelastic (−0.875). Each extra tonne of small fish therefore depresses revenue, while scarcity of large fish raises it.
- **Policy is now catching up.** Japan cut its Pacific saba TAC from 353,000 t (FY2024) to 139,000 t (FY2025, a 61% cut approved by the Fisheries Policy Council per Minato Shimbun) and then to 112,000 t (FY2026, −19%, also approved by the Council). NPFC capped high-seas catch at 66,740 t. Korea set a merged "mackerels" TAC of 138,224 t per season for 2026–2029. The most valuable next step is to build environment–size–price models that put a value on growth lost to over-harvesting juveniles.

## Scope and defaults applied
- **Species:** *Scomber japonicus*, kept distinct from *S. australasicus* (blue/spotted mackerel, ゴマサバ, 망치고등어) and from imported *S. scombrus* (Atlantic mackerel, mainly Norwegian).
  - Japanese TAC and most port statistics combine masaba and gomasaba as "さば類".
  - NPFC reports both species together as "chub mackerel". Chinese reporting puts blue mackerel at roughly 6% of China's 2024 high-seas mackerel catch.\[1\]
  - From 2026/27, Korea manages "고등어류" (chub mackerel plus blue mackerel) under one TAC.\[2\]
- **Geography:** Japan, South Korea, China, Taiwan, the Russian Far East, Peru/Chile, and the US/Mexico.
- **Period:** 2000–2026, focusing on 2013–2026. Currency conversions use approximate 2025 rates: ¥150/USD, ₩1,400/USD, NOK 11/USD.
- **Thin coverage:** Taiwan (Nanfang'ao), Chile and Russian price data were weakly covered in accessible sources. They are discussed qualitatively and flagged as data gaps.

## Key Findings
1. **The price–quantity relationship has decoupled from size.** Korea illustrates this best. Cumulative 2025 landings to mid-August were 83,774 t, against 55,997 t in 2024 and a 44,930 t long-run average. Yet retail prices for chilled and salted mackerel rose more than 30%, because medium and large fish became scarce.\[3\] At Busan Cooperative Fish Market, the medium-to-large share fell to 5.6% in January 2025, from 15.6% a year earlier.\[4\]
2. **The Pacific stock collapsed from its post-2013 boom, and the assessment was sharply revised.** FRA biomass estimates for 2021–2023 fell from 3.44–3.85 Mt in the 2023 assessment to 2.04, 1.75 and 1.22 Mt in the 2024 assessment. The revision came from low egg and dip-net indices and from Chinese age data showing more young fish in the catch than assumed. The FY2023 catch of about 130,000 t was the poorest since 2013.\[5\]
3. **There is an east–west divergence.** The Tsushima Warm Current stock is healthy: its SSB was above the MSY level in 2024 per FRA's 2025 assessment, and East China Sea/Sea of Japan landings were 179,000 t in 2025. The Pacific stock is near its limit reference point, with Pacific-side landings of 41,000 t in 2025 against 64,000 t in 2024.
4. **The oceanographic forcing is clear but secondary to stock depletion now.** JAFIC's 2025 review concludes that the poor Pacific masaba catch in 2025 reflected stock decline more than ocean conditions. This came after the Kuroshio Large Meander ended in April 2025 and the northward shift of the Kuroshio Extension resolved in late 2024.
5. **Norwegian supply shocks set the ceiling for Japanese and Korean prices.**
   - Norway's 2025 quota was cut 22%, and its 2026 quota fell a further 51% to 81,375 t.\[6\]
   - Norway's 2025 mackerel exports fell 34% by volume but reached a record value of NOK 8.5 bn.\[7\]
   - Japan, facing a 7% tariff on whole fish and 10% on fillets, is losing raw material to Korea and to duty-free Vietnamese processing.\[8\]

## Part A — Price Dynamics

### A1. Japan

**Wholesale (Toyosu).** Tokyo Metropolitan Central Wholesale Market statistics, as compiled by GD Freak, give the monthly average wholesale price of fresh saba (tax-inclusive, ¥/kg):

| Month | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 | 5-yr avg (2021–25) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 598 | 608 | 811 | 531 |
| Feb | 543 | 653 | 693 | 503 |
| Mar | 582 | 609 | – | 494 |
| Jun | 501 | 445 | – | 433 |
| Sep | 551 | 611 | – | 563 |
| Oct | 571 | 702 | – | 580 |
| Dec | 651 | 864 | – | 650\[9\] |

Three things stand out:
- **Strong seasonality.** In the five-year average, December runs about 50% above June.\[9\] This is the fat-content cycle: autumn–winter "fatty saba" carry a premium.
- **A step up in level.** December 2025 (¥864 ≈ US$5.8/kg) was 33% above December 2024, and January 2026 (¥811) was 53% above the five-year January mean.
- **Frozen imports price above fresh domestic fish.** Imported frozen saba (largely Norwegian) traded at ¥540–972/kg at Toyosu on 14 December 2024. By August–September 2026 the range had risen to ¥864–1,566 and then ¥972–1,620/kg.\[10\]\[11\]

**Ex-vessel (landing ports).**
- **National level.** The Zhoushan Export Aquatic Products Association, citing Japanese data, reports January–May 2026 landings of 106,954 t (+3% year on year). However, 80–90% of these were fish under 200 g. May 2026 landings were 13,826 t (−25%) at a record average of ¥199/kg (+116%).\[6\]
- **Hachinohe.**
  - Saba landings in 2025 were only 1,726 t, worth ¥534 million (¥309.5/kg).\[12\]
  - Purse-seine saba landings at the port were 1,161 t, just 14% of the previous year.\[13\]
  - The first autumn-saba landing on 24 October 2025 sold at ¥4,280–4,800 per 10 kg (¥428–480/kg) because the fish were fatty.\[14\]
- **Size grades at western ports.** JAFIC daily data for October 2026 show the same-day spread directly:
  - Karatsu: 大 (large) masaba at ¥467–817/kg versus 極小 (very small) at ¥225–342/kg, a premium of about 2–2.5×.\[15\]
  - Matsuura: large at ¥503/kg.
  - Sakaiminato: 498 t of masaba at only ¥76–216/kg.\[15\]
  - Ishinomaki: ungraded saba at ¥215/kg.\[15\]
- **Choshi.** A processor's press release claims Choshi's saba landings fell from about 100,000 t in 2021 to about 7,500 t recently.\[16\]\[17\] This is a company PR figure and should be verified against Choshi port statistics.

**Size-grade structure.**
- **Market thresholds.** Japanese practice treats about 500 g as the threshold for the fresh-fish (鮮魚) market. Smaller fish go to salted, dried and canned products, and the smallest go to aquaculture feed or export.\[18\]
- **Feed-grade prices.** Minato Shimbun reports small fish at:
  - ¥130–140/kg delivered to Kyushu/Shikoku fish farms for fish ≤150 g (described as a record high);\[19\]
  - the low ¥90s/kg for 100–300 g fish in an earlier season;
  - ¥160s/kg for 200–300 g fish in mid-2025 amid a "no-supply market".\[19\]
- **The resulting price ladder (approximate):** feed-grade ¥90–165/kg; export frozen fish ≤300 g; processing grades; fresh 400 g+ at ¥450–800+/kg; branded fish far higher.
  - Retail examples: set-net masaba from Miyagi at 550–750 g sells for ¥1,680/kg wholesale; farmed live-bled masaba from Amakusa at ¥2,580/kg.\[20\]\[21\]
  - Fish encyclopedia Zukan-bouz puts the overall market range at about ¥600–8,000/kg, from commodity purse-seine fish to brands such as Seki saba and Matsuwa saba.\[22\]

**Retail.** The Ministry of Internal Affairs (MIC) retail price survey, as aggregated by Toyo Keizai, shows the 79-city average saba price rising from ¥154/100 g in March 2025 to ¥169/100 g in March 2026 (+10%).\[23\]

**Trade.**
- **Exports.** Japan's saba exports grew from 2,300 t in 2000 to about 230,000 t in 2017.\[24\] In 2018 they reached 248,700 t, of which Nigeria took 50,500 t, Egypt 45,700 t and Ghana 25,700 t.\[25\]\[26\] Exported fish are mostly ≤300 g, the same size class that is used for feed domestically.\[27\]
- **Imports.** In 2025 Japan received about 80,000 t (whole-fish equivalent) of Norwegian mackerel; NSC's 2025 annual export release states "Japan took around 40 per cent of the Norwegian mackerel exported in 2025, down from 42 per cent in 2024" (of a total of 207,927 t worth NOK 8.5 bn):
  - direct imports of 27,000 t, at CFR $5.30–5.80/kg;
  - 26,800 t via Vietnamese processing;
  - 14,900 t via China;
  - over 10,000 t via Thailand and Indonesia.
- **Import prices in 2025.**
  - Minato Shimbun reported opening offers of ¥640–650/kg C&F for 400–600 g fish (+50%, a record), with quotes passing ¥800/kg by late September 2025.\[28\]\[29\]
  - Jiji reports that Norwegian landing prices rose from about ¥250/kg in 2021 to about ¥900/kg in 2025.\[30\]
  - In early 2026, more Norwegian herring than mackerel was shipped directly to Japan for the first time, a sign of substitution.\[8\]

### A2. South Korea
- **Volume versus size, 2025.**
  - Total production surged in 2025 (see Key Finding 1), but the medium-to-large share at Busan Cooperative Fish Market, which handles more than 60% of national mackerel auctions, collapsed: 5.6% in January (15.6% a year earlier) and 6.4% in March (18.7%).\[4\]
  - Small "갈고등어" (<200 g) dominated the catch and went largely to export.\[4\]
  - Noryangjin's average price rose from ₩2,900/kg to ₩4,400/kg during March 2025, against ₩1,500–2,800 a year earlier.\[4\]
- **January 2026.** Busan auctions handled 10,880 t at ₩2,495/kg (≈US$1.8/kg), about 60% higher year on year.\[31\]
- **Grades.** Busan sorts fish at 350 g into two groups: 350 g and above (소갈고, 소소고, 소고, 중고) and under 350 g (갈소고, 갈고, 고도리). One Q1 2026 report put only about 8% of auctioned volume at 350 g or above.\[32\] Its 465 t base looks inconsistent with the January figure and needs verification.
- **Historic size premium.** In 2002, a 20 kg box of 갈고 (≤25 cm) sold for about ₩10,000, versus about ₩70,000 for 소고 (≥35 cm), a premium of about 7×.\[33\]
- **Small fish depress unit values in earlier seasons too.**
  - In H1 2015, Busan mackerel landings rose 152% (to 33,180 t), but total auction value across all species rose only 18%, because the fish were small.\[34\]
  - In an early-2010s season, volume rose 93% while unit value fell 23%, from ₩2,694/kg to ₩2,079/kg, because fish under 300 g dominated.\[35\]
- **Imports.**
  - **Volume and price.** Korea imported 40,567 t of frozen mackerel in January–October 2025 (+53%), worth $108.41 m, at an average of $2.67/kg (+18%). Norwegian fish averaged $2.81/kg, Chinese $1.88/kg and British $2.86/kg.\[36\]
  - **Domestic wholesale.** Norwegian 400–600 g fish in 20 kg cartons sold for about ₩165,000, against about ₩90,000 a year earlier (+83%).\[36\]
  - **Monthly unit values.** The Norwegian unit value to Korea rose from $2.71/kg in January 2025 to $5.14/kg in October 2025. KMI reports a record $5.1/kg in March 2026 (versus $2.7 in March 2025).\[37\]\[38\]
  - **Relative scale.** Norwegian imports of 43,093 t in 2024 equalled about one-third of Korean domestic production.\[38\]
  - **Consumer prices.** KMI put the consumer price at ₩12,131/kg in October 2025, 16.8% above the average year.\[38\]
- **Policy response.** The Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries (MOF) has damped frozen-mackerel prices (₩3,981 per fish on 19 August 2025, −3.9% year on year) using tariff-rate quotas (2,000 t in July–August 2025), releases of government stockpiles (1,500 t cumulative in 2025) and discount campaigns.\[3\]

### A3. China
- **Catch.** China's chub mackerel catch (FAO) rose rapidly after 2000, peaked at 592,637 t in 2008, and was 405,541 t in 2024.\[39\]\[40\]
- **High seas.** China's NPFC Convention Area catch (light purse seine plus trawl) was:

| 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 64,446 t | 92,456 t | 108,266 t | 110,856 t | 48,850 t | 72,367 t | 43,229 t (of 44,022 t total; TAC 66,740 t) |

- **Domestic price position.** Domestically, 鲐鱼 is a low-value species ("the bottom" of the seafood market).\[41\] Its domestic prices are poorly documented in official statistics. Online trade listings show frozen light-boat mackerel at about ¥2.1 RMB/jin (≈US$0.58/kg) and fresh fish at RMB 6–10/jin, but these are anecdotal.\[42\]
- **Role in trade.** China's economic role is mainly as a processor and re-exporter of Norwegian fish into Japan (14,900 t in 2025)\[6\] and as a low-price supplier to Korea ($1.88/kg).\[36\] The Zhoushan export association expects Chinese processors to gain share in Japan as Norwegian supply tightens. This is a forecast, not an outcome.

### A4. Russia, Taiwan, the Americas
- **Russia.**
  - Chub mackerel became a significant Russian target from 2015, and the catch reached 87,388 t in 2021 as the stock expanded toward the Kurils.\[43\]
  - By 23 November of the 2025 season, the Russian catch was only 3,542 t of mackerel (alongside 52,283 t of sardine). Part of that fleet fished in Japanese waters under intergovernmental quotas.\[44\]
  - Russian mackerel has been offered to Asian buyers at about $0.89/kg, against about $2.50/kg for Norwegian.\[45\] This trade-guide figure is undated and indicative only, but it shows a large quality discount.
- **Taiwan.** There is a Nanfang'ao/Suao fishery, and Taiwan appeared in 2025 as a new minor supplier to Korea (258 t).\[46\] No reliable recent price series was accessible.
- **Peru (caballa, *S. j. peruanus*).**
  - Since 2002, caballa has been reserved by law for direct human consumption (canning, freezing), with a 30% juvenile tolerance.\[47\]
  - Large-scale fleet limits were 25,991 t in 2024 (94% taken) and 30,104 t in 2025. A 2026 artisanal limit of 41,333 t covers vessels up to 32.6 m³ for September–December. A trade-press report also cites an 84,000 t caballa limit alongside 175,000 t for jack mackerel.\[48\]\[49\]\[50\]\[51\]
  - Uptake is weak: by November 2025 the artisanal fleet had used only 27.5% (<10 m³) and 10.8% (10–20 m³) of its quotas.\[52\] This suggests availability or economics constrain the catch, not the quota.
- **United States.**
  - Pacific mackerel is a low-value commodity. 2024 California landings were 3,056,272 lb worth $800,895, about $0.26/lb (≈$0.58/kg). NOAA gives a near-identical 3.3 million lb at $800,000.\[53\]\[54\]
  - In 2023–24, about 778 t (worth ≈$406,702, or $0.52/kg) was taken against a 7,871 t harvest guideline.\[55\]
  - Real ex-vessel prices were $127–173/t in 1993–2002, so nominal prices have roughly tripled but remain about one-tenth of Japanese fresh wholesale.\[56\]

### A5. Determinants of price — what the evidence shows
- **Volume (inverse demand).** Japan's official review (Fish Fund, 2012) found Choshi price formation "hard to explain by landings volume". It also found that prices rise toward spring as landings fall and bottom out in January at peak landings.\[27\] The quantitative evidence comes from Korea. Park (2025), using Busan auction data, instrumented with SST and wind speed (2SLS), estimated these demand elasticities:\[57\]

| Size class | Demand elasticity | Implied price flexibility (≈1/elasticity, approximate) |
|---|---|---|
| Large | −0.875 (inelastic) | ≈ −1.14 |
| Medium | −2.450 | ≈ −0.41 |
| Small | −3.444 (feed/processing use) | ≈ −0.29 |

  - **What this means:** extra landings of small fish barely move their price, because they compete with feed substitutes. Scarcity of large fish, on the other hand, raises prices more than proportionally. Revenue therefore rises when the catch shifts toward large fish, which is the economic case for size-selective harvesting.
- **Import price transmission.** In Korea, a 1% rise in the Norwegian import price raises the domestic producer price by 0.38% in the short run and 0.91% in the long run (an ARDL bounds-test study, checked with FMOLS/DOLS on monthly 2015–2024 data, published in 수산해양교육연구 / Journal of Fisheries and Marine Sciences Education). Korean and Japanese domestic prices are effectively anchored to Norwegian quotas.
- **Exchange rates and tariffs.**
  - Yen depreciation from 2022 to 2025, combined with a krone that rose about 30% against the dollar after January 2025, raised the yen cost of imports.\[6\]
  - Johan Kvalheim, the NSC's envoy to Japan, says "producers in Japan are now clearly falling behind in the competition for raw materials, not only because of the price level, but also because the tariffs make direct imports more expensive" (7% on whole fish, 10% on fillets).
- **Demand-side shocks.** The 2017–2019 saba-can boom and COVID-19 disruption mattered, but no quantitative estimates were found. NSC describes Asian demand as inelastic, which is consistent with large fish holding value despite price spikes.\[7\]

## Part B — Interannual Variation in Weight and Length

### B1. Pacific stock (太平洋系群)
- **Biomass history.** Biomass peaked historically in the 1970s, with catch reaching 1.43 Mt in 1978. It crashed to catches of about 20,000 t in 1990–91, recovered on the 2004 and 2013 year classes, and was estimated at 3.411 Mt in 2013 (FRA 2019 assessment).\[5\]\[58\]
- **The 2013 year class.** It made up 90% of abundance in 2014 and 57% in 2015, and age-1+ abundance jumped 4.8× from 2013 to 2014.\[59\]
- **Growth.**
  - FRA reports that mean weight at age in 2018 was well below 2011–2014 and 1970s levels, especially at age 5 (the 2013 cohort).\[58\]
  - NPFC/Japan growth analyses show asymptotic fork length falling from 44.6 to 37.1 cm, with a higher growth coefficient K, after the 2013 year class.\[60\]
  - Kamimura et al. (2021, ICES JMS) found body condition, habitat temperature and growth all declined with both conspecific abundance and Japanese sardine abundance. Annual growth correlated positively with condition.\[61\] The mechanism is density pushing fish into colder, poorer habitat.
- **Maturation.** Isu et al. (2026, Fisheries Science) show the 2013 year class increased the share of older spawners and lowered the gonadosomatic index (GSI). NPFC notes a shift to older average age at maturity, so stunting also lowers reproductive output per tonne.\[62\]\[63\]
- **The 2018 year class.** It appeared strong in survey indices but was "lower-than-expected" in catch-at-age data, according to NPFC 2025.\[63\] This is unresolved, and possibly reflects distribution offshore or toward Russia, or high juvenile catches.
- **Status (NPFC 2025, SAM model).**
  - Catch fell from about 514,000 t (2018) to 135,000 t (2024).\[63\]
  - SSB in 2023 was 16% of its FY2018 peak; SSB2023/SSB_lim = 1.23 (SSB_lim = 107 kt; reference levels 289 kt and 585 kt). SSB in FY2024 was lower again.\[63\]
  - Current fishing mortality corresponds to 16% SPR, "unsustainable".\[63\]
  - Mean recruitment in FY2014–2023 was about 7.4 billion fish, against about 15 billion in the 1970s.\[63\]
  - The Beverton-Holt fit is nearly flat, which means density dependence in recruitment is weak but density dependence in growth is strong.\[63\]
- **2025 FRA assessment.** Pacific stock SSB fell about 30% further.\[64\]
- **Japanese TACs (masaba + gomasaba, Pacific).**
  - FY2024: 353,000 t.
  - FY2025: the Fisheries Agency first proposed 109,000 t (−69.1%; a 68,000 t option was also tabled).\[65\] The final figure was 139,000 t, a 61% cut approved by the Fisheries Policy Council's resource management subcommittee, per Minato Shimbun. It was approved despite exceeding the ABC, which drew criticism from Fisheries Policy Council members and from WWF.\[66\]
  - FY2026: 112,000 t (−19%), approved by the Fisheries Policy Council for July 2026–June 2027, per Minato Shimbun.
  - Because recent catches have run below TAC, the cuts bind less than they appear: the FY2023 Pacific masaba catch was only about 70,000 t.\[24\]

### B2. Tsushima Warm Current / East China Sea–Yellow Sea stock
- **Japan.** Tsushima-stock catch peaked at 411,000 t in 1996 and fell to about 100,000 t in the 2000s. It was 135,000 t in 2024, and 2025 landings on the western side were strong at 179,000 t.\[39\]\[67\] The FY2025 TAC proposal was 225,500 t (+5%).\[65\]
- **Korea.**
  - Catch peaked at 415,003 t in 1996, hit a low of 77,603 t in 2020, and was 124,928 t in 2024. Large purse seiners take 82.2%.\[39\]
  - The fleet shrank from 342 vessels (1992) to 106 (2024), but tonnage per vessel rose from 131 to 188 GT and engine power from 827 to 1,471 hp, a case of technical creep.\[39\]
  - Kwak et al. (2026, Frontiers in Marine Science) estimate B/B_MSY in 2024 at 0.63 (CMSY++) and 0.59 (Bayesian state-space model, BSS), so the stock is overfished.\[39\]\[68\]
  - Korea moved to a 3-year multi-year TAC in 2024 (about 123,000 t per year). For 2026/27–2028/29 it adopted 138,224 t per season for "고등어류" (large purse seine) plus 7,991 t for small purse seine on a pilot, non-sanctioned basis, with 20% banking and borrowing.\[2\]\[69\]
- **The Korean size paradox.** In 2025, high volume coincided with record scarcity of large fish. This points to a strong recent recruitment pulse with density-limited growth, mirroring the Pacific-stock pattern a decade earlier. No peer-reviewed Korean weight-at-age series for 2020–2025 was accessible; it is a gap.
- **Environment–recruitment.** Hiyama et al. (2002, Fisheries Oceanography) found spawning success in the East China Sea/Sea of Japan to be significantly and inversely correlated (P<0.05) with February–June SST at 30°N 125°E.\[39\]\[70\]

### B3. Southeast and Northeast Pacific
- **Peru/Chile.** IMARPE describes the jack mackerel and caballa stocks as being in "healthy condition" (2025). Caballa biology is monitored through gonadosomatic indices and juvenile shares.\[47\]\[71\] No accessible time series of mean length was found.
- **California Current.**
  - Summary biomass (age 1+): 69,823 t (2020); 50,282 t (2021); 45,898 t (2022); 51,060 t (2023); 58,301 t (2024); 61,737 t (2025, projected); 67,954 t (2026, projected). The 2025 assessment is catch-only, so estimates from 2023 onward rest mainly on the stock-recruit curve, and SD is about ±41,000 t.\[72\]
  - The 2024 model-year catch totals 24,163 t, the large majority from Mexican (Baja California) fleets rather than US vessels.\[72\]

## Part C — Oceanographic Theory and Mechanisms

### C1. Kuroshio–Oyashio system
- **Life cycle.** Pacific-stock adults spawn mainly around the Izu Islands from March to June. Larvae are carried along the Kuroshio and Kuroshio Extension to the Kuroshio–Oyashio transition zone, a nursery with high productivity. Juveniles feed toward the Kurils in summer and migrate south to winter off Chiba/Ibaraki.\[63\]
- **Kuroshio Large Meander.** It lasted August 2017–April 2025, 7 years 9 months; JMA's 29 August 2025 press release declared it over and called it the longest since 1965, ahead of the 1975–1980 meander (4 years 8 months).
- **Kuroshio Extension.** From July 2022 the Extension shifted strongly northward, reaching 40°N in winter 2024.\[73\] With a weakened Oyashio, Tohoku waters stayed 3–5 °C warmer than usual, which blocked the autumn southward migration. Fishing grounds formed late and offshore, and seasons shortened.\[5\]\[74\]
  - JAMSTEC notes the meander ended without any recovery in Kuroshio strength, and that the wind systems behind it have shifted northward.\[75\]
  - A multi-century model (Tamura et al., 2026, JGR Oceans) links long meanders to a northward-shifted Extension.\[76\] Recurrence is therefore plausible.
- **Effect on growth and recruitment.** A coupled growth–population model (Progress in Oceanography, 2026) reproduces the 2018–2024 decline and attributes it to reduced growth and recruitment caused by low food under the meander.\[77\]
  - An NPFC working paper finds catch positively related to net primary productivity (NPPV) and to PDO-linked Kuroshio meandering, but not significantly related to the Oyashio southern limit.\[74\]
  - A growth-heterogeneity study found April–June SST at 38–40°N, 141–143°E negatively correlated with age-0 fork length (length–weight relation W = 1.41×10⁻⁶·FL^3.37).\[78\]
- **Distribution shifts and their market effects.**
  - Adults have reached 47°N 166°E since 2018.\[63\] This favoured Russian trawlers and Chinese high-seas light purse seiners and disadvantaged Japanese coastal ports.\[63\]
  - Landing ports shifted (Choshi and Hachinohe collapsed while western ports held up), and freshness and fat varied with distance.
  - Climate projections put the Kuroshio and Oyashio fronts up to 2° further north, with the Oyashio front shifting about 4° in some RCP8.5 models, by the late 21st century.\[79\] Spawning grounds are already reported shifting north with longer spawning seasons.\[77\]

### C2. Recruitment and growth theory applied
- **Growth–survival / match–mismatch.** Temperature- and zooplankton-mediated prey fields along the Kuroshio–Oyashio pathway set early growth rates, and early growth determines mortality (Li et al., 2023).\[77\] This is consistent with the "bigger is better" and stage-duration hypotheses.
- **Optimal environmental window.** Positive SST anomalies are associated with higher biomass. Kuroshio transport has opposite seasonal effects: positive in winter, negative in summer.\[77\]
- **Density dependence.** It is strong in growth, condition and maturity, but weak in recruitment, as the near-flat stock–recruitment curve shows. This asymmetry is why strong year classes produce more but smaller, less valuable fish.
- **Species alternation (Kawasaki).** Japanese sardine resurged after 2010. Kamimura et al. show sardine abundance depresses mackerel condition (interspecific density dependence), and Russian surveys report sardine dominance growing while mackerel's role declines.\[80\] This is consistent with the regime-alternation concept, but it is not proof of a deterministic cycle.\[81\]

### C3. Other systems
- **Tsushima Warm Current.** Current variability and East China Sea SST govern the western stock. The inverse SST–spawning-success relation (Hiyama et al., 2002) suggests warming is a risk. Even so, 2024–2025 western catches were strong, and KNN reports anomalous currents shifted Korean schools toward the East Sea in a recent bumper season.\[70\]\[82\]
- **Humboldt Current.** El Niño drives caballa inshore and south and changes availability to artisanal fleets. The low 2025 quota uptake fits availability limits, but no quantitative ENSO–price link was found.
- **California Current.** Pacific mackerel recruitment is warm-favoured, peaking in the 1980s regime. The current low biomass, with large Mexican catches, persists despite recent marine heatwaves.

## Part D — Synthesis and Correlations

### D1. Conceptual framework
**Ocean state** (Kuroshio path and Extension latitude, Oyashio extent, SST and heatwaves, PDO/ENSO, productivity)
→ **Stock biology** (recruitment through early growth and survival; density- and temperature-dependent growth; condition and fat; maturity; distribution)
→ **Catch composition** (volume, size and fat by port and season; split between national EEZs and the high seas)
→ **Price formation** (size-grade premiums; inelastic demand for large fish, elastic for small; substitution with Norwegian *S. scombrus*, tied to ICES quotas, exchange rates and tariffs)
→ **Fishery economics** (revenue per tonne; whether fish go to feed, export or food; processing raw-material costs; fleet incentives)
→ **Management feedback** (TAC, size limits, closed seasons), which in turn changes density and size.

The critical loop: when juveniles are caught heavily, growth is foregone, so lost revenue exceeds the volume caught. And when stunting comes from density, abundance itself depresses value per fish. A well-designed harvest strategy should target spawning biomass and size structure, not tonnes.

### D2. Strongest quantitative relationships found

| Relationship | Evidence | Strength / sign |
|---|---|---|
| Abundance → growth/condition | Kamimura et al. 2021; L∞ 44.6→37.1 cm | Strong negative (intra- and interspecific) |

| Japan landings ↓ & small size → ex-vessel price | May 2026 national data | −25% volume, +116% price |

No published study was found that statistically links an environmental index directly to mackerel *prices*. That is the central gap this research programme can fill.

### D3. Proposed methodology
1. **Data assembly (monthly, 2000–2026):**
   - Toyosu and Osaka daily and monthly prices by product (fresh, frozen, salted).
   - JAFIC port data by size grade (大/中/小/極小) for Choshi, Hachinohe, Ishinomaki, Matsuura, Karatsu and Sakaiminato.
   - Busan Cooperative Fish Market auction data by grade, and KMI outlook reports.
   - Japan Ministry of Finance (MOF), Korea Customs and UN Comtrade data on HS 030354 (frozen mackerel).
   - NSC weekly prices by size.
   - FRA/NPFC weight-, length- and maturity-at-age and port length-frequency data.
   - Ocean indices: Kuroshio path type (JMA), Kuroshio Extension latitude, Oyashio southern limit, FRA-ROMS temperatures, OISST, PDO, NPGO, ONI and net primary production.
2. **Biology module.** Mixed-effects von Bertalanffy or weight-increment models with cohort random effects. Covariates: own-stock and sardine abundance, habitat SST and prey proxies. Outputs: predicted size-at-age and condition.
3. **Price module.**
   - Hedonic regressions of log price on size grade, fat proxy (month and condition), freshness (distance and port), branding and import price.
   - Inverse-demand systems (GSIDS or inverse AIDS) by size class, with landings instrumented by weather and ocean conditions, following Park (2025).
4. **Time-series linkage.** VAR/VECM or ARDL cointegration across ex-vessel, wholesale and retail prices, Norwegian FOB price, the JPY/NOK exchange rate and landings. Use structural breaks for 2013, 2017, 2022, 2025 and the 2026 Norwegian quota cut.
5. **Integration.** Structural equation modelling (ocean → size → price → revenue) or a Bayesian hierarchical bioeconomic model. Then use management strategy evaluation (MSE), which NPFC now recommends within 2–3 years, to compare HCRs, size limits and closed seasons in terms of revenue, not just tonnes.\[63\]
6. **Key metric.** The "value of foregone growth": the revenue difference between harvesting a cohort at 150–250 g (feed price, ≈¥100–165/kg) and at 400–500 g+ (¥450–800/kg), net of natural mortality (M ≈ 0.6 at age 1 and 0.51 at age 2 under NPFC assumptions).\[63\]

## Timeline of key events
- **1978:** Pacific-stock catch peaks at 1.43 Mt. **1990–91:** catch collapses to about 20 kt.
- **1996–1999:** Korean and Tsushima-stock catches peak (1996). Japan adopts TAC in 1997, Korea in 1999.
- **2002:** Peru reserves caballa for direct human consumption.
- **2004, 2013:** strong Pacific year classes; biomass reaches 3.41 Mt in 2013.
- **2008:** China's chub mackerel catch peaks (592,637 t).
- **~2014–2015:** Chinese high-seas fleet expands; NPFC prioritises chub mackerel (2015); Russian fishery develops.
- **2017–2019:** Japanese exports reach about 230–249 kt and the saba-can boom peaks. The Kuroshio Large Meander begins in August 2017.
- **2018:** stock and catch peak (about 514 kt). The 2018 year class is strong in surveys but weak in catches. Adults reach 47°N 166°E.
- **2021:** Russian catch reaches 87,388 t.
- **2022–2024:** the Kuroshio Extension shifts north; Pacific catches collapse; yen weakens.
- **2024:** NPFC sets a 66,740 t high-seas TAC. Korea introduces a multi-year mackerel TAC.\[83\]\[84\]
- **2025:** NPFC agrees its first stock assessment. Japan cuts the Pacific TAC by 61%, to 139,000 t. Norway's quota falls 22% and prices hit records. The Kuroshio Large Meander ends in April.
- **2026:** Norway's quota falls 51% (to 81,375 t). Japan's FY2026 Pacific TAC is 112 kt. Korea merges mackerels into one 138,224 t multi-year TAC. Japanese ex-vessel and Korean import prices hit records.\[2\]

## Data Gaps
- Size-graded price series (in gram bands) for Japanese Pacific ports, and official Busan auction statistics by grade (bcfm.co.kr; KMI 수산관측).
- Japanese peer-reviewed price-flexibility or hedonic estimates for mackerel. None were found, although 松井 (2007) offers a general framework.
- Korean weight-at-age for 2020–2025, and the Q1 2026 Busan volume inconsistency.
- Chinese domestic wholesale prices for 鲐鱼 (Zhoushan, Ningbo, Qingdao), and a split of China's coastal catch between chub and blue mackerel.
- Taiwan (Nanfang'ao) prices and catch; Russian ex-vessel prices; Chilean (Subpesca/IFOP) caballa data.
- Fat-content time series: no systematic monitoring of 脂肪率 linked to price was found.

## Recommendations
1. **For researchers:** make the size–price elasticity structure the core of any analysis. Estimate it for Japan using JAFIC grade data, then link it to FRA growth models with ocean covariates. This is the most direct, novel way to tie "high-quality biological resources" to market value.
2. **For managers:** the evidence supports protecting juvenile and small fish (size limits, closures in nursery areas, limits on high-seas young-fish catch) on economic grounds as well as biological ones. Revenue from large fish is resilient to price because their demand is inelastic, while small-fish revenue is close to commodity feed value.
3. **For industry and trade:** expect Norwegian scarcity to keep Japanese and Korean prices high through at least 2026–2027, given the ICES advice and the 2026 quota. A recovery in the western Tsushima stock and in Korean volumes will not relieve prices for large fish, because growth is density-limited. Herring substitution and Chinese/Vietnamese processing are the near-term adjustments.
4. **For monitoring:** watch for a recurrence of the Kuroshio meander (cold eddies re-attaching off Kii), Kuroshio Extension latitude, sardine abundance, and the 2024–2026 year-class signals in FRA surveys. All of these lead size and price outcomes by 1–3 years.

## Caveats
- Several price figures come from trade press (Minato Shimbun, Nikkei, Jiji, SeafoodSource, Korea JoongAng Daily, Busan Ilbo) or from secondary aggregators (GD Freak, the Zhoushan export association, Seafood Media). The Choshi landing figure comes from a company press release. These are generally consistent with each other but are not official series.
- Stock assessments are uncertain and have been revised heavily: the 2024 FRA revision cut recent biomass by more than half. Independent analysts dispute both the assessments and how adequate the TACs are.
- Elasticities from Korea may not transfer to Japan. The price-flexibility conversion here is approximate.
- Statements about future prices, and Chinese processors gaining share, are projections.
- Multi-species reporting (さば類, NPFC "chub mackerel", Korea's 고등어류) mixes *S. japonicus* with *S. australasicus*.
