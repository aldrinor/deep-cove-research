# Bioeconomic Dynamics and Morphometric Variability of Pacific Chub Mackerel Across Pacific Rim Aquatic Markets

## Executive Synthesis: The Trans-Pacific Pelagic Nexus

Pacific chub mackerel (*Scomber japonicus* ) represents one of the most ecologically pivotal and commercially valuable small pelagic resources in the global marine economy, ranking as the world's largest mackerel fishery by volume at approximately 1,581,000 metric tons (MT) annually and accumulating 15.4 million MT in global landings between 2010 and 2020 [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Operating within a global mackerel market valued at USD 12.4 billion to USD 13.48 billion in 2025—and projected to expand at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 5.31% to 5.40% through 2030–2034—the chub mackerel segment alone generates USD 3.08 billion in primary commercial value, with the Asia-Pacific region representing the fastest-growing consumption corridor at a 6.41% CAGR [3](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/mackerel-market). Despite its massive harvest volume, the economic valuation of *Scomber japonicus*  across Pacific Rim aquatic markets is highly asymmetric, governed by a steep hedonic pricing function tied to two interdependent biological traits: individual body weight (commercial size grade) and somatic crude lipid concentration [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel).

A synthesis of empirical market records, precision port sampling, and physical oceanography across the Northwest Pacific (Japan, South Korea, China), the Southeast Pacific (Peru, Chile), and the Northeast Pacific (United States) demonstrates that aquatic market price dynamics are directly regulated by basin-scale environmental forcing [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). In the Northwest Pacific, decadal secular warming (+1.11∘C+1.11^\circ\text{C} over the past century), the prolonged 2018–2024 Kuroshio Large Meander (KLM), and intense intra- and inter-specific competition with resurgent Japanese sardine (*Sardinops melanostictus* ) populations in the Kuroshio–Oyashio Transition Zone (KOTZ) have suppressed cohort growth rates, compressed thermal habitats, and triggered a severe stock decline between 2022 and 2025 [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276). Conversely, in the Humboldt Current upwelling system off Peru, *Scomber japonicus*  responds to El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) warm anomalies via oceanic habitat expansion, even as post-ENSO reproductive disruptions and regulatory quota closures constrain industrial supply [10](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227526988_From_small-scale_habitat_loopholes_to_decadal_cycles_a_habitat-based_hypothesis_explaining_fluctuation_in_pelagic_fish_populations_off_Peru).

These oceanographic regimes alter the fork length (FLFL), body weight (BWBW), and lipid accumulation of harvested cohorts, which subsequently bifurcate global supply chains [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043). When environmental forcing and density-dependent growth skew landings toward juvenile, lipid-poor fish (<30 cm<30\text{ cm} FLFL, <300 g<300\text{ g} BWBW, <10%<10\% lipid), domestic East Asian table-fish markets experience acute supply deficits and severe retail price inflation, forcing processors to import high-priced Norwegian Atlantic mackerel (*Scomber scombrus* ) while diverting domestic small-grade catches into low-margin bulk export channels for West Africa, canning, or aquafeed reduction [8](https://globalmackerel.com/insights/price-index).

## Hedonic Price Architecture Across Major Pacific Rim Markets

### Size-Lipid Valuation Mechanics and Global FOB Benchmarks

In global pelagic trade, wholesale and ex-vessel prices for *Scomber japonicus*  are determined by hedonic valuation models in which physical attributes—specifically individual body weight, muscle lipid percentage, freezing protocol, and gear handling—dictate market clearing prices [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Because *Scomber japonicus*  possesses a swim bladder, it is slightly more buoyant and less dense than Atlantic mackerel (*Scomber scombrus* ) at equivalent body weights, yielding distinct fillet textures, Harmonized System (HS) tariff classifications, and culinary applications [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Across all Pacific Rim trading hubs, price formation follows a non-linear step function anchored by size and fat thresholds [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Fish below 10% crude lipid trade at a global commodity floor regardless of geographic origin, whereas fish between 10% and 18% lipid command moderate incremental premiums per percentage point, and specimens exceeding 18% lipid capture an exponential price premium in Northeast Asian sashimi, sushi, and gourmet curing sectors [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel).

At the global level, wholesale frozen mackerel prices in 2022–2025 spanned a wide spectrum depending on market tier and processing state: global farmgate and ex-vessel equivalent prices ranged between USD 1.89/kg and USD 4.66/kg (with ultra-premium specialty lots reaching USD 21.89/kg in 2022), while international wholesale and import/export clearing prices generally operated between USD 1.84/kg and USD 14.49/kg [21](https://dir.tridge.com/prices/frozen-mackerel). Within this global pricing hierarchy, Pacific chub mackerel specifications compete against benchmark Atlantic mackerel (*S. scombrus* ), spotted mackerel (*S. australasicus* ), and horse/jack mackerel (*Trachurus*  spp.) tiers, with five standardized commercial weight grades segmenting the *Scomber japonicus*  supply chain into non-overlapping economic channels [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel).

The structural stratification across these tiers highlights why origin and harvest timing are non-substitutable in commercial procurement [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Buyers requiring consistent volumes of 300 g+ *Scomber japonicus*  with lipid concentrations above 14% must source from Japanese or South Korean autumn-winter fisheries, as Peruvian landings yield predominantly 100–300 g fish that cannot satisfy East Asian culinary fat thresholds [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Conversely, for high-volume bulk buyers in West Africa and the MENA region who prioritize landed cost per metric ton without strict lipid clauses, Peruvian year-round BQF blocks and Korean September–February 200–300 g BQF production provide the most competitive FOB pricing, competing directly against Atlantic horse mackerel (*Trachurus trachurus*  from Mauritania at USD 900–1,400/MT and Morocco at USD 800–1,200/MT) and Cape horse mackerel (*Trachurus capensis*  from Namibia at USD 1,000–1,600/MT) [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel).

### Regional Market Divergence: Premium Table Fish vs. Bulk Protein and Aquafeed

The economic interplay among Pacific Rim markets is defined by acute regional specialization and cross-origin substitution [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Japan operates the most quality-stratified market globally, harvesting two primary subpopulations: the Pacific Ocean stock and the Tsushima Warm Current stock (shared in the East China Sea and Sea of Japan) [24](https://www.seafoodwatch.org/globalassets/sfw-data-blocks/reports/s/seafood-watch-sardines-japan-27868.pdf). Landings are led by Choshi Port in Chiba Prefecture—which anchors eastern Japan's volume and produces branded high-fat *Choshi Gokujo Saba* —followed by Matsuura and Nagasaki Ports in Kyushu, Kesennuma in Miyagi, and Sakaiminato in Tottori [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). While ultra-fresh, hook-and-line or pound-net *Sekisaba*  from Oita Prefecture commands elite domestic restaurant prices, Japan's frozen processing sector relies on autumn purse-seine landings of 300–500 g fish with 16–22% fat [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). However, because Northwest Pacific chub mackerel catches collapsed by two-thirds between 2021 and 2024 (falling to 158,000 MT regionally), the Fisheries Agency of Japan enacted a 60% reduction in the 2025 Total Allowable Catch (TAC) from 350,000 MT to 139,000 MT [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276). This domestic supply shock has deepened Japan's structural reliance on long-term supply contracts with Norwegian exporters of *Scomber scombrus*  (trading at USD 2,500–5,000/MT across standard and FAS grades) as well as counter-seasonal Q2 imports of Australian spotted mackerel (*Scomber australasicus*  at USD 1,800–2,800/MT) [3](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/mackerel-market).

South Korea exhibits the highest per capita consumption of Pacific chub mackerel (*godeungeo* ) in the world, positioning Busan as the central clearinghouse for Northeast Asian pelagic trade [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Historically, Korean-origin frozen *Scomber japonicus*  (supplied by major conglomerates such as Sajo and Dongwon) has traded at a 5–12% FOB price discount relative to equivalent Japanese specifications, making Busan the preferred sourcing hub for international buyers seeking 300–500 g IQF whole round fish (10–18% fat in the October–December peak) at a lower landed cost [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Between 2020 and 2024, South Korean total landings averaged 111,143 MT annually (rising from 77,603 MT in 2020 to 124,928 MT in 2024), with the Busan-based large purse-seine fleet accounting for 82.2% (91,349 MT) of national production, supplemented by set nets (6.4%, 7,075 MT), small purse seines (3.6%, 3,984 MT), and drift gillnets (2.3%, 2,606 MT) [29](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1814806/full).

Despite stable aggregate landing volumes in South Korea, a profound structural bifurcation occurred between 2022 and 2026 due to the morphometric composition of the catch [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). The National Institute of Fisheries Science (NIFS) classifies commercial landings into four size grades that map rigidly onto end-use markets: Grade 1 (mean FLFL 36.8 cm, mean BWBW 694 g), Grade 2 (34.5 cm, 543 g), and 83% of Grade 3 (31.8 cm, 403 g) exceed the 30 cm threshold required by the domestic human food market, whereas Grade 4 (mean FLFL 27.6 cm, mean BWBW 251 g, with 83% under 30 cm and 25% under 200 g) is rejected by Korean retail consumers and diverted to export, canning, or animal/aquaculture feed [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). As Grade 4 swelled to 97% of landed weight by 2025, domestic supply of table-grade mackerel (≥30 cm\ge 30\text{ cm}) evaporated [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Consequently, the Korean retail price for a pair of large salted mackerel surged by 51.5% from 6,803 KRW in December 2023 to 10,363 KRW in December 2025 [18](https://www.seoulz.com/korea-norway-mackerel/). To bridge the domestic deficit in large fish, Korean importers were forced to absorb a 93.2% price hike on imported frozen mackerel (predominantly Norwegian *S. scombrus*  and Taiwanese/regional supply), with landed import prices doubling from USD 2.70/kg in the first half of 2025 to USD 5.20/kg in the first half of 2026 [18](https://www.seoulz.com/korea-norway-mackerel/). Demonstrating extreme price inelasticity for a culturally essential protein, Korean buyers did not curtail volume when unit prices doubled, pushing first-half 2026 import value to USD 115 million [18](https://www.seoulz.com/korea-norway-mackerel/). Simultaneously, Korean processors exported massive volumes of small Grade 4 fish to West Africa (Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Nigeria), expanding total chub mackerel export revenues from USD 28 million to USD 200 million on the back of a depreciating Korean won (KRW) and supply gaps left by sanctioned Russian pelagic fleets [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full).

In China, *Scomber japonicus*  underpins a massive domestic canning and re-export complex centered in Zhoushan and Dalian, which drives the USD 3.08 billion global chub mackerel canning segment and fuels emerging Asian canned seafood demand (+0.8% impact on global market CAGR) [3](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/mackerel-market). Following the 2014 launch of China's distant-water lighting purse-seine fleet in the North Pacific Fisheries Commission (NPFC) Convention Area (33°N–45°N), Chinese vessels have harvested high-seas aggregations composed primarily of small-to-medium individuals (mean FLFL 21.2–26.8 cm), which are suited for thermal canning and industrial processing rather than the high-fat sashimi market [30](https://academic.oup.com/mcf/article-pdf/17/6/vtaf039/65499884/vtaf039.pdf).

Across the eastern Pacific Rim, market structures diverge sharply between South and North America. In Peru, *Scomber japonicus peruanus*  (*caballa* ) is co-harvested with Chilean jack mackerel (*Trachurus murphyi* ) by industrial and artisanal purse-seine fleets monitored by the Instituto del Mar del Perú (IMARPE) and regulated by the Ministry of Production (PRODUCE) [15](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/imarpe/informes-publicaciones/4762072-informe-sobre-la-pesca-exploratoria-de-los-recursos-jurel-y-caballa-del-21-al-27-de-julio-del-2023). Under Supreme Decree D.S. 001-2002, Peruvian chub mackerel is legally restricted to Direct Human Consumption (CHD), yielding 90,515 MT in 2022 (1.6% of Peru's 5.5 million MT marine capture volume) [15](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/imarpe/informes-publicaciones/4762072-informe-sobre-la-pesca-exploratoria-de-los-recursos-jurel-y-caballa-del-21-al-27-de-julio-del-2023). Because Peruvian landings consist primarily of 100–300 g fish with modest lipid levels, Callao processors package the catch in 10 kg and 20 kg BQF blocks at USD 1,100–1,500/MT FOB, establishing the global reference price floor for human-consumption pelagic trade into West Africa and MENA [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). However, an intense shadow price linkage connects chub mackerel to the global aquafeed sector: while retail outlets capture 49.31% of global mackerel revenue, aquaculture feed and pet-food demand is the fastest-growing end-use segment at a 7.95% CAGR through 2030 (+0.6% global CAGR driver) [3](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/mackerel-market). When El Niño events disrupt Peruvian anchoveta (*Engraulis ringens* ) landings, global fish oil prices spike—surpassing USD 11,000/MT in January 2024 alongside salmon price volatility above USD 10,000/MT—which intensifies economic pressure to divert sub-200 g chub mackerel in Asia and South America into marine aquafeed diets (such as Japanese amberjack, *Seriola quinqueradiata* , and bluefin tuna cage culture) [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). In the United States, by contrast, the California Current Pacific mackerel fishery managed by the Pacific Fishery Management Council (PFMC) has contracted from 7,000 MT in 2001 to 1,357 MT in 2014 (generating just USD 155,337 in ex-vessel revenue, or ~USD 114/MT) and only 234,003 pounds (~106 MT) in the 2022–2023 season against an Optimum Yield of 1.2 million pounds [37](https://calcofi.org/downloads/publications/calcofireports/v56/Vol56-Fisheries.web.1-30.pdf). As California's fishing economy pivoted toward high-value benthic and pelagic invertebrates (market squid and Dungeness crab generating USD 152 million ex-vessel vs. USD 32 million for groundfish and salmon), US domestic chub mackerel has been relegated to incidental wetfish catch, while US foodservice demand for table-grade mackerel is satisfied by importing 500–1,200 MT annually of frozen Japanese *S. japonicus*  and *S. australasicus*  [24](https://www.seafoodwatch.org/globalassets/sfw-data-blocks/reports/s/seafood-watch-sardines-japan-27868.pdf).

## Interannual Variations in Morphometrics, Growth Trajectories, and Proximate Composition

### Allometric Weight-Length Scaling and Von Bertalanffy Growth Heterogeneity

Because commercial grading and price tiers depend on fish crossing strict weight (200 g, 300 g, 400 g, 500 g) and length (30 cm FLFL) thresholds, interannual variations in the length-weight relationship (LWR) and somatic growth trajectory exert a direct multiplier effect on fishery revenue [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). The somatic scaling of *Scomber japonicus*  is modeled via the power-law allometric equation:

W=aLbW = a L^b

where WW is total body weight (g), LL is fork length (mm or cm), aa is the condition scalar, and bb is the allometric growth exponent indicating isometric (b≈3.0b \approx 3.0) or hyperallometric (b>3.0b > 3.0, where fish become progressively more rotund and lipid-dense as they lengthen) growth [32](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358772181_Growth_Heterogeneity_of_Chub_Mackerel_Scomber_japonicus_in_the_Northwest_Pacific_Ocean). Cohort length-at-age (LtL_t) is governed by the Von Bertalanffy growth function (VBGF):

Lt=L∞(1−e−K(t−t0))L_t = L_\infty \left(1 - e^{-K(t - t_0)}\right)

where L∞L_\infty is the asymptotic fork length, KK is the Brody growth rate coefficient (yr−1\text{yr}^{-1}) measuring the rate at which L∞L_\infty is approached, and t0t_0 is the theoretical age at zero length [32](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358772181_Growth_Heterogeneity_of_Chub_Mackerel_Scomber_japonicus_in_the_Northwest_Pacific_Ocean).

Empirical sampling from distant-water fishing vessels operating in the Northwest Pacific high seas between 2016 and 2021 reveals substantial interannual plasticity in both growth rates and allometric body condition [32](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358772181_Growth_Heterogeneity_of_Chub_Mackerel_Scomber_japonicus_in_the_Northwest_Pacific_Ocean). Across 2,686 specimens collected between 2016 and 2020 (spanning 40–294 mm FLFL and 0.8–311.8 g BWBW), the baseline pooled length-weight relationship was estimated at W=1.41×10−6L3.18W = 1.41 \times 10^{-6} L^{3.18} (in mm-g units), with linear mixed-effects models (LMEM) confirming that the scalar parameter aa varies significantly as a random effect of both year and season (spring, summer, autumn) [32](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358772181_Growth_Heterogeneity_of_Chub_Mackerel_Scomber_japonicus_in_the_Northwest_Pacific_Ocean). Notably, individual chub mackerel during the 2016–2020 high-density regime exhibited a significantly slower somatic growth rate than cohorts documented in earlier historical decades [32](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358772181_Growth_Heterogeneity_of_Chub_Mackerel_Scomber_japonicus_in_the_Northwest_Pacific_Ocean).

A granular examination of the 2016–2021 time series exposes three interconnected biological dynamics that govern commercial yield [33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12024016/). First, the stock experienced pronounced allometric flattening under increasing mortality: in 2016, Northwest Pacific chub mackerel displayed strong positive hyperallometry (b=3.53b = 3.53), meaning individuals accumulated substantial somatic mass and condition per unit of length gain [33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12024016/). Second, growth coefficients (KK) and asymptotic sizes (L∞L_\infty) oscillated sharply across cohorts: during 2018–2019, the Brody growth rate KK decelerated to 0.31–0.33 yr−10.31–0.33\text{ yr}^{-1} and the Length-Based Bayesian (LBB) asymptotic fork length contracted to a minimum of 36.2 cm in 2019, before rebounding to L∞=41.3–42.35 cmL_\infty = 41.3–42.35\text{ cm} (K=0.58 yr−1K = 0.58\text{ yr}^{-1}) in 2020 as recruitment density temporarily eased [33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12024016/).

Third, decadal morphometric monitoring along the Korean Peninsula reveals a stark contrast between stable population-wide mean length and shifting commercial grade proportions [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). However, while population-wide length-at-age did not uniformly shrink, the commercial grade composition within the small-fish fraction shifted drastically, with Grade 4 (27.6 cm27.6\text{ cm} mean FLFL, 251 g251\text{ g} mean BWBW) rising monotonically from 77% to 97% of total landed weight (p=0.003,τ=+0.67p = 0.003, \tau = +0.67) [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). ### Ontogenetic Trophic Shifts and Seasonal Lipid Accumulation

The strict commercial boundary at 30 cm FLFL (~300 g BWBW) in East Asian markets is not an arbitrary culinary convention; it is a direct reflection of the ontogenetic bioenergetics and trophic ecology of *Scomber japonicus*  [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043). During its early life history (hatching after 2.5 days into post-larvae up to 1.09 cm, juveniles from 1.09 to 4.55 cm, young from 4.55 to 16.36 cm, and immature stages from 16.36 to 24.8 cm, before reaching adult classification at ≥24.8 cm\ge 24.8\text{ cm} and 50% sexual maturity at Lm50=29.5 cmL_{m50} = 29.5\text{ cm}), chub mackerel prioritizes skeletal elongation and protein synthesis over lipid storage [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043).

Stable isotope (δ13C\delta^{13}\text{C} and δ15N\delta^{15}\text{N}, using a copepod baseline of δ15Nbase=6\textperthousand\delta^{15}\text{N}_{\text{base}} = 6\text{\textperthousand} and a trophic enrichment factor of 3.4\textperthousand3.4\text{\textperthousand}) and fatty acid biomarker analyses of Northwest Pacific high-seas specimens (100–250+ mm FLFL) show that smaller size classes occupy an average trophic level of 3.19 (ranging from 3.12 to 3.22 across five length bins) [49](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/12/8/1335). In these sub-25 cm cohorts, the 161–190 mm length group exhibits the widest isotopic niche width (0.901\textperthousand20.901\text{\textperthousand}^2 vs. 0.653–0.895\textperthousand20.653–0.895\text{\textperthousand}^2 in other groups) and the highest niche overlap, indicating severe intraspecific foraging competition for zooplankton and planktonic bacteria (evidenced by C15:0 and C17:0 odd-chain fatty acid signatures) [49](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/12/8/1335). At this stage, muscle lipids are dominated by structural membrane phospholipids rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs account for 53.22% to 67.47% of total fatty acids, with docosahexaenoic acid [DHA, C22:6n-3] comprising over 65.69% of PUFAs, alongside 26.07%–33.36% saturated fatty acids [SFAs]), while total crude lipid concentration in wet muscle remains low [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043).

As individuals grow past 25–30 cm FLFL, stomach content analyses of 2,503 specimens from Jeju Island (21.0–43.6 cm21.0–43.6\text{ cm} FLFL) and the Yellow Sea (19.6–41.5 cm19.6–41.5\text{ cm} FLFL) demonstrate a decisive ontogenetic shift from zooplanktivory (copepods, euphausiids, and amphipods) to active piscivory [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/17/12/1804). Larger chub mackerel school at ~25 m depth and prey heavily on energy-dense small pelagic fish—principally Japanese anchovy (*Engraulis japonicus* ), which constitutes up to 98.8% of the summer Index of Relative Importance (IRI%) in the Yellow Sea [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/17/12/1804). Consequently, the trophic level of *Scomber japonicus*  jumps from 3.80 in small coastal size groups (Group A) and 3.82 in medium groups (Group B) to 4.05 in the largest size class (Group C, overall mean 3.87) [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/17/12/1804).

This trophic elevation unlocks the metabolic capacity for massive seasonal neutral lipid (triacylglycerol) storage, creating a stark biochemical divergence between sub-30 cm and supra-30 cm fish along the Korean coast [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043). In sub-30 cm cohorts (25–30 cm25–30\text{ cm} FLFL), crude lipid content remains depressed throughout the year, ranging between 3.06 and 3.87 g/100g from January to April, reaching a minor maximum of only 5.82 g/100g in March, and plummeting to 1.16 g/100g in July among individuals <25.5 cm<25.5\text{ cm} [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043). Even in late autumn, 200–300 g Korean BQF exports achieve only 8–14% fat during the September–November peak and drop to 6–10% in the January–February tail season [22](https://globalmackerel.com/origins/south-korea). Conversely, in supra-30 cm cohorts (30–35 cm30–35\text{ cm} FLFL), crude lipid content is inversely coupled with the gonadosomatic index (GSI, r2=0.19–0.59r^2 = 0.19–0.59 across the year, peaking in May before spawning) [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043). Lipid reserves in these larger adults hit their annual nadir in March–May (6.77 g/100g in March for 30–35 cm fish) as stored energy is mobilized for gonadal recrudescence and spring spawning [6](https://www.trjfas.org/pdf.php?id=1043). Following spawning, intensive summer piscivory and subarctic copepod foraging drive rapid lipogenesis from June through November, propelling crude lipid content in 30–35 cm fish to 27.39 g/100g in September (with ash content ranging from 1.26 g/100g in August to 1.96 g/100g in November, while 400–500 g Japanese Pacific coast catches sustain 16–22% fat through December and hot-smoked processed fillets reach 30.29 g/100g lipid and 23.88 g/100g protein) [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). However, high polyunsaturated lipid content also accelerates post-harvest lipid oxidation: during storage at 0∘C0^\circ\text{C}, the acid value of *S. japonicus*  rises rapidly from 13 mg/g to 21 mg/g within 48 hours alongside total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), necessitating rapid *koori-jime*  (ice-slurry) or RSW (refrigerated seawater) chilling and FAS/IQF blast freezing to preserve premium auction value [8](https://globalmackerel.com/insights/price-index).

## Oceanographic Forcing and Bioeconomic Feedback Loops

### Western Boundary Currents, the Kuroshio Large Meander, and Density-Dependent Growth

The interannual morphometric and biomass fluctuations that destabilize Pacific Rim mackerel markets are governed by two distinct physical-biological coupling regimes: western boundary current convergence in the Northwest Pacific and eastern boundary wind-driven upwelling in the Southeast and Northeast Pacific [10](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227526988_From_small-scale_habitat_loopholes_to_decadal_cycles_a_habitat-based_hypothesis_explaining_fluctuation_in_pelagic_fish_populations_off_Peru).

In the Northwest Pacific, the Pacific stock of *Scomber japonicus*  executes an extensive annual migration within a dynamic boundary current system [24](https://www.seafoodwatch.org/globalassets/sfw-data-blocks/reports/s/seafood-watch-sardines-japan-27868.pdf). Spawning occurs between February and June in warm coastal waters around the Izu Islands, off southwestern Japan, and in the East China Sea (where water temperatures span 14–22∘C14–22^\circ\text{C} within the species' broader 5–27∘C5–27^\circ\text{C} and 0–100 m0–100\text{ m} depth tolerance) [23](https://globalmackerel.com/products/japanese-jack-mackerel). Larvae and age-0 juveniles are advected northeastward by the Kuroshio Current and Kuroshio Extension into the Kuroshio–Oyashio Transition Zone (KOTZ, 35∘N–42∘N,140∘E–160∘E35^\circ\text{N}–42^\circ\text{N}, 140^\circ\text{E}–160^\circ\text{E}), while older cohorts migrate northward from June to September to forage in the nutrient-rich subarctic Oyashio Current Large Marine Ecosystem (a Class II moderately productive ecosystem yielding 150–300 g C m−2 yr−1150–300\text{ g C m}^{-2}\text{ yr}^{-1} and chlorophyll-*a*  concentrations >2 mg/m3>2\text{ mg/m}^3) before returning south in November–January [23](https://globalmackerel.com/products/japanese-jack-mackerel).

Within this western boundary system, thermal forcing in nursery grounds interacts with intense density-dependent competition and current path anomalies to dictate the size distribution and abundance of harvested mackerel [12](https://access.earthdata.nasa.gov/citations/CIT4321030586-ESDIS). Otolith oxygen isotope (δ18O\delta^{18}\text{O}) reconstructions and long-term VPA series (1970–1997 and 2014–2025) demonstrate that the fork length of age-0 chub mackerel is negatively correlated with sea surface temperature (SST) in the KOTZ nursery and feeding grounds [52](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2022.1063468/full). Higher SST elevates basal metabolic demands while stratifying the surface mixed layer, reducing nutrient flux and shifting the zooplankton assemblage from large, high-caloric, lipid-rich subarctic copepods (*Neocalanus*  and *Calanus*  spp.) toward small, low-caloric, lipid-poor warm-water copepods [23](https://globalmackerel.com/products/japanese-jack-mackerel).

Concurrently, intra- and inter-specific density-dependent mechanisms constrain cohort growth trajectories [13](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/78/9/3254/6380055). During the 1970–1997 cycle, total stock biomass fluctuated between 0.2 and 5.9 million MT and age-0 recruitment varied by nearly two orders of magnitude between 0.2 and 14 billion individuals, with fork length at age 0 (at 6 months of age) and subsequent annual length increments (GL=LA+1−LAGL = L_{A+1} - L_A) correlating negatively with year-class strength and total pelagic biomass [13](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/78/9/3254/6380055). Following a prolonged low-biomass regime from the late 1990s through 2007, strong recruitment cohorts in 2012 and 2013–2016 triggered a rapid population resurgence alongside a massive expansion of the Japanese sardine (*Sardinops melanostictus* ) stock [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276). When both conspecific (chub mackerel) and heterospecific (Japanese sardine) abundances peaked in the shared subarctic summer feeding grounds, intense trophic competition depressed mackerel body condition (KK), forced schools into cooler, suboptimal thermal habitats, and significantly retarded length-at-age growth trajectories after age 0 (for the 2013–2016 cohorts) and after age 2 (for the 2012 cohort) [13](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/78/9/3254/6380055). Because maternal fecundity and egg diameter in *Scomber*  spp. depend on adult somatic condition, this density-dependent growth bottleneck delayed sexual maturation and reduced reproductive output [13](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/78/9/3254/6380055).

Compounding density-dependent stunting, the Kuroshio Current entered a prolonged Large Meander (KLM) state between 2018 and 2024 [12](https://access.earthdata.nasa.gov/citations/CIT4321030586-ESDIS). The southward deflection of the Kuroshio axis off central Honshu altered coastal spawning ground distributions around the Izu Islands, disrupted larval transport pathways into the KOTZ, and induced severe cohort-specific recruitment vulnerability [12](https://access.earthdata.nasa.gov/citations/CIT4321030586-ESDIS). Pooled Catch-MSY and Bayesian Schaefer production models (where Bt+1=Bt+r(1−Bt/k)Bt−CtB_{t+1} = B_t + r(1 - B_t/k)B_t - C_t, with intrinsic growth rate r≈0.48–0.58 yr−1r \approx 0.48–0.58\text{ yr}^{-1} and carrying capacity k≈2,334 ktk \approx 2,334\text{ kt}) show that while the North Pacific stock remained healthy (B/BMSY>1.0B/B_{\text{MSY}} > 1.0; B/B0=0.80B/B_0 = 0.80 in 2021 and 0.670.67 in 2022) during the peak of the 2010s cohorts, recruitment failure under the 2018–2024 KLM anomaly caused B/BMSYB/B_{\text{MSY}} to plunge to 0.510.51 in 2023–2024, classifying the stock as overfished [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276). Even though high-seas lighting purse-seine vessel numbers in the NPFC area remained stable during 2022–2024, regional catches fell by two-thirds from 2021 levels to just 158,000 MT in 2024—triggering Japan's 60% TAC cut for 2025 and starving East Asian markets of 400–500 g+ adult cohorts [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276).

### Eastern Boundary Upwelling, ENSO Teleconnections, and Aquafeed Substitution

In the Southeast Pacific, the Northern Humboldt Current System (NHCS) off Peru operates under an inverted oceanographic response to basin-scale warming [10](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227526988_From_small-scale_habitat_loopholes_to_decadal_cycles_a_habitat-based_hypothesis_explaining_fluctuation_in_pelagic_fish_populations_off_Peru). Under normal and La Niña conditions, intense coastal wind-driven upwelling supports massive biomasses of cold-water stenothermal Peruvian anchoveta (*Engraulis ringens* , accounting for 76.7% of Peru's landings at 4,118,122 MT in 2022), which forms the foundation of the global fishmeal and fish oil industry [34](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/policies-for-the-future-of-fisheries-and-aquaculture-in-peru_3cccc492-en/full-report/the-state-of-fisheries-and-aquaculture-in-peru_539ad89e.html). Conversely, *Scomber japonicus peruanus*  and *Trachurus murphyi*  are transboundary, eurithermal pelagic predators distributed across the 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) boundary [10](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227526988_From_small-scale_habitat_loopholes_to_decadal_cycles_a_habitat-based_hypothesis_explaining_fluctuation_in_pelagic_fish_populations_off_Peru). During El Niño events (such as 1997–1998, the 2017 Coastal El Niño, and the 2023–2024 ENSO cycle), Kelvin wave propagation deepens the thermocline and suppresses cold nutrient upwelling, causing anchoveta biomass to collapse (for example, plunging from 5.8 million MT to 1.2 million MT during the 1997–1998 event) [10](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227526988_From_small-scale_habitat_loopholes_to_decadal_cycles_a_habitat-based_hypothesis_explaining_fluctuation_in_pelagic_fish_populations_off_Peru). Simultaneously, the coastward intrusion of warm subtropical oceanic waters opens a spatial "habitat loophole" for *Scomber japonicus* , increasing its coastal availability and biomass in Peruvian waters during and immediately following moderate warm anomalies, where it has contributed up to 57% of specific artisanal pelagic catch increases in northern Peru [10](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227526988_From_small-scale_habitat_loopholes_to_decadal_cycles_a_habitat-based_hypothesis_explaining_fluctuation_in_pelagic_fish_populations_off_Peru).

The bioeconomic consequences of the 2023–2024 ENSO cycle illustrate a complex two-stage market transmission across both direct human consumption and global aquafeed channels [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11482318/). To manage high environmental variability in the first stage, IMARPE conducts acoustic and exploratory fishing assessments (such as the July 2023 exploratory campaign) and recommends adaptive exploitation rates (E=0.026,0.039, and 0.052 yr−1E = 0.026, 0.039,\text{ and }0.052\text{ yr}^{-1}) [15](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/imarpe/informes-publicaciones/4762072-informe-sobre-la-pesca-exploratoria-de-los-recursos-jurel-y-caballa-del-21-al-27-de-julio-del-2023). Under an IMARPE biological limit reference point of 91,000 MT (or 68,081 MT effective limit), PRODUCE allocates approximately 65% to the large-scale industrial purse-seine fleet (46,081 MT quota plus 22,000 MT incidental reserve) and 35% (22,919 MT) to the artisanal fleet (where vessels <12 m<12\text{ m} LOA represent 78% of Peru's 18,163 registered vessels) to ensure affordable CHD supply [34](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/policies-for-the-future-of-fisheries-and-aquaculture-in-peru_3cccc492-en/full-report/the-state-of-fisheries-and-aquaculture-in-peru_539ad89e.html). During early 2024, concentrated coastal availability allowed the industrial fleet to rapidly exhaust its *caballa*  allocation, prompting PRODUCE (via Ministerial Resolution No. 2255410-1 on January 24, 2024) to close industrial extractive operations [16](https://busquedas.elperuano.pe/dispositivo/NL/2255410-1). Following the post-ENSO oceanographic normalization in 2024—which drove a 62.6% rebound in Peru's aggregate fishery landings to 5.8 million MT due to anchoveta recovery—total CHD landings contracted by 26.8%, with *caballa*  landings falling 29.6% year-over-year alongside jumbo flying squid (*Dosidicus gigas* , −69.7%-69.7\%) and mahi-mahi (*Coryphaena hippurus* , −38.3%-38.3\%), tightening global supply of 150–300 g BQF blocks and firming the Callao FOB price floor at USD 1,100–1,500/MT (while overall Peruvian fishery export prices averaged USD 2,413/MT across 1.495 million MT, worth USD 3.608 billion in 2024) [8](https://globalmackerel.com/insights/price-index).

In the second stage of transmission, the 2023 El Niño anchoveta disruption triggered an acute global shortage of marine lipids, propelling global fish oil prices above USD 11,000/MT in January 2024 [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11482318/). In Pacific Rim mariculture—most notably Japan's floating raft culture of amberjack (*Seriola quinqueradiata* , reared at 20–29∘C20–29^\circ\text{C} and 30–36\textperthousand30–36\text{\textperthousand} salinity at densities of 25,000 fish per 30×30×15 m30 \times 30 \times 15\text{ m} cage, where 1-year to 3-year cultured fish historically fetched 500 to 800 JPY/kg) and China's massive fed marine finfish sector (1,750,000 MT in China out of 9,190,000 MT globally)—juvenile and sub-200 g *Scomber japonicus*  (25% of Korean Grade 4 and small Peruvian/Chinese grades) serve as a direct feed input [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). When fishmeal and fish oil prices surge, aquafeed mills and cage operators bid up the floor price of sub-200 g chub mackerel, creating a hard bioeconomic floor under small-fish grades even when human-consumption markets reject them [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11482318/).

Historically, Korean purse-seine operators steamed 250 to 400+ km into the Yellow Sea (August–December) and around Jeju Island and the East China Sea (October–February) to target mixed and adult overwintering schools [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel). Because Grade 4 fish fetch a severe price discount—the unit price ratio of Grade 4 relative to Grade 1 (P4/P1P_4 / P_1) remained depressed and virtually unchanged from 0.160.16 to 0.150.15 between 2014 and 2025—conventional bioeconomic theory predicts that fishers should selectively avoid juvenile-dominated grounds [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Instead, in the winter of 2025, the Busan large purse-seine fleet executed an unprecedented spatial pivot into the Southern East Sea (SES; statistical blocks 76–80, 82–91, and 93–96) [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). The SES share of winter catch surged to 83.8% in 2025, compared to 0.0%–36.7% across the preceding decade (a single-year residual anomaly of z=+5.3z = +5.3 against the 2001–2024 trend), while SES accounted for 62% of total annual auctioned weight (versus 0.04%–15.3% historically) [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Simultaneously, the spatial Gini coefficient of catch rose to 0.80 (z=+2.3z = +2.3) and the catch centroid shifted to 129.4∘E129.4^\circ\text{E} (z=+2.7z = +2.7), the easternmost coordinate on record [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Oceanographically, the SES region sits along the southward overwintering migration route contiguous with coastal nursery grounds, where autumn SST warming provided a permissive thermal background for dense juvenile aggregation, resulting in Grade 4 fish (<30 cm<30\text{ cm} FLFL) comprising 97.0% of the winter 2025 catch inside SES compared to 75.3% in non-SES waters [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full).

Although the unit price ratio P4/P1P_4 / P_1 remained stagnant at 0.150.15, three macroeconomic and operational variables shifted the fleet's profit function (Πm,t\Pi_{m,t}) in favor of SES juvenile harvesting [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). First, fishing in SES slashed the catch-weighted steaming distance from Busan (DmD_m) to just 117 km in winter 2025, compared to 402 km in 2022 and 253 km in 2024 (z=−3.2z = -3.2), drastically cutting fuel expenditure (cfuel,t⋅Dmc_{\text{fuel},t} \cdot D_m) in an era of elevated post-2022 marine fuel costs and high global reefer freight rates (which peaked at USD 4,200/FEU on Asia–Europe lanes in Q3 2024) [4](https://www.marketresearchfuture.com/reports/mackerel-market-27192). Second, dense schooling in SES maximized physical catch per unit effort (q4,SESB4,SESq_{4,\text{SES}} B_{4,\text{SES}}) [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Third, the opening of the high-volume West African frozen export channel (absorbing sub-30 cm fish to replace Russian supply) and a weakening Korean won (FXt\text{FX}_t) prevented auction market saturation, allowing the fleet to clear virtually unlimited volumes of Grade 4 fish without collapsing P4P_4 further [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Consequently, Grade 4's contribution to total purse-seine chub mackerel revenue climbed from 47% to 89% [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). This rational short-term fleet adaptation creates a hazardous third-order bioeconomic feedback loop: by concentrating 83.8% of winter effort on immature fish below Lm50L_{m50} (29.5 cm29.5\text{ cm}) right at the doorstep of Busan, the fishery intensifies growth overfishing, truncates the survivorship of cohorts before they can migrate into the KOTZ to become 400–500 g lipid-rich adults, and locks the domestic Korean market into structural dependence on USD 5.20/kg Norwegian imports [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full).

## Strategic Bioeconomic Conclusions and Governance Imperatives

The empirical synthesis of Pacific Rim chub mackerel markets and oceanographic dynamics demonstrates that aggregate biomass metrics must be decoupled from commercial value in contemporary fisheries management [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Traditional surplus production models—such as standard Schaefer, Fox, or ASPIC formulations that treat stock biomass BtB_t as a homogeneous commodity—fail to capture the economic reality of *Scomber japonicus*  [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). As evidenced in South Korea between 2020 and 2025, aggregate national landings can remain stable or expand (reaching 124,928 MT in 2024 with historical B/BMSYB/B_{\text{MSY}} ratios near 1.00–1.071.00–1.07) while the domestic table-fish market suffers a 51.5% retail price spike and a 93.2% import price surge because 97% of landed weight is trapped in the <30 cm<30\text{ cm} Grade 4 tier [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Bioeconomic target reference points (TRPs) must therefore transition to age-, length-, and lipid-structured dynamic optimization models that explicitly price cohort growth across the 30 cm (300 g300\text{ g}) and 14–18% lipid thresholds [1](https://globalmackerel.com/products/pacific-mackerel).

Furthermore, the 2022–2024 Northwest Pacific stock collapse (B/BMSY=0.51B/B_{\text{MSY}} = 0.51, with regional catches falling to 158,000 MT) proves that high-seas fishing effort cannot remain static when physical anomalies such as the 2018–2024 Kuroshio Large Meander and KOTZ warming depress recruitment and length-at-age [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276). While Japan's unilateral 60% TAC reduction for 2025 (to 139,000 MT) and the NPFC Technical Working Group on Chub Mackerel Stock Assessment (TWG CMSA) implementation of a unified State-space Stock Assessment Model (SAM) represent essential milestones, NPFC Conservation and Management Measures (such as CMM 2023-07) must evolve from non-binding effort monitoring and transshipment notifications into binding, climate-indexed transboundary TAC allocations across China, Japan, South Korea, and Russia [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2410-3888/11/5/276).

At the national operational level, when export markets for small pelagic grades (such as West African bulk demand or aquafeed reduction) combine with high fuel costs, fleets face perverse economic incentives to target nearshore juvenile overwintering corridors like the Southern East Sea [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full). Because consumer demand in East Asia is highly price-sensitive at the search and retail level (digital search volume for mackerel correlates negatively with market price at β=−0.211,p=0.01\beta = -0.211, p = 0.01, while institutional import demand remains inelastic), domestic food security requires targeted spatial-temporal effort controls [18](https://www.seoulz.com/korea-norway-mackerel/). Fisheries ministries—including MAFF in Japan, MOF and NIFS in South Korea, and PRODUCE and IMARPE in Peru—should pair aggregate TACs with dynamic spatial-temporal closures in juvenile overwintering blocks whenever precision port sampling indicates that sub-Lm50L_{m50} (<29.5 cm<29.5\text{ cm}) proportions exceed precautionary thresholds [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full).

Finally, commercial processors and international buyers must structure procurement and valorization strategies around anti-phased trans-Pacific environmental teleconnections [8](https://globalmackerel.com/insights/price-index). Securing consistent volumes of 300–500 g+ high-fat (>14–18% lipid) mackerel requires multi-origin portfolio hedging across Japanese Pacific coast ports (Choshi, Kesennuma), Korean autumn IQF production, counter-seasonal Q2 Australian *S. australasicus* , and Norwegian *S. scombrus*  contracts, increasingly assisted by AI-optimized purse-seine route planning systems (+0.4% global CAGR impact) [3](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/mackerel-market). Simultaneously, in regions experiencing chronic small-fish dominance or post-ENSO size compression, processors can mitigate the low 0.15 relative price ratio of small grades by adopting marine biorefinery protocols—such as enzymatic hydrolysis using Novozyme proteases to yield 123–154 g of dry fish protein hydrolysate (FPH) per kg of heads, tails, and skin-bone byproducts, alongside omega-3 DHA extraction—thereby capturing higher bioeconomic value from sub-30 cm harvests without expanding fishing mortality [9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1945408/full).
