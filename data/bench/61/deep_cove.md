# Chub Mackerel Across the Pacific Rim: Prices, Body Size and the Ocean–Fishery–Market Connection

**The strongest finding is market-specific.** Ocean conditions influence chub mackerel recruitment, growth, distribution and catchability, while size grading, fishing decisions, processing, trade and exchange rates determine how those biological changes reach prices. In Busan, the clearest species-specific market case, the smallest grade’s real auction price increased **55.6% between 2014 and 2025**, yet its price relative to the largest grade did not improve. Biological evidence also differs among stocks: Japanese Pacific-stock fish became lighter at age, Korean landed mean length fluctuated without a significant trend, and Chinese monitoring recorded both annual fluctuations and a recent increase in mean length.

**The evidence supports connected relationships.** Marine-heatwave frequency was negatively associated with Northwest Pacific catch per unit effort, and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation led Japanese catch positively by one year. These findings support an environment–resource–fishery–market interpretation, rather than a single Pacific-wide coefficient translating warming directly into seafood prices.

## 1. Species, stocks and the comparison window

### One species, several market names

**The canonical species is Scomber japonicus.** “Pacific chub mackerel,” NOAA’s “Pacific mackerel,” and the Chilean and Peruvian market name “caballa” refer to this species in the relevant records. Scientific names are the controlling identifier because common names can cross species boundaries: some North American assessments also use “blue mackerel” for *S. japonicus*, whereas Northwest Pacific studies normally use that name for *S. australasicus*. FishBase’s species account [7] and the North American assessment [8] establish these usages.

| Scientific taxon | Relevant market names | Treatment in this report |
|---|---|---|
| *Scomber japonicus* | Pacific chub mackerel, Pacific mackerel, caballa | Target species |
| *Scomber australasicus* | Blue or spotted mackerel | Separate species; flag combined records |
| *Scomber colias* | Atlantic chub mackerel | Separate from Pacific comparisons |
| *Scomber scombrus* | Atlantic mackerel | Substitute-market comparator |
| *Trachurus murphyi* | Chilean jack mackerel, jurel, Inca scad | Substitute and mixed-fishery comparator |

**Taxonomy changes economic interpretation.** A price increase for generic frozen mackerel can result from changing proportions of these species, not just higher prices for *S. japonicus*. Korean customs statistics, for example, combine chub and spotted mackerel under one commodity heading, while Chinese high-seas catches can contain both species. Species composition therefore belongs inside the interpretation of trade unit values, not merely in a biological footnote. [2], and NPFC documents mixed Chinese catches [3].

### Stock boundaries matter more than national flags

**The Northwest Pacific contains distinct stocks.** The Tsushima Warm Current stock occupies the East China Sea, Korean waters and waters west of Japan, including the Japan/East Sea. The Pacific stock occupies Japan’s Pacific coast and offshore waters toward the Kuril Islands and approximately 170°E. Korea’s Busan large-purse-seine case mainly concerns the former; the China–Japan–Russia NPFC assessment principally concerns the latter. [9].

| Biological comparison unit | Geographic boundary | Main national cases | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tsushima Warm Current stock | East China Sea–Korea Strait–Japan/East Sea | Korea, China, western Japan | Marginal-sea recruitment and migration |
| Northwest Pacific Pacific stock | Pacific Japan–Kuril offshore waters | Japan, China, Russia | Straddling coastal–high-seas stock |
| North American assessed sub-stock | Pacific coast north of Punta Abreojos | United States, Mexico | Transboundary management unit |
| Peruvian regional stock context | Peruvian Southeast Pacific waters | Peru | National production and assessment context |
| Chilean management context | Chilean waters, including northern regions | Chile | National management unit and regional fisheries |

**North America requires a narrower boundary.** The species’ overall range extends from southeastern Alaska to Mexico, but the U.S. assessment focuses on the population north of Punta Abreojos, harvested by U.S. and Mexican fisheries. Three spawning aggregations have been hypothesized—Gulf of California, Cabo San Lucas and the Pacific coast farther north—so the species range should not be equated with one demonstrated biological population. The approximately 70% distribution in U.S. waters is a management assumption, not an annual observation. [8].

**Southeast Pacific boundaries are also regional.** Seafood Watch describes historical hypotheses separating Central–North Peru from a population extending from northern Chile toward 40°S, with additional possible separation in Ecuador and central Chile. Chile manages chub mackerel as one national stock, but that management convention does not establish complete biological connectivity with Peru. These countries are consequently compared as regional fishery and product-destination cases. The Southeast Pacific stock discussion [6] supports this distinction.

For the 2000–2025 comparison, the Northwest Pacific units remain separate: Pacific and Tsushima Warm Current cohorts are distinct; the North American management unit extends from southeastern Alaska to Banderas Bay, Mexico, with stock structure unresolved; and Chile and Peru are treated as Southeast Pacific FAO Area 87 fishery contexts.

**The year labels are consequential.** Busan biological year 2025 runs from March 2025 through February 2026, whereas winter 2025 means December 2024–February 2025. ## 2. Price dynamics and market structure

### Busan: rising real prices without a small-fish premium

**Grade 4 prices rose substantially.** The smallest commercial grade increased from 1,120 to 1,743 KRW/kg in constant 2020 prices between biological years 2014 and 2025. The calculated increase is 1,743 ÷ 1,120 − 1 = **55.6%**, equivalent to approximately 4.1% annual compound growth over eleven intervals; the reported Mann–Kendall trend test gives p = 0.03. [2].

**Large fish retained higher unit values.** In 2024, nominal auction prices declined consistently from Grade 1 to Grade 4. The pooled biological measurements also declined in length and individual weight across those same grades, establishing a directly interpretable commercial size–price ordering.

| Busan grade | Mean FL, 2014–2025 | Mean individual weight | Nominal 2024 auction price | Principal market channel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grade 1 | 36.8 cm | 694 g | 9,752 KRW/kg | Domestic food |
| Grade 2 | 34.5 cm | 543 g | 6,503 KRW/kg | Domestic food |
| Grade 3 | 31.8 cm | 403 g | 3,576 KRW/kg | Mostly domestic food |
| Grade 4 | 27.6 cm | 251 g | 1,355 KRW/kg | Export, canning and feed |

The grade measurements, prices and channel definitions come from the Busan auction and NIFS sampling study [2].

**Relative prices answer the premium question.** Grade 4’s price relative to Grade 1 moved from approximately 0.16 to 0.15, within a record range of 0.12–0.25. Thus, small fish became more valuable in real currency terms without becoming more valuable relative to large fish. A “small-fish premium” would require a rising relative price after comparable product and quality controls; the observed ratio points to broad market appreciation instead. [2].

**Revenue growth reflected changing composition.** Grade 4 increased from 77% to 97% of landed weight and from 47% to 89% of chub-mackerel revenue. A lower-priced product can dominate revenue when its quantity becomes sufficiently large. This is commercially different from improving biological quality or obtaining a stronger price premium.

**Composition changes the aggregate-price story.** A useful conditional estimate follows from the accounting identity that a grade’s revenue share equals its quantity share multiplied by its price relative to the market average. Applying one annual deflator across grades gives estimated average real prices of 1,120 × 0.77 ÷ 0.47 ≈ **1,835 KRW/kg** in 2014 and 1,743 × 0.97 ÷ 0.89 ≈ **1,900 KRW/kg** in 2025—a rise of about 3.5%. These are estimates from rounded published shares, not a source-published aggregate index.

**The mechanism is a composition offset.** Grade-specific appreciation raised prices, while movement toward the cheapest grade pulled down the average price per kilogram. This explains why a 55.6% increase in the smallest grade’s real price need not produce comparable growth in average landed value. It also shows why an aggregate market price can obscure economically important changes in resource size structure.

**Market coverage is substantial.** Busan records covered 68.4% of national large-purse-seine chub-mackerel production during 2014–2025, ranging from 59.8% to 79.2% by year. Large purse seine supplied 98.9% of the chub-mackerel weight auctioned there. A worked volume anchor is approximately **50,000 t in 2022**, calculated from the study’s 402 t of SES landings divided by its rounded 0.8% annual share: 402 ÷ 0.008 = 50,250 t. [2].

**Seasonality operates through several channels.** Busan’s reconstruction distinguishes March–August feeding-season landings from September–February overwintering landings. Seasonal catch availability, fat accumulation and the domestic-versus-export destination can move prices in different directions: abundant winter supply may depress a grade’s price, while higher fat content can increase its food-market appeal. The published annual real-price trend should therefore be interpreted separately from monthly seasonal price movements.

### Tokyo: a wholesale system, not an interchangeable Busan price

**Tokyo uses quantity-weighted wholesale prices.** J-Fish defines price as total sales value divided by total quantity, drawing on Tokyo Metropolitan Central Wholesale Market statistics. Its continuous series uses Tsukiji through September 2018 and Toyosu from October 2018; quantities are stored in kilograms and charted in metric tonnes. Shipping origin denotes the reported dispatch origin, which can differ from production country. [12].

**Wholesale composition needs its own controls.** A Tokyo average can change because of different fish sizes, forms, shipping origins or transaction volumes, even if individual product prices remain stable. Its official statistical system separates annual and monthly average prices, species quantities and market-by-wholesaler volumes. Those definitions make Tokyo an important wholesale comparison case, but not a substitute for Busan’s explicitly grade-resolved auction series. [13].

**Low-volume observations deserve different weight.** J-Fish’s five-year seasonal benchmark gives each eligible year’s monthly weighted price equal weight. Its low-volume warning requires quantity below 10% of the corresponding month’s historical median and no more than 50 kg, using at least three preceding observations. That rule illustrates why a thin, high-priced consignment should not be interpreted as a broad market shift. [12].

**Endpoint arithmetic clarifies the contrast.** Calculated from the printed tonnages, China’s imports rose 14.16% in 2025, Japan’s fell 14.34%, Korea’s rose 35.91%, and Taiwan’s rose 6.66%. These calculations use the quantity columns directly, rather than reproducing the report’s percentage-change column.

**Volume variability is not price volatility.** China’s five annual quantities span 64,395–109,494 t, a maximum/minimum ratio of 1.70; Japan’s span 68,996–92,266 t, a ratio of 1.34. China’s 2021–2025 endpoint decline was 4.3%, compared with Japan’s 23.6%. These measures characterize trade-volume variability and exposure to imports, not the standard deviation of species-specific price changes.

**Imports can buffer local biological shocks.** A country can maintain consumption despite a weak domestic year class by importing alternative origins or species. Conversely, processors may import raw material for re-export, so higher imports do not necessarily mean greater final domestic consumption. FAO describes precisely such international processing and substitution channels in the mackerel trade. [5].

### American and Russian cases: landing value, production and destination

**The U.S. anchor is ex-vessel value.** NOAA reports 3.3 million lb of commercial Pacific mackerel landings worth USD 800,000 in 2024. The calculated landed-value ratio is USD 800,000 ÷ 3,300,000 lb = **USD 0.242/lb**, or approximately **USD 0.53/kg**; the rounded weight converts to about 1,497 t. This is current-year aggregate landed value per unit weight, not a retail, wholesale or export quotation. [4].

**Mexico belongs through the shared fishery.** Ensenada and Magdalena Bay appear in the North American landing compilation, which totals 16,000 t across U.S. and Mexican components in 2022. An accounting bound for the Mexican component is **0–16,000 t**, obtained by placing a nonnegative component within the published combined total; it is a bound, not an estimate of Mexico’s likely share. The assessment treats the stock jointly rather than distributing catch according to the 70% U.S.-waters biomass assumption. [10].

**Chile’s dominant outlet is reduction.** The two listed 2021 fisheries supplied 67,422 t and 4,889 t of chub mackerel, totaling **72,311 t**. Separately, 97.74% of Chilean chub mackerel in 2023 entered fishmeal or fish-oil production; the calculated remainder was 2.26%, mainly frozen or refrigerated. The 2021 quantities and 2023 processing proportions describe different years and should remain separate observations. [6].

For the 2021 assessment and 2023 processing record, Chile’s *Scomber japonicus* case covers Regions XV, I and II of the Chilean exclusive economic zone in FAO Area 87, using purse-seine and pelagic-trawl fisheries. SERNAPESCA tables organize landings by species, month, region and port and production by species, region, port and processing line, making the comparison a regional fishery and port-provenance case rather than a named city-market observation.

**Peru represents a different market destination.** Its artisanal/small-scale purse-seine caballa fishery averaged **73,361 t/year during 2020–2022** and is identified with direct human consumption. The often-cited figure that only around 2% of Peruvian small-pelagic landings enter direct human consumption describes the broader, anchoveta-dominated system; it should not be assigned to this caballa fishery. [6].

**Russia’s production trajectory is highly variable.** The fleet caught about 87,000 t in 2021, while the 2025 NPFC meeting reported 7,200 t for 2024. Russian 2024 catches were concentrated in winter, with summer and early-autumn CPUE below 5 t per vessel-day and an increase beginning in November. These observations characterize fishery supply and seasonal access, not a Vladivostok wholesale-price trajectory. [3].

**Canada remains a contextual comparison.** Fisheries and Oceans Canada identifies trade tables containing quantity in kilograms and value in Canadian dollars from 1989 onward, classified by market, country, product and species. NPFC also records Canadian domestic bycatch and survey-capture information. This combination justifies keeping Canada in the geographic screen without assigning it a major-market rank from the existence of a statistical system alone. [11], and NPFC records the bycatch information [3].

### Retail and international substitution add another price layer

**Korean retail prices confirm broader inflation.** FAO reports that a domestic Pacific-mackerel retail item cost 4,690 KRW per fish in December 2025, up 16.9% year-on-year, versus 5,180 KRW for imported Atlantic mackerel, up 28.8%. The imported item was approximately 10.4% more expensive per fish, calculated as 5,180 ÷ 4,690 − 1; the comparison remains per item because matched individual weights are not specified. [5].

**The 2026 snapshot shows substitution incentives.** In March 2026, FAO placed Pacific chub wholesale prices near USD 1.85/kg, Chilean jack wholesale prices at USD 1.25–2.51/kg, and Norwegian Atlantic export prices above approximately USD 4.97/kg for frozen whole fish below 600 g. ### Japan: strong evidence of reduced weight-at-age

**Japanese age-specific weights declined.** In 2019, weights at ages 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6+ were the lowest since 1970 in the Japanese Pacific-stock record; ages 0 and 1 also remained low relative to other years. This is stronger evidence of altered growth or condition than a change in the average length of a mixed-age commercial catch. [16].

**Sampling intensity supports the result.** The Japanese protocol measured approximately 60,000 fork lengths annually across 19 Pacific-coast prefectures. Its 2019 example contained 65,122 length measurements, 13,728 individual weights and 3,915 age observations. Fork length was measured to 1 mm and weight to 0.1 g, with samples aggregated by prefecture, month and gear. [16].

**Regional allometry changes inferred weight.** Japanese relationships differ among prefectures, even within the Pacific-stock sampling system. Applying the reported 2018 fits at the same 30 cm fork length illustrates the practical consequence.

Differences in body shape, season, feeding history and sampling composition can therefore alter inferred biomass or grade assignment. A length-only comparison does not fully measure individual resource quality.

**Density offers a competing explanation.** NPFC’s species summary links recent lower weight-at-age partly to feeding competition associated with increased chub-mackerel and Japanese-sardine density. Thus, lighter fish can occur during periods of high abundance, not only during environmentally driven depletion. Distinguishing food competition from thermal stress requires age-specific condition and prey information, rather than interpreting low weight as a direct biomass index. [17].

**The joint assessment extends the pattern.** NPFC’s assessment uses seven age groups—0–5 and 6+—and annual weight-at-age for FY2014–FY2023. Its biological inputs became lighter after the strong 2013 year class, particularly above age 0, with some recovery at age 4+. This connects individual growth characteristics to reproductive biomass because spawning biomass depends jointly on fish numbers, weight and maturity. [18].

### Korea: commercial composition is not population size-at-age

**Korean landed length fluctuated modestly.** Busan’s reconstructed catch mean fork length ranged from 26.4 to 29.4 cm over biological years 2014–2025, changing from 28.7 to 27.9 cm with p = 0.24. The share below the estimated 29.5 cm length at 50% maturity ranged from 48% to 85%, changing from 66% to 78% with p = 0.15. The smallest mean and largest below-maturity share occurred in 2022, not in the record-Grade-4 year of 2025. [2].

**The reconstruction corrects unequal fish numbers.** NIFS precision sampling used approximately one box per grade per month, equivalent to about 48 grade–month sampling cells in a complete annual design. Auction mass supplies grade prevalence, while individual measurements supply within-grade weights and lengths. Dividing grade mass by mean individual mass converts landings into estimated fish numbers:

\[
\widehat N_{g,t}=Q_{g,t}/\overline W_{g,t},\qquad \widehat{\overline L}_t=\frac{\sum_g\widehat N_{g,t}\overline L_{g,t}}{\sum_g\widehat N_{g,t}}.
\]

**Weight and number shares differ.** A kilogram of average Grade 4 fish contains approximately 1 ÷ 0.251 = 3.98 fish, compared with 1 ÷ 0.694 = 1.44 Grade 1 fish. Treating equally sampled boxes as representative of the catch would therefore distort population-style length composition. The published approach reconstructs a numbers-weighted length distribution from grade-specific landing masses. [2].

**Maturity estimates provide biological context.** The study’s spawning-season female sample comprised 2,373 fish. Its logistic maturity model estimated Lm50 at **29.5 cm FL**, with a bootstrap 95% interval of 29.2–29.8 cm. Reconstructed annual mean-length intervals were typically about ±0.2 cm, suggesting that year-to-year variability and the short twelve-point series, rather than broad reconstruction error, explain the weak trend result. [2].

**Seasonal changes favor a fishing-ground explanation.** Comparing 2014–2016 with 2023–2025, feeding-season mean FL changed from 28.9 to 28.3 cm, whereas overwintering mean FL changed from 29.4 to 27.7 cm. The seasonal reductions were −0.6 and −1.7 cm respectively. Neither seasonal trend was significant, but the stronger winter change is consistent with a shift in where the fleet obtains fish rather than uniform stock-wide shrinking. [2].

**Spatial sorting supplies the strongest contrast.** During winter 2025, Grade 4 constituted 97.0% of SES landings versus 75.3% outside SES, based on 4,865 transactions from 21 vessels. Yet within Grade 4, mean fork length was slightly larger in SES—28.1 versus 27.6 cm. The “smaller catch” therefore arose from grade composition, not from smaller individuals within that grade. [2].

**Korean weight changes were strongly seasonal.** Shim and colleagues’ March 2012–February 2013 Busan market sampling found monthly mean total length and weight lowest in July and highest in September among the reported endpoints.

**Fat varied more than size alone.** Crude lipid content ranged from 4.99 g/100 g in March to 19.27 g/100 g in December, nearly a 3.9-fold difference. Relationships involving total length, body weight and lipid content had reported r² values of 0.71–0.81 during September–December. Such variation can materially change food quality or processing suitability without requiring an equivalent change in length. [19].

**Japanese condition showed similar seasonality.** A separate study measured 2,085 Pacific-stock fish of 21–42 cm FL from northeastern Japanese purse-seine catches during 2012–2017. Fat content and condition factor were high from September through January and declined in February; GAMs controlling for capture date and length also identified significant year effects, including positive fat effects in 2016–2017. [20].

**Condition is weight relative to length.** A conventional condition index scales weight by length cubed, while an allometry-adjusted index compares observed weight with the expected \(aL^b\). Where b differs from three, the latter avoids treating larger fish as inherently better-conditioned. Neither index is interchangeable with measured lipid concentration, gonad development or freshness.

**Freshness is a separate market attribute.** Commercial size grade identifies sorting and destination, not storage history or food safety. The shorter distance from Busan to SES could plausibly affect travel costs and time to landing, but the observations do not measure a corresponding freshness premium. Biological quality should therefore remain a set of distinguishable attributes: size, condition, fat, reproductive state and post-capture handling.

## 4. Oceanographic mechanisms and their statistical support

### Temperature has life-stage-specific effects

**There is no single thermal optimum.** Northwest Pacific spawning suitability is generally described as 15–22°C, with an assumed optimum near 18°C. A Japanese age-0 survey model placed peak encounter probability near 17.5°C, while a Chinese mixed-species analysis placed the chub-mackerel relative-abundance peak around 17–19°C. These refer to spawning habitat, survey encounter probability and relative catch composition respectively. [24][25], and mixed-species habitat analysis [26] measure different endpoints.

**Encounter and catch magnitude also differ.** In the Japanese VAST survey model, positive-catch probability peaked around 17.5°C, but overall CPUE was highest above 20°C. The model explained 57.5% of deviance and treated environmental effects as catchability covariates. A temperature associated with finding fish more often need not be the temperature associated with the largest catches when fish are encountered. [25].

**Early-life growth provides a direct pathway.** Kamimura and colleagues analysed 532 otoliths from juveniles collected in May–June during 2002–2011 in the Kuroshio–Oyashio transition region. April-cohort mean daily growth ranged from 1.03 ± 0.10 mm/day in 2006 to 1.52 ± 0.23 mm/day in 2009. Higher experienced SST was significantly associated with faster larval growth and shorter larval duration, while these growth characteristics were related to recruitment. [27].

**Faster larvae do not imply larger adults.** Warmer conditions can shorten a vulnerable early-life stage, yet later growth depends on prey, density, metabolic expenditure and migration. A strong surviving cohort can subsequently experience food competition, producing many lighter fish. Temperature can therefore have favorable recruitment associations and unfavorable adult-condition associations within the same stock history.

**Large-scale averages can conceal exposure.** NPFC’s 1995–2022 spatial analysis found little significant SST–catch association across most mapped areas, whereas selected sea-level-anomaly and near-surface productivity fields showed positive relationships. This does not negate individual thermal responses; it indicates that broad SST averages may poorly represent the location, life stage or transport process controlling a particular year’s catch. [28].

### Productivity, currents, fronts and upwelling

| Driver | Observed or theoretical pathway | Principal spatial scale | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| SST | Spawning suitability, development, growth and catchability | Spawning grounds to survey stations | Days to seasons; cohort effects later |
| Chlorophyll-a | Productivity proxy affecting prey and aggregation | Chinese 0.5° fishing cells; frontal zones | Monthly, season-dependent |

| Fronts | Concentration and separation of water masses and prey | Kuroshio frontal fishing grounds | Event to seasonal |

The spatial mechanisms draw on the Chinese chlorophyll analysis [29], Korean CPUE and current study [30], coupled early-life model [31], and Humboldt-system synthesis [6].

**Chlorophyll changes sign with season.** The Chinese 2014–2020 high-seas study evaluated 256 mixed-model candidates using monthly fishing and environmental data. SST had a significant negative fixed association with CPUE, but log chlorophyll had no single fixed effect. Allowing its slope to vary by month improved fit significantly, with positive associations in April–May and November–December and negative associations during June–October. [29].

**The sign change has an ecological explanation.** Chlorophyll measures phytoplankton, whereas chub mackerel often respond to zooplankton and fish prey downstream in the food web. Fish can aggregate along fronts displaced from the highest chlorophyll, or arrive after a bloom has been consumed. Consequently, a negative contemporaneous chlorophyll–CPUE relationship need not mean that primary production harms fish; it may reflect timing and spatial aggregation.

**Currents connect habitats across jurisdictions.** The Tsushima Warm Current transports warm water and larvae northeastward from the East China Sea through the Korea Strait. The Korean Strait Bottom Cold Current supplies water below 10°C with salinity around 34–34.4‰ along the Korean east coast. Their interaction changes thermal habitat, prey fields and the location of fishing grounds. [30].

**Fronts influence catchability as well as feeding.** A front can concentrate fish into a fishable area without increasing total stock biomass. This distinction is essential for purse-seine fisheries, whose success depends on locating dense schools. Changes in frontal position can raise or lower local CPUE and sailing distance while leaving the basin-wide number of fish comparatively unchanged.

**Upwelling creates competing processes.** The Humboldt Current system supplies nutrient-rich coastal waters supporting productive food webs; northern Chile experiences persistent coastal upwelling, while central Chile has important seasonal wind-driven upwelling. As oceanographic theory, stronger nutrient delivery can improve feeding, but strong offshore transport or poorly timed cold-water exposure can reduce larval retention or habitat suitability. The relevant judgment is therefore conditional on transport, prey timing and life stage, rather than “more upwelling means more mackerel.” [6].

### Heatwaves and oxygen distinguish stress from mean warming

**Heatwave associations are comparatively strong.** A study matching Chinese fishing logbooks to Northwest Pacific SST during June–November 2014–2021 found marine-heatwave frequency negatively correlated with CPUE: r = −0.72, p < 0.001. Its GAM included intensity, duration, year, month, longitude and latitude, explaining 33.9% of deviance, with adjusted R² = 0.336. The intensity smooth was significant and nonlinear, with edf = 6.49 and F = 15.74. [34].

**Event definitions preserve comparability.** Heatwaves were periods of at least five consecutive days above the local seasonal 90th-percentile SST threshold, based on a 1982–2011 climatology. Intensity reached about 3.5–4.5°C in 2016; local duration reached 80 days in 2019 and exceeded 90 days centrally in 2020–2021. These are anomalies relative to local seasonal conditions, not absolute habitat temperatures. [34].

**Individual event contrasts were less decisive.** The reported before-versus-during paired comparison had p = 0.0511, just above 0.05, while during-versus-after had p = 0.0398. Some regional catches rebounded after events and others remained depressed. That mixture is consistent with temporary displacement and catchability changes alongside possible longer biological effects; it does not isolate mortality. [34].

**Oxygen belongs in the energy mechanism.** Growth depends on assimilated food energy minus maintenance, swimming and reproductive expenditure. Warmer water changes metabolic demand, while oxygen exposure can constrain aerobic activity and usable habitat. Guo and colleagues’ California Current bioenergetic work measured swimming and oxygen consumption in fish of 14–33 cm, but those physiological parameters are different quantities from an environmental dissolved-oxygen effect on field growth or market price. [35].

**Section judgment.** Oceanographic theory explains plausible pathways, and several stock-specific associations are strong; the direction reaching markets nevertheless depends on life stage, location, lag, prey supply and whether the immediate response is abundance or catchability.

## 5. Fishery economics: supply, trade, effort and management

### Catch-dependent prices support an inverse supply relationship

**Korean fitted prices decline with catch.** A monthly study covering July 2017–June 2022 fitted three catch-dependent price functions using catch in tonnes and price in thousand KRW/t. All fitted parameters had p < 0.05, with the highest R² for the function the authors termed “irrational.”

| Fitted price function | R² | Monthly catch below which fitted price exceeded average | Comparative behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Logarithmic | 0.724 | About 13,000 t | Steeper decline above roughly 15,000 t |
| Rational | 0.757 | About 16,600 t | Less sensitive at high catches |
| “Irrational” | 0.769 | About 14,600 t | Highest reported in-sample fit |

The functions and thresholds are reported in the Korean catch-dependent pricing study [36].

**Supply is informative but not sufficient.** These fits support the proposition that higher monthly landings can reduce price, holding the modeled market setting fixed. They do not show that catch alone determines price: demand variation was omitted and effort cost was treated as fixed. Seasonal sorting, substitute availability and export orders can shift the entire price–catch relationship.

**Revenue depends on price responsiveness.** For a single product, revenue is \(V=PQ\). Its response to supply follows the identity:

\[
\frac{d\ln V}{d\ln Q}=1+\frac{d\ln P}{d\ln Q}.
\]

If the proportional price decline is smaller than the proportional quantity increase, revenue rises; if larger, revenue falls. This explains why catch stabilization, income stabilization and stock rebuilding can require different policies.

**Price assumptions change fishing incentives.** The Korean study’s ten-year optimization simulations produced different seasonal effort schedules across the three price functions. The logarithmic case yielded modeled monthly-average catch of 12,424 t, profit of 15.35 billion KRW and biomass of 189,967 t; rational pricing produced the highest reported average profit, 16.51 billion KRW. These are published model scenarios, illustrating sensitivity to market assumptions rather than forecasts of realized catches or earnings. [36].

### Effort standardization changes the supply signal

**CPUE depends on fishing capability.** In its simplest interpretation, CPUE equals biomass multiplied by catchability. Improved electronics, vessel capacity, targeting and aggregation can increase catchability, allowing nominal CPUE to remain stable while biomass declines. Korean studies accordingly use vessel tonnage, horsepower, AIS, logbooks and environmental covariates rather than vessel counts alone. [38].

**Standardized trends can reverse nominal impressions.** A Korean GLMM using year, month, area, 50-m temperature, Tsushima transport and the mackerel catch ratio found nominal CPUE increasing across areas while standardized CPUE decreased or increased more slowly. Another AIS–logbook GAM explained 69.9% of nominal CPUE variation, with month, year and SST contributing 50.6 percentage points. The GLMM comparison [39] and AIS–logbook study [30] show why nominal catch rates need interpretation.

Across the 2010–2024 comparison window, Chinese high-seas CPUE fell from 15.501 t/net in 2015 to 8.355 t/net in 2020, while Japanese age-0 VAST CPUE was 2,665.93 ind/h in 2018 and 153.54 ind/h in 2023. Korean GAM and GLMM analyses standardize CPUE with environmental and spatiotemporal covariates, so these are distinct nominal, age-specific and standardized indices rather than one pooled Pacific-wide series.

**Technology assumptions influence biomass conclusions.** The 2026 Korean assessment cumulatively corrected CPUE downward by 2% annually for technical creep and estimated 2024 B/BMSY at 0.63 under CMSY++ and 0.59 under its Bayesian state-space model. Those values describe a modeled stock component under stated effort assumptions, not a direct interpretation of higher market prices. [38].

**Section judgment.** Supply affects price, but trade outlets, currencies, fuel costs, substitutes and fleet capability can strengthen, buffer or reverse the signal transmitted from biological abundance to market value.

### Environment-to-resource and resource-to-market links

**Several links have explicit statistical support.** Their responses, spatial scales and controls differ, so they should be compared by the question they answer rather than by ranking p-values or R² values.

|---|---|---|---|
| Heatwave frequency–CPUE | r = −0.72; p < 0.001 | 2014–2021; contemporaneous | Adverse fishery association |

**Correlations are not interchangeable slopes.** An r of −0.72 is not a 72% decline in catch per degree, and an R² of 0.769 is not a price elasticity. Nor can a climate–catch correlation be multiplied by a catch–price correlation to obtain an environment–price effect. Each statistic depends on its own sample, transformation, controls and response definition.

**The grade data permit a rank calculation.** Across the four published Busan grade means, weight and 2024 price have identical rank order, giving a calculated Spearman ρ = 1 for four grade summaries. Under an exchangeable-price-ranks null, two of the 4! = 24 permutations produce |ρ| = 1, yielding a two-sided exact p of 0.083. This is a transparent aggregate ordering check, not a fish-level hedonic-price estimate or an independent validation of the grading system.

**Biological quality has multiple economic routes.** Larger fish can enter higher-value domestic food channels; heavier fish at a given length can alter saleable yield; higher fat can affect consumer appeal or oil recovery; and handling determines freshness. Busan directly quantifies the size-grade channel, while the Japanese and Korean composition studies quantify condition and fat. Their combination supports mechanisms, but not the assumption that all quality attributes receive the same premium.

## 8. Appendix: measurement, calculations and comparability

### Common measurement rules

**Prices retain their market identity.** Auction, wholesale, ex-vessel, retail and border unit values describe different transactions. The general annual unit-value calculation is total value divided by total quantity, not an unweighted average of monthly quotations. A species-specific price comparison also holds product form, size grade and origin constant where those attributes define the product.

| Measurement issue | Rule applied here | Source or example |
|---|---|---|
| Taxon | Use *S. japonicus*; retain combined-species labels | FishBase; NPFC; Korean customs |
| Stock | Keep Pacific, Tsushima and North American units separate | NPFC and PFMC assessments |
| Product and market level | Separate auction, wholesale, landing, retail and trade | Busan, Tokyo, NOAA and FAO |
| Inflation | Preserve the source deflator and base year | Busan CPI; Korean assessment PPI |
| Currency | Keep local currency primary; convert only with explicit rates | Bank of Korea endpoints |
| Length | Preserve FL, TL or reported-length designation | NIFS, Japanese and U.S. sampling |
| Weight | Distinguish individual g from aggregate landed t | Biological samples versus production |
| Time | Label calendar, biological, fishing and winter years | March–February versus July–June |

**Deflation and conversion answer different questions.** A local real-price series measures purchasing-power change within that economy. A dollar-converted nominal series measures value at an exchange rate, which can move independently of fish quality. For a nominal local price \(P_t\), a base-year real price is \(P_t I_b/I_t\); a dollar price is \(P_t/E_t\) when \(E_t\) is local currency per dollar. Applying both requires compatible dates and a clearly stated interpretation.

**Dispersion measures remain distinct.** A length range describes observed extremes, an SD describes spread around a sample mean, a confidence interval describes uncertainty in an estimate, and cross-model disagreement describes structural sensitivity. They are not substitutes. Likewise, annual price volatility conventionally refers to dispersion in price returns after defining frequency and seasonal treatment; the import ranges and relative-grade-price range reported here retain their narrower meanings.

### Sampling and sensitivity records

**Sampling design governs representativeness.** Japanese port spill sampling, Korean grade-stratified boxes, Chinese commercial specimens, Russian observer collections and U.S. acoustic-trawl surveys observe different selections of fish. CDFW’s stated port targets were 25 fish per landing boat and twelve samples per month per port during the season. Those design targets should not be interpreted as achieved annual sample counts. [21].

**Age and maturity require particular care.** U.S. reader agreement was 100% at ages 0–2 but fell to 70% at age 5 in the tested otolith set. NPFC also documented regional differences in maturity assessment: Japan reviewed a gonad-index threshold against GSI, while China used visual stages. A change in maturity criterion can change estimated reproductive biomass even with identical fish counts and weights. [21], and NPFC describes the maturity comparisons [3].

**Published sensitivity tests bound interpretation.** The main checks include five competing Northwest Pacific stock models, alternative abundance-index configurations, Korean detrending and first differences, technical-creep corrections, alternative maturity thresholds and the early-life model’s biological perturbations. These tests support conclusions that survive reasonable alternatives—such as heterogeneous size dynamics and the distinction between grade price and revenue share—more strongly than a single preferred model’s exact biomass or recovery date.

**Evidence boundaries are concentrated here.** The comparisons retain species aggregation, different stock footprints, market levels, product forms, deflators, length definitions and sampling strata; the Chinese 2016–2021 source mixes standard/fork-length labels and inconsistent fitted sample counts, and the Korean seasonal paper’s July count differs from its stated monthly design. Tokyo’s early records include paper reconstructions, NPFC uses specified age–length-key substitutions, Chinese chlorophyll analysis covers April–December, and North American assessments omit specified survey observations. Country-level production and metadata remain at those levels; calculated unit values, fitted masses, accounting bounds, reconstructed aggregate prices and harvest-rule quantities are labelled estimates. A direct environment–price conclusion would be decided by synchronized species-specific transactions and biological measurements, matched environmental lags, and controls for catch, CPUE, biomass, effort, quotas, fuel, currencies, inventories, substitutes and demand.

**The overall judgment is clear.** Chub mackerel markets are useful observations of how marine resources are selected, processed and valued, but price alone is not a stock-health indicator. The most defensible interdisciplinary account combines age-specific biology and ocean exposure with grade-resolved prices, product destinations and fleet behavior: environmental change shapes the resource, and markets determine which part of that resource becomes commercially visible.
