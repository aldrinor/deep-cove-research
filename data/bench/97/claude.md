# Can a 500,000-Passenger Airport Generate Significant, Measurable Regional Socioeconomic Impact? An Evidence Review

**Bottom line: The hypothesis is conditionally supported. A 500,000-passenger airport reliably produces *measurable* gross economic activity, typically on the order of 400–600 direct on-site jobs and roughly 1,000–3,000 total "supported" jobs once supply-chain, induced and visitor-spending effects are added. Whether that activity is *significant* and *causal* (net new to the region rather than relocated or a symptom of growth) depends heavily on peripherality, the absence of nearby alternative airports, tourism inflows, and the quality of the route network. The strongest causal studies find little or no growth effect from small airports in well-connected core regions, but real effects in remote and peripheral regions.**

## TL;DR

- **Measurable: yes.** Published impact studies for airports at or near this size consistently report gross footprints of roughly 700–3,000 jobs and tens to hundreds of millions in output or GVA. Examples are Bellingham (WA) at about 725k passengers with 2,940 total jobs, Inverness at 875k with 748 on-site FTE plus 1,774 visitor-spend FTE, and Cornwall Newquay at 440k with about £72m GVA. ACI Europe's benchmark of 1.2 direct jobs per 1,000 passengers at airports under 1 million passengers implies about 600 direct jobs at 500k.
- **Significant and causal: only under conditions.** Quasi-experimental studies find no detectable growth spillovers from small airports in Germany (Breidenbach 2020) and Norway (Tveter 2017; Rattsø & Sheard 2025, for small and STOL airports). Pot & Koster (2022) find that for small European airports, accessibility follows growth rather than causing it. Causal effects do appear in peripheral regions (Mukkala & Tervo 2013), for airports more than 100 km from an existing airport (Rattsø & Sheard 2025), and in tourism (Memmingen, Doerr et al. 2020).
- **Cost side matters.** EU rules explicitly recognise that airports under 700,000 passengers struggle to cover operating costs. The European Court of Auditors found that half of the EU-funded airports it audited could not demonstrate need. Cornwall Newquay, a textbook 500k airport, runs underlying trading losses of more than £4m a year. In August 2026 Cornwall Council's cabinet member said taxpayers "cannot be expected to find anything like £9-£10 million a year" to run it, as reported by The Post/Newquay Voice. "Significant" must therefore be judged net of subsidy and displacement, not on gross advocacy-study headlines.

## Key Findings

1. **Classification.** In the US, 500,000 total passengers is about 250,000 enplanements, which places an airport in the FAA "nonhub primary" category (more than 10,000 enplanements but under 0.05% of national enplanements).\[1\] The FAA Terminal Area Forecast summary gives 970.2 million US enplanements in 2025, so the small-hub threshold is about 485,000 enplanements, or roughly 970,000 total passengers. In the EU, such an airport falls within the 2014 Aviation Guidelines' most-favoured operating-aid band (up to 700,000 passengers a year). It is also inside the "under 1 million" investment-aid band, where aid intensity can reach 75%.\[2\]
2. **Gross impact is real and quantifiable.** Every airport-level study reviewed reports hundreds of direct jobs and a larger total footprint. Visitor spending is usually the single largest component for tourism-oriented regional airports.
3. **Gross is not net.** The input-output studies that produce headline numbers do not measure displacement or substitution. Nor do they measure the counterfactual of travellers driving to a neighbouring airport. Causal econometric studies of small airports in dense, well-connected regions mostly find effects that cannot be distinguished from zero.\[3\]
4. **Peripherality is the key moderator.** The best-identified evidence finds that small airports matter where surface alternatives are poor and the next airport is far away.\[4\] Examples are northern Norway, the Scottish Highlands, regional Australia and peripheral EU regions.
5. **Financial viability is the weak point.** At around 500k passengers most airports need public operating support.\[5\] This cost belongs in any judgement of "significance".

**What these airports serve:** Communities of roughly 100,000–500,000 people, often peripheral or tourism-dependent. Service is typically a mix of hub-feeder flights (e.g., Inverness–Heathrow, Kerry–Dublin), seasonal low-cost and leisure routes, general aviation, and sometimes military or cargo activity. That mix inflates or deflates impact figures. I found no authoritative global count of airports in the 300k–800k band. Given the FAA's count of 386 US non-hub airports and about 500 European regional airports (most under 1 million passengers), the worldwide band plausibly numbers in the several hundreds. That is my inference, not a sourced figure.

**What the table means:**
- **Totals per 1,000 passengers vary about fourfold**, from about 0.4 total jobs per 100 passengers at Bellingham to about 1.5 at Duluth. The spread reflects what each study counts as "airport-related" (manufacturers, military, FedEx, business parks), not passenger volume. Passenger throughput alone is a poor predictor of measured impact.
- **Visitor spending dominates at tourism airports.** At Inverness, visitor-spend jobs (1,774 FTE) are more than twice the on-site footprint (748 FTE).\[15\] At Knock, €182m of regional visitor spend dwarfs the €17m airport GVA.\[11\]\[29\] That is where the money is, and also where attribution is weakest, because some of those visitors would have come via Dublin, Shannon or Glasgow anyway.
- **Kerry Airport** illustrates the attribution problem. The airport cites 1.3 million overseas visitors spending €337m in County Kerry (2017, Tourism Ireland), but the airport handled only about 340k passengers (171,140 in H1 2019).\[30\] Most of that tourism arrives through other gateways. Such county tourism figures should not be read as airport impact.

## 3. Academic and Causal Evidence

| Study | Setting / method | Finding relevant to ~500k airports |
|---|---|---|
| **Mukkala & Tervo (2013)**, *Environment & Planning A* | 86 regions, 13 European countries, 1991–2010; panel Granger causality | Causality from air traffic to regional growth is **significant in peripheral regions, indicative in intermediate regions, absent in core regions**.\[31\] Causality from growth to air traffic is homogeneous across regions.\[4\] |
| **Pot & Koster (2022)**, *J. Transport Geography* | European regions; Granger causality by airport size, accounting for overlapping catchments | For small airports, "accessibility provided by these airports is derived from economic growth rather than the other way around". The economic damage of closing a small airport is "likely limited". In 2018, 19% of the population covered got air accessibility mainly from medium and small airports, and 3% mainly from small ones.\[32\]\[33\] |
| **Breidenbach (2020)**, *Regional Studies* | 24 German regional airports; difference-in-differences using EU deregulation as an exogenous shock | **No evidence of spillover effects** on regional growth from regional airport expansions. Breidenbach also tested each airport separately.\[3\]\[34\]\[35\] |
| **Tveter (2017)**, *Research in Transportation Economics* | 9 Norwegian regional airports built 1970–72; difference-in-differences | Point estimate about +5% population growth near new airports over 1970–80, but **not statistically significant**.\[36\]\[37\]\[38\] |
| **Rattsø & Sheard (2025)**, *J. Economic Geography* | Norwegian network expansion, 1950–2019; difference-in-differences\[39\]\[40\] | Airports opened **more than 100 km from an existing airport** raise regional population growth (significant from about year 4). Those within 100 km have no significant effect, and STOL or small regional airports show no significant effect. A link to a major city increases the effect. "A small airport with little air traffic capacity and few connections has little effect on regional development."\[39\] |
| **Baker, Merkert & Kamruzzaman (2015)**, *J. Transport Geography* | 88 Australian regional airports, 1985/86–2010/11; cointegration and causality | **Bidirectional short- and long-run causality** between regional aviation (passenger movements) and regional income. The authors support targeted funding of regional airports.\[41\]\[42\] |
| **Sheard (2014)**, *J. Urban Economics* | US metro areas; instrumental variables | Airport size raises **tradable-services employment** (elasticity about 0.22), but the effect on overall metro employment is "practically zero". Airports change specialisation rather than total growth.\[43\] |
| **Sheard (2019)**, *Economica* | US metros; instrumental variables | Airport size has a positive effect on local employment and GDP, concentrated in services and localised.\[44\]\[45\] |
| **Blonigen & Cristea (2015)**, *J. Urban Economics* | US 1978 deregulation as a quasi-natural experiment | Air service raises local growth. The effect persists, but is **smaller**, when large hubs are excluded.\[46\] |
| **Brueckner (2003); Green (2007)** | US metro cross-sections | Airline traffic is associated with service-sector employment and population growth. Both studied larger metros, mainly hubs.\[45\]\[47\] |
| **Tsui et al. (2019)** (via 2026 review) | Six smaller New Zealand regional airports | A 1% rise in airline capacity is associated with a 0.07% rise in regional visitor numbers.\[38\] |
| **Doerr, Dorn, Gaebler & Potrafke (2020)**, *Regional Studies* | Memmingen Airport (opened 2007, LCC-focused); synthetic control | Statistically robust increases in tourist arrivals and overnight stays in Allgäu over 2008–16, concentrated in arrivals from abroad. A survey found 21% of respondents saw improved business connections.\[48\]\[49\] |

**Synthesis of the causal literature:**
- **Thresholds.** No paper sets a hard passenger cutoff. The Norwegian evidence (Rattsø & Sheard) and Pot & Koster both imply that effects grow with airport size and network connectivity, and that small airports with thin, non-hub-linked networks rarely show detectable growth effects.\[32\]\[39\] A 500k airport sits in the zone where effects are plausible but not guaranteed.
- **Reverse causality is real.** Both Mukkala & Tervo and Pot & Koster find growth driving air traffic.\[32\]\[50\] ACI Europe's own InterVISTAS synopsis concedes the relationship "goes both ways".\[51\] Correlations between airport traffic and regional GDP therefore overstate causal impact.
- **Core/periphery heterogeneity is the most robust pattern.** Effects appear in peripheral regions (Mukkala & Tervo; Rattsø & Sheard at 100 km or more; Baker et al. in regional Australia) and vanish in core regions with substitute airports (Breidenbach; Pot & Koster).\[3\]\[4\]\[39\]\[42\]

## 4. Refuting and Skeptical Evidence

**European Court of Auditors, Special Report 21/2014.** The Court audited 20 EU-co-funded airports in Estonia, Greece, Italy, Poland and Spain, which received €666m of EU funds in 2000–2013 (out of about €4.5bn allocated to airports EU-wide).\[52\]\[53\] Its findings:
- only half could demonstrate need for the investment;\[54\]
- 13 had significant catchment overlap with neighbouring airports;\[55\]
- infrastructure was often oversized, and about €38m worth was never used;\[54\]
- seven were not profitable and "may need to be closed unless they receive continuous public financial support";\[56\]
- Córdoba handled fewer than 7,000 passengers in 2013 against a forecast of 179,000.\[53\]

This is the clearest official evidence that small-airport investment often fails to deliver projected traffic, let alone regional impact.

**Spanish "ghost airports."**
- **Ciudad Real:** cost about €1–1.1bn and opened in 2008. Its operator went bankrupt in 2012 with about €300m of debt. A €10,000 bid in 2015 was rejected (the court set a €40m minimum), and the airport finally sold for €56.2m in 2018. Secondary sources put its lifetime throughput at about 190,000 passengers.\[57\]\[58\]\[59\]
- **Castellón:** opened in 2011 but received its first commercial flight only in 2015.\[60\]\[61\]

These cases show that building an airport does not create demand.

**The structural economics of sub-1m airports.**
- The EU's 2014 Guidelines themselves state that airports under 700,000 passengers "may face increased difficulties in achieving the full cost coverage". That is why they allowed 80% operating-aid coverage without compulsory phase-out, a regime since extended to 2027.\[5\]\[8\]
- **Cornwall Newquay** shows this in practice. With about 440k passengers it has "long relied on substantial public funding", with underlying trading losses regularly above £4m a year. After the council ended the Newquay–Gatwick PSO and Eastern Airways collapsed (a £1.4m shortfall), it commissioned an efficiency review from Steer. According to The Post/Newquay Voice (20 August 2026), the review made 71 recommendations, and the cabinet member told an extraordinary council meeting that "council taxpayers cannot be expected to find anything like £9-£10 million a year."
- So a £70m+ gross GVA headline sits alongside a recurring £4m+ public cost. That is not necessarily poor value, but it cannot be judged without net-impact analysis.

**US Essential Air Service (EAS) and small-community air service.**
- GAO reported in 2000 that average EAS subsidy per passenger rose 47%, from about $56 to $82. A 1983 GAO review found extreme cases such as Blythe, CA, at $836 per passenger.\[62\]\[63\]
- The FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 raised the per-passenger subsidy cap to $650 for communities within 175 miles of a medium or large hub. A $1,000 overall cap applies, lowered to $850 from 1 October 2026.\[64\]\[65\]
- GAO-24-106681 found total EAS subsidies "increased from approximately $349 million to $456 million per year in real 2023 dollars—a 31-percent increase" from 2018 to 2023. Average subsidy per community rose 33%, from $3.2m to $4.3m.
- GAO-26-109182 found that over 70% of nonhub airports *without* EAS lost average daily departures per route between 2018 and 2024. As reported by AVweb, average daily departures per route at these airports fell 19% over the period, and airlines increasingly demand larger revenue guarantees.

In short, US nonhub service is fragile, and "impact" can evaporate when an airline leaves.

**Critiques of input-output impact studies.** Several methodological problems inflate headline numbers:
- **Gross not net.** They count all activity at the airport, including jobs that would exist elsewhere in the region without it (displacement).
- **Visitor spending** is attributed in full, even when visitors would have arrived through another airport.
- **Scope inflation.** Manufacturers (Duluth/Cirrus), military bases (Great Falls), cargo hubs (Bellingham/FedEx) and business parks (Newquay/Aerohub) are folded in.
- **Advocacy.** Most studies are commissioned by airports, state aviation agencies or airport associations to support funding cases. The Knock example, with "almost 3,000 jobs" in one EY study versus about 1,100 in another, shows how sensitive totals are to scope.\[18\]\[29\]
- **Catalytic multipliers.** Figures such as InterVISTAS's "10% connectivity → 0.5% GDP per capita" are national cross-country associations\[51\] and should not be scaled down to a single small airport.

**Closures.** I found no rigorous before-and-after evaluation of UK regional airport closures (Plymouth 2011, Manston 2014, Blackpool's loss of scheduled service). This gap is itself telling: closures have not produced a body of evidence documenting measurable regional damage. Pot & Koster's conclusion that closure damage is "likely limited" for small airports is the best available inference, but it is not a closure study.\[33\]

## 5. Catalytic and Social Impacts

- **Tourism** is the most robustly documented catalytic channel. Memmingen (synthetic control) shows causal tourism gains for a peripheral Alpine tourism region.\[48\] The New Zealand elasticity (0.07) shows positive but modest effects.\[38\] Inverness (£89m visitor spend) and Knock (€182m, 900,000 bed nights) show large gross visitor flows.\[14\]\[29\] Low-cost carrier service is usually the trigger for tourism growth, but LCC passengers generate about 20% fewer direct airport jobs than non-LCC passengers (ACI Europe/InterVISTAS).\[51\]
- **Business attraction and connectivity.** Inverness Airport is "cited in every inward investment proposition" by Highlands and Islands Enterprise,\[10\] and hub links (Inverness–Heathrow) give access to global networks.\[10\] Sheard's evidence suggests the effect works mainly through tradable-services specialisation rather than total employment.\[43\]
- **Time savings.** Inverness passengers' time savings were valued at £43m a year (2017), split evenly between business and leisure.\[13\]\[15\] This user benefit is a welfare gain often omitted from employment-based impact studies.
- **Social inclusion and remote access.** Highlands and Islands Airports cites literature consensus that "airports in peripheral regions have some stimulating effect on employment and population growth".\[11\] In Norway, regional airports remain politically justified largely on accessibility grounds, though Tveter and Rattsø & Sheard find small airports' population effects weak.\[39\]\[66\] The ACI Europe/SEO study links connectivity to education and gender-equality SDG indicators,\[67\] but those are national-level associations.
- **Health and emergency services.** Montana's 2026 study documents air ambulance, firefighting, law enforcement and agricultural aviation functions at Great Falls.\[24\] These are real benefits that do not depend on scheduled passenger volume.

## 6. Benchmarks Applied to a 500,000-Passenger Airport

| Benchmark (source) | Ratio | Implied at 500k passengers |
|---|---|---|
| ACI Europe/InterVISTAS (2015), airports under 1 mppa | 1.2 direct jobs per 1,000 extra passengers\[51\] | **~600 direct jobs** |
| ACI Europe/InterVISTAS, all-airport average | 0.954 direct jobs per 1,000 passengers\[51\] | ~480 direct jobs |
| Same, low-cost carrier traffic | −20% direct jobs vs non-LCC\[51\] | ~380–480 direct jobs if LCC-dominated |
| ACI Europe average direct wage | €40,400 a year (indirect €29,500; induced €27,400)\[51\] | ~€15–24m direct payroll (European average; lower in low-wage regions) |
| Inverness regional multiplier (on-site total ÷ direct) | 748 ÷ 554 ≈ 1.35 within the catchment\[15\] | ~500–800 on-site total jobs in the region |
| Inverness total incl. visitors | ~2.9 FTE per 1,000 passengers; ~£107 GVA per passenger | ~1,400 FTE; ~£53m GVA (gross) |
| Bellingham total (US, IMPLAN) | ~4.1 jobs per 1,000 passengers; ~$650 output per passenger | ~2,000 jobs; ~$325m output (gross) |
| Newquay | ~1.6 jobs per 1,000 passengers; ~£164 GVA per passenger | ~820 jobs; ~£80m GVA (gross, incl. business park) |

**Indicative range for a 500k airport:**
- direct on-site jobs: about 400–600;
- total regional gross jobs including multipliers and visitor spending: about 1,000–2,500;
- gross GVA: about £/€40–100m;
- US-style "output": about $150–350m.

**Caveats:**
- Economies of scale mean smaller airports are more job-intensive per passenger.\[51\]
- Charter/LCC versus legacy/hub-feeder traffic, and cargo, military or MRO activity, can move the numbers by a factor of 2–4.
- Regional multipliers are smaller than national ones.
- All of these are gross figures. Applying a displacement or deadweight adjustment of 30–70% for regions with alternative airports within about 1–1.5 hours (my judgement, consistent with the null causal findings in Germany) would substantially cut net figures. For a remote region with no substitute, net is closer to gross.

**Putting it in proportion:** In a catchment with 100,000 jobs, 1,500 gross supported jobs equals about 1.5% of employment. That is noticeable but not transformative. Net of displacement it could be well under 1%. In a sparsely populated periphery (e.g., the Highlands, northern Norway, Montana), the same airport can be among the largest single employment sites and the only practical long-distance link. That is where "significant" becomes defensible.

## 7. Overall Assessment

**Verdict: conditionally supported.**

**"Measurable": supported, with caveats on reliability.** Gross impacts of a 500k airport can be measured with standard input-output methods, and every study reviewed finds hundreds of direct jobs and a multi-tens-of-millions GVA footprint. But measurement reliability is low for the components that matter most, namely visitor-spending attribution and catalytic effects. Totals can swing by a factor of three with scope choices, as at Knock. Causal (net) effects are often too small to detect statistically at this airport size, as in Germany, Norway's small airports, and Pot & Koster.

**"Significant": supported only when most of the following conditions hold:**
1. **Peripherality and no substitute:** no comparable airport within about 100 km or about 1–1.5 hours' drive (Rattsø & Sheard's 100 km result; Mukkala & Tervo's periphery result; ECA's catchment-overlap finding).
2. **Network quality:** at least one link to a major hub or capital (Inverness–Heathrow, Kerry/Knock–Dublin/UK), which Rattsø & Sheard find amplifies effects.
3. **Tourism potential:** a destination with latent inbound demand, ideally unlocked by LCC or leisure service (Memmingen/Allgäu, Knock/Wild Atlantic Way).
4. **A base of tradable services or specialised industry:** Sheard's mechanism, plus on-airport clusters such as aerospace, MRO or cargo.
5. **Sustainable finances:** an operating deficit that is small relative to net regional benefits. Newquay's £4m+ annual losses against about £72m gross GVA may pass this test; Ciudad Real and the ECA's unprofitable seven did not.
6. **Stable airline commitment:** GAO shows nonhub service is fragile, and impact disappears with the route.

**What decision-makers should do:**
- Treat airport-commissioned gross impact figures as an upper bound.
- Require a net-impact or cost-benefit appraisal that includes the counterfactual airport, displacement, user time savings, and the full subsidy stream.
- Prioritise support for genuinely remote airports with hub links and tourism demand.
- Be sceptical of new or expanded 500k-scale airports in regions already served within about 100 km.

## Caveats

- Several passenger volumes (Helena, Great Falls, Bangor, Green Bay) were not stated in the sources reviewed. Those comparability estimates are approximate.
- The Memmingen, Baker et al. and Tsui et al. findings were taken from abstracts or secondary reviews, so effect sizes beyond those stated were not verified.
- Ciudad Real's lifetime passenger total comes from secondary sources.
- No authoritative worldwide count of 300k–800k-passenger airports was found; the "several hundred" figure is an inference.
- UK, Australian and Canadian formal size definitions, and rigorous closure evaluations (Plymouth, Manston, Blackpool), were not covered by primary sources in this review.
- Several Cornwall Newquay financial figures come from 2026 local news reporting of council statements rather than audited accounts.
