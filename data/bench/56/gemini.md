# Equilibrium Characterization and Solution Methodologies for Ex-Ante Asymmetric Two-Bidder First-Price Sealed-Bid Auctions

## Executive Synthesis: Analytical Intractability Versus Methodological Generality

In the study of mechanism design and incomplete-information games, the two-bidder first-price sealed-bid auction (FPA) with asymmetric independent private values (IPV) occupies a pivotal yet mathematically challenging position [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). When bidders are ex-ante symmetric—drawing private valuations independently from an identical cumulative distribution function F(v)F(v)—the Bayesian-Nash Equilibrium (BNE) reduces to a single linear first-order ordinary differential equation (ODE) that admits an explicit closed-form integral solution for any smooth distribution via a standard integrating factor [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Once ex-ante asymmetry is introduced (F1(v)≠F2(v)F_1(v) \neq F_2(v)), however, no universal closed-form analytical solution exists for arbitrary continuous valuation distributions [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf).

Despite the absence of a universal closed-form integral, auction theory provides a general mathematical and computational methodology for characterizing, analyzing, and solving the two-bidder asymmetric FPA [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Established through foundational contributions by Lebrun (1996, 1999) and Maskin and Riley (2000), the general solution procedure reformulates the equilibrium problem in terms of inverse bidding strategies ϕi(b)≡βi−1(b)\phi_i(b) \equiv \beta_i^{-1}(b), transforming the bidders' first-order optimization conditions into a coupled system of non-autonomous, nonlinear first-order ODEs governed by a two-point boundary value problem (BVP) over an endogenous bid support [b‾,bˉ][\underline{b}, \bar{b}] [10](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/econ805%202007/econ%20805%20lecture%209.pdf).

Solving this canonical BVP requires confronting two structural obstacles that do not arise in symmetric environments. The first obstacle is the endogenous free upper boundary bˉ\bar{b}: because neither bidder will submit a bid exceeding the maximum possible bid of their rival, both equilibrium bid distributions must terminate at a common supremum bˉ\bar{b} that is unknown ex-ante and cannot be computed via revenue equivalence [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). The second obstacle is a lower-boundary mathematical singularity (0/00/0): at the minimum winning bid b‾\underline{b}, the probability of winning approaches zero while the equilibrium markup over the bid also vanishes, creating an indeterminate 0/00/0 form in the governing ODEs [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). This singularity violates the Lipschitz continuity conditions required by the Fundamental Theorem of Differential Equations (Picard–Lindelöf Theorem), destroying local uniqueness of the initial value problem at b‾\underline{b} and rendering naive forward numerical integration or forward Taylor-series expansion ineffective [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

Consequently, the general methodology for solving a two-bidder asymmetric FPA encompasses four rigorous paradigms tailored to the analytical structure of the underlying distributions F1F_1 and F2F_2. First, exact analytical reductions via first integrals apply to specific parametric families—most notably general shifted and stretched uniform distributions (Griesmer, Levitan, and Shubik 1967; Kaplan and Zamir 2012) and power-law distributions (Plum 1992; Cheng 2006)—where the coupled ODEs admit an algebraic conservation law or linear ansatz that decouples the system into an explicit closed-form solution [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). Second, global numerical BVP solvers and shooting algorithms address general continuous distributions through recursive Taylor-series backward-shooting algorithms (Marshall et al. 1994; Gayle and Richard 2008), regularized forward-shooting and boundary-value solvers that bypass the analytic instability of backward integration (Fibich and Gavish 2011, 2012), or global shape-preserving polynomial collocation methods guided by economic theory (Hubbard, Kirkegaard, and Paarsch 2013; Hubbard and Paarsch 2014) [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Third, asymptotic perturbation expansions (Fibich and Gavious 2003) handle environments with small distributional asymmetry (Fi(v)=F(v)+εHi(v)F_i(v) = F(v) + \varepsilon H_i(v)) by linearizing the ODE system around the closed-form symmetric equilibrium to yield explicit analytical approximations [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf). Fourth, structural transformations and distribution-free bounds demonstrate that when post-auction resale is permitted (Hafalir and Krishna 2008), the asymmetric ODEs collapse into a symmetric system with identical bid distributions, restoring analytical solvability [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf), while qualitative comparative statics (Maskin and Riley 2000; Kirkegaard 2009) and computer science Price of Anarchy techniques (Syrgkanis and Tardos 2013; Hoy, Taggart, and Wang 2018) extract exact welfare and revenue guarantees directly from the best-response inequalities without integrating the ODEs [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf).

## The General Mathematical Characterization: The Inverse-Bid Boundary Value Problem

### Setup and Inversion of the Bidding Strategies

Consider a single indivisible object auctioned to two risk-neutral buyers, indexed by i∈{1,2}i \in \{1, 2\} (frequently designated as the Strong bidder SS and Weak bidder WW when ordered by stochastic dominance), who attach independent private values viv_i to the object [5](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/riley/research/asyRES.PDF). Each bidder ii draws viv_i from a publicly known cumulative distribution function (CDF) Fi(vi)F_i(v_i) with support [v‾i,vˉi][\underline{v}_i, \bar{v}_i] and a continuous, strictly positive probability density function (PDF) fi(vi)=Fi′(vi)>0f_i(v_i) = F_i'(v_i) > 0 on (v‾i,vˉi](\underline{v}_i, \bar{v}_i] [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). The seller may also impose a publicly known minimum bid (reserve price) m≥0m \ge 0 [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae).

In a sealed-bid first-price auction, both buyers simultaneously submit bids bi≥mb_i \ge m; the highest bidder wins the object and pays their own bid bib_i, while the losing bidder pays nothing and receives a payoff of zero [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Under continuous valuation distributions, ties occur with probability zero in any strictly monotonic equilibrium [5](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/riley/research/asyRES.PDF). As proven by Lebrun (1991, 1996, 1999) and Maskin and Riley (2000), when v‾1<vˉ1\underline{v}_1 < \bar{v}_1 and v‾2<vˉ2\underline{v}_2 < \bar{v}_2 with overlapping competitive supports, any pure-strategy BNE consists of bidding functions βi:[v‾i,vˉi]→R+\beta_i : [\underline{v}_i, \bar{v}_i] \to \mathbb{R}_+ that are strictly increasing and differentiable over the interval of valuations that win with strictly positive probability [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf).

Attempting to formulate the equilibrium directly in terms of the forward bidding functions β1(v1)\beta_1(v_1) and β2(v2)\beta_2(v_2) yields a functional composition problem because Bidder 1's probability of winning with a bid bb depends on Pr⁡(β2(v2)≤b)=F2(β2−1(b))\Pr(\beta_2(v_2) \le b) = F_2(\beta_2^{-1}(b)) [10](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/econ805%202007/econ%20805%20lecture%209.pdf). Because β1(v1)≠β2(v2)\beta_1(v_1) \neq \beta_2(v_2), the state variable v1v_1 cannot be directly aligned with v2v_2 as it is in the symmetric case where β1(v)=β2(v)=β(v)\beta_1(v) = \beta_2(v) = \beta(v) implies Pr⁡(β(v2)≤β(v1))=F(v1)\Pr(\beta(v_2) \le \beta(v_1)) = F(v_1) [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

The universal first step in solving an asymmetric FPA is therefore coordinate inversion: treating the bid bb as the independent variable and solving for the inverse bid functions ϕi(b)≡βi−1(b)\phi_i(b) \equiv \beta_i^{-1}(b) (also denoted vi(b)v_i(b), λi(b)\lambda_i(b), or αi(b)\alpha_i(b) across the literature) [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Here, ϕi(b)\phi_i(b) represents the valuation that bidder ii must possess in equilibrium to submit the bid bb [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

### Derivation of the Coupled Nonlinear ODE System

Over the common equilibrium interval of winning bids b∈[b‾,bˉ]b \in [\underline{b}, \bar{b}], if bidder ii has true valuation viv_i and submits a bid bb, bidder ii outbids opponent j≠ij \neq i whenever βj(vj)<b\beta_j(v_j) < b, which by strict monotonicity of βj\beta_j is equivalent to vj<ϕj(b)v_j < \phi_j(b) [10](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/econ805%202007/econ%20805%20lecture%209.pdf). The probability that bidder ii wins with bid bb is therefore Fj(ϕj(b))F_j(\phi_j(b)) [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Bidder ii's expected surplus maximization problem is [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf):

max⁡b∈[b‾,bˉ]Πi(b;vi)=(vi−b)Fj(ϕj(b))\max_{b \in [\underline{b}, \bar{b}]} \Pi_i(b; v_i) = (v_i - b) F_j(\phi_j(b))

Differentiating Πi(b;vi)\Pi_i(b; v_i) with respect to bb yields the necessary first-order condition (FOC) for an interior optimum [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf):

∂Πi(b;vi)∂b=(vi−b)fj(ϕj(b))ϕj′(b)−Fj(ϕj(b))=0\frac{\partial \Pi_i(b; v_i)}{\partial b} = (v_i - b) f_j(\phi_j(b)) \phi_j'(b) - F_j(\phi_j(b)) = 0

In equilibrium, a bidder who optimally chooses bid bb must have valuation vi=ϕi(b)v_i = \phi_i(b) [10](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/econ805%202007/econ%20805%20lecture%209.pdf). Substituting v1=ϕ1(b)v_1 = \phi_1(b) and v2=ϕ2(b)v_2 = \phi_2(b) into the respective first-order conditions for i∈{1,2}i \in \{1, 2\} and rearranging yields the canonical system of coupled nonlinear first-order ODEs for the two-bidder asymmetric FPA [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf):

ϕ1′(b)=F1(ϕ1(b))f1(ϕ1(b))(ϕ2(b)−b)\phi_1'(b) = \frac{F_1(\phi_1(b))}{f_1(\phi_1(b)) \left(\phi_2(b) - b\right)}

ϕ2′(b)=F2(ϕ2(b))f2(ϕ2(b))(ϕ1(b)−b)\phi_2'(b) = \frac{F_2(\phi_2(b))}{f_2(\phi_2(b)) \left(\phi_1(b) - b\right)}

Observing that ddbln⁡Fi(ϕi(b))=fi(ϕi(b))ϕi′(b)Fi(ϕi(b))\frac{d}{db} \ln F_i(\phi_i(b)) = \frac{f_i(\phi_i(b)) \phi_i'(b)}{F_i(\phi_i(b))}, this system can be written compactly in its logarithmic reverse-hazard-rate form [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf):

ddbln⁡F1(ϕ1(b))=1ϕ2(b)−b,ddbln⁡F2(ϕ2(b))=1ϕ1(b)−b\frac{d}{db} \ln F_1(\phi_1(b)) = \frac{1}{\phi_2(b) - b}, \qquad \frac{d}{db} \ln F_2(\phi_2(b)) = \frac{1}{\phi_1(b) - b}

This formulation exposes the fundamental economic feedback loop of asymmetric bidding: the rate at which Bidder 1's cumulative bid distribution G1(b)≡F1(ϕ1(b))G_1(b) \equiv F_1(\phi_1(b)) expands in log-space at bid bb is equal to the inverse of Bidder 2's equilibrium rent (bid shading) ϕ2(b)−b\phi_2(b) - b at that exact same bid bb, and vice versa [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf).

### Boundary Conditions Across Support Configurations

Because the ODE system comprises two first-order equations but the domain of equilibrium winning bids [b‾,bˉ][\underline{b}, \bar{b}] is itself endogenous, closing the model requires four boundary conditions that pin down both the trajectories (ϕ1(b),ϕ2(b))(\phi_1(b), \phi_2(b)) and the endpoints b‾\underline{b} and bˉ\bar{b} [10](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~dquint/econ805%202007/econ%20805%20lecture%209.pdf).

#### The Upper Boundary Condition (Common Maximum Bid bˉ\bar{b})

Regardless of whether one bidder's valuation support extends far above the other's (vˉ1>vˉ2\bar{v}_1 > \bar{v}_2), the upper supports of the two equilibrium bid distributions must coincide at a single unknown value bˉ\bar{b} [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf):

β1(vˉ1)=β2(vˉ2)=bˉ⟺ϕ1(bˉ)=vˉ1 and ϕ2(bˉ)=vˉ2\beta_1(\bar{v}_1) = \beta_2(\bar{v}_2) = \bar{b} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \phi_1(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_1 \;\;\text{and}\;\; \phi_2(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_2

The proof follows by contrapositive: suppose β1(vˉ1)=bˉ1>bˉ2=β2(vˉ2)\beta_1(\bar{v}_1) = \bar{b}_1 > \bar{b}_2 = \beta_2(\bar{v}_2) and bˉ1\bar{b}_1 is bid with zero point mass [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Then Bidder 1 with valuation vˉ1\bar{v}_1 wins with probability 11 by bidding bˉ1\bar{b}_1, but could reduce their bid to bˉ1+bˉ22>bˉ2\frac{\bar{b}_1 + \bar{b}_2}{2} > \bar{b}_2, still win with certainty (F2(ϕ2(b))=1F_2(\phi_2(b)) = 1), and strictly reduce their payment, contradicting optimality [9](https://ratio.huji.ac.il/files/dp117.pdf). Thus, both inverse bid functions must hit their respective upper valuation bounds at the exact same terminal bid bˉ∈(b‾,min⁡{vˉ1,vˉ2})\bar{b} \in (\underline{b}, \min\{\bar{v}_1, \bar{v}_2\}) [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf).

#### The Lower Boundary Condition (b‾\underline{b}) Under Identical Versus Shifted Supports

The lower boundary condition depends critically on the relationship between the lower endpoints of the valuation supports v‾1,v‾2\underline{v}_1, \underline{v}_2 and the reserve price mm, where the bidders are ordered without loss of generality such that v‾1≤v‾2\underline{v}_1 \le \underline{v}_2 [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae). Under identical lower supports (v‾1=v‾2=v‾\underline{v}_1 = \underline{v}_2 = \underline{v}) with a non-binding reserve price (m≤v‾m \le \underline{v}), both bidders begin competing at the minimum valuation b‾=v‾\underline{b} = \underline{v} (commonly normalized to 00), and any bidder with valuation v‾\underline{v} wins with probability zero and bids v‾\underline{v}, yielding the standard initial conditions ϕ1(v‾)=v‾\phi_1(\underline{v}) = \underline{v} and ϕ2(v‾)=v‾\phi_2(\underline{v}) = \underline{v} [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Conversely, when a binding reserve price is imposed (m≥v‾2≥v‾1m \ge \underline{v}_2 \ge \underline{v}_1), neither bidder can submit a valid bid below mm and any valuation type vi<mv_i < m is excluded from winning, which sets the lowest winning bid to b‾=m\underline{b} = m with boundary conditions ϕ1(m)=m\phi_1(m) = m and ϕ2(m)=m\phi_2(m) = m [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae).

In the intermediate regime of shifted supports (v‾1<v‾2<vˉ1\underline{v}_1 < \underline{v}_2 < \bar{v}_1) with m<v‾2m < \underline{v}_2, as analyzed by Maskin and Riley (2000) and Kaplan and Zamir (2012), no buyer can win with positive probability by bidding less than v‾2\underline{v}_2, because Buyer 2's valuation is at least v‾2\underline{v}_2 with probability 11 [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae). Under the standard equilibrium refinement that any buyer with zero probability of winning bids their true valuation (which eliminates extraneous equilibria that are allocationally equivalent to artificial reserve prices), Buyer 1 bids β1(v1)=v1\beta_1(v_1) = v_1 for all v1∈[v‾1,b‾]v_1 \in [\underline{v}_1, \underline{b}] [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae). Meanwhile, Buyer 2's lowest type v‾2\underline{v}_2 chooses the entry bid b‾≥max⁡{v‾2,m}\underline{b} \ge \max\{\underline{v}_2, m\} to maximize expected profit against Buyer 1's truthful bidding schedule ϕ1(b)=b\phi_1(b) = b [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions):

b‾=max⁡{m, arg⁡max⁡b≥v‾2(v‾2−b)F1(b)}\underline{b} = \max\left\{m,\; \arg\max_{b \ge \underline{v}_2} (\underline{v}_2 - b) F_1(b)\right\}

At this endogenously determined threshold b‾\underline{b}, the boundary conditions for the ODE system on [b‾,bˉ][\underline{b}, \bar{b}] are ϕ1(b‾)=b‾\phi_1(\underline{b}) = \underline{b} and ϕ2(b‾)=v‾2\phi_2(\underline{b}) = \underline{v}_2 [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae). For instance, when F1F_1 is uniform on [v‾1,vˉ1][\underline{v}_1, \bar{v}_1] and m≤v‾1m \le \underline{v}_1, maximizing (v‾2−b)b−v‾1vˉ1−v‾1(\underline{v}_2 - b)\frac{b - \underline{v}_1}{\bar{v}_1 - \underline{v}_1} yields the exact lower bound b‾=v‾1+v‾22\underline{b} = \frac{\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2}{2} [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf).

#### Degenerate Corner and Pooling Equilibria Under Extreme Shifts

If the asymmetry between supports is sufficiently extreme that b‾\underline{b} calculated above reaches or exceeds vˉ1\bar{v}_1 (for uniform distributions, when v‾2≥2vˉ1−v‾1\underline{v}_2 \ge 2\bar{v}_1 - \underline{v}_1), the interior ODE regime vanishes entirely [5](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/riley/research/asyRES.PDF). In Maskin and Riley's canonical example of non-overlapping uniform supports where VW∼U[0,1]V_W \sim U[0, 1] and VS∼U[2,3]V_S \sim U[2, 3], the differential equation regime collapses: the weak buyer bids truthfully (βW(vW)=vW\beta_W(v_W) = v_W for vW∈[0,1]v_W \in [0, 1]), and the strong buyer's unconstrained optimization problem arg⁡max⁡b≤1(vS−b)b\arg\max_{b \le 1} (v_S - b)b hits the corner solution b=1b = 1 for all vS≥2v_S \ge 2 [5](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/riley/research/asyRES.PDF). Consequently, the strong buyer pools at a flat bid of βS(vS)=1.00\beta_S(v_S) = 1.00 across their entire valuation support [2,3][2, 3], winning the object with probability 11 at a deterministic price of 1.001.00 and doubling expected seller revenue relative to a second-price auction, where the strong buyer still always wins but pays the expected value of the weak buyer's valuation, E[vW]=0.50\mathbb{E}[v_W] = 0.50 [5](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/riley/research/asyRES.PDF).

### Existence, Uniqueness, and the Origin Singularity

The theoretical validity of solving the coupled ODE system as a general method rests on two foundational theorems proved by Lebrun (1996, 1999) and Maskin and Riley (2000): under standard regularity conditions (atomless distributions with continuous positive densities on compact supports), a pure-strategy BNE always exists, and when the lower supports coincide or overlap non-trivially, the equilibrium is unique and strictly characterized by the solution to the coupled ODE system subject to the boundary conditions at b‾\underline{b} and bˉ\bar{b} [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf).

However, inspecting the ODE system at the lower boundary b‾=v‾=0\underline{b} = \underline{v} = 0 reveals the central analytical pathology of asymmetric first-price auctions [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf):

lim⁡b→0+ϕi′(b)=lim⁡b→0+Fi(ϕi(b))fi(ϕi(b))(ϕj(b)−b)=0fi(0)⋅0=00\lim_{b \to 0^+} \phi_i'(b) = \lim_{b \to 0^+} \frac{F_i(\phi_i(b))}{f_i(\phi_i(b))(\phi_j(b) - b)} = \frac{0}{f_i(0) \cdot 0} = \frac{0}{0}

Because both the numerator Fi(ϕi(0))=0F_i(\phi_i(0)) = 0 and the denominator factor ϕj(0)−0=0\phi_j(0) - 0 = 0 vanish simultaneously, the right-hand side of the ODE system is undefined and non-Lipschitz at b=0b = 0 [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Consequently, the standard local existence and uniqueness theorem for initial value problems (Picard–Lindelöf / Cauchy–Lipschitz) fails at b=0b = 0: infinitely many trajectories emerge from the origin (0,0)(0,0), and only one specific trajectory simultaneously reaches (vˉ1,vˉ2)(\bar{v}_1, \bar{v}_2) at a common upper bound bˉ\bar{b} [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

Furthermore, if f1(0)>0f_1(0) > 0 and f2(0)>0f_2(0) > 0, applying L'Hôpital's rule to the singularity at b=0b = 0 (where Fi(ϕi(b))fi(ϕi(b))∼ϕi(b)\frac{F_i(\phi_i(b))}{f_i(\phi_i(b))} \sim \phi_i(b) as b→0b \to 0) yields a coupled algebraic system for the initial slopes s1≡ϕ1′(0)s_1 \equiv \phi_1'(0) and s2≡ϕ2′(0)s_2 \equiv \phi_2'(0) [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf):

s1=lim⁡b→0+ϕ1(b)ϕ2(b)−b=s1s2−1,s2=lim⁡b→0+ϕ2(b)ϕ1(b)−b=s2s1−1s_1 = \lim_{b \to 0^+} \frac{\phi_1(b)}{\phi_2(b) - b} = \frac{s_1}{s_2 - 1}, \qquad s_2 = \lim_{b \to 0^+} \frac{\phi_2(b)}{\phi_1(b) - b} = \frac{s_2}{s_1 - 1}

Since interior bidding requires si>1s_i > 1, this system forces s1=2s_1 = 2 and s2=2s_2 = 2, meaning β1′(0)=β2′(0)=12\beta_1'(0) = \beta_2'(0) = \frac{1}{2} regardless of the global shape of F1F_1 and F2F_2 [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Even more problematic for local series methods, Gayle and Richard (2008) and Marshall et al. (1994) demonstrated that successive differentiation of the transformed probability equations li(b)≡Fi(ϕi(b))l_i(b) \equiv F_i(\phi_i(b)) at the lower bound b=v‾b = \underline{v} yields li′(v‾)=fi(v‾)2l_i'(\underline{v}) = \frac{f_i(\underline{v})}{2} and evaluates all higher-order derivatives li(k)(v‾)l_i^{(k)}(\underline{v}) (k≥2k \ge 2) to zero [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Thus, any attempt to integrate the ODEs directly forward from b=v‾b = \underline{v} using standard Taylor expansions produces a spurious linear function rather than the true nonlinear equilibrium [17](https://d.repec.org/n?u=RePEc:sce:scecf5:472&r=).

## Exact Analytical Solution Methods for Integrable Parametric Families

While the coupled ODE system cannot be integrated in closed form for arbitrary pairs (F1,F2)(F_1, F_2), a general analytical reduction strategy succeeds whenever the hazard-rate structure Fi(v)fi(v)\frac{F_i(v)}{f_i(v)} permits an exact first integral (conservation law) or a scale-invariant linear ansatz [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). In the auction theory literature, exact closed-form solutions have been established for three primary parametric classes [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf).

### The General Uniform Family: First-Integral Reduction (Griesmer, Levitan, and Shubik 1967; Kaplan and Zamir 2012)

Suppose Bidder 1 and Bidder 2 draw valuations uniformly from arbitrary intervals V1∼U[v‾1,vˉ1]V_1 \sim U[\underline{v}_1, \bar{v}_1] and V2∼U[v‾2,vˉ2]V_2 \sim U[\underline{v}_2, \bar{v}_2] with v‾1≤v‾2\underline{v}_1 \le \underline{v}_2, subject to a reserve price mm [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae). Because Fi(v)=v−v‾ivˉi−v‾iF_i(v) = \frac{v - \underline{v}_i}{\bar{v}_i - \underline{v}_i} and fi(v)=1vˉi−v‾if_i(v) = \frac{1}{\bar{v}_i - \underline{v}_i}, the ratio Fi(ϕi(b))fi(ϕi(b))=ϕi(b)−v‾i\frac{F_i(\phi_i(b))}{f_i(\phi_i(b))} = \phi_i(b) - \underline{v}_i is independent of the upper support parameters vˉ1,vˉ2\bar{v}_1, \bar{v}_2 [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf). Over the competitive bid interval [b‾,bˉ][\underline{b}, \bar{b}], the canonical ODE system simplifies to [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf):

ϕ1′(b)(ϕ2(b)−b)=ϕ1(b)−v‾1\phi_1'(b)(\phi_2(b) - b) = \phi_1(b) - \underline{v}_1

ϕ2′(b)(ϕ1(b)−b)=ϕ2(b)−v‾2\phi_2'(b)(\phi_1(b) - b) = \phi_2(b) - \underline{v}_2

Direct inspection of this system reveals that the upper bounds vˉ1,vˉ2\bar{v}_1, \bar{v}_2 enter only through the terminal boundary conditions ϕ1(bˉ)=vˉ1\phi_1(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_1 and ϕ2(bˉ)=vˉ2\phi_2(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_2 [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf). To solve this coupled system analytically, Kaplan and Zamir (2012) (generalizing Griesmer, Levitan, and Shubik 1967) sum the two ODEs and rearrange terms into exact total derivatives [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae):

ϕ1′(b)ϕ2(b)+ϕ2′(b)ϕ1(b)=ϕ1(b)+ϕ2(b)+b(ϕ1′(b)+ϕ2′(b))−(v‾1+v‾2)\phi_1'(b)\phi_2(b) + \phi_2'(b)\phi_1(b) = \phi_1(b) + \phi_2(b) + b(\phi_1'(b) + \phi_2'(b)) - (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)

ddb[ϕ1(b)ϕ2(b)]=ddb[(ϕ1(b)+ϕ2(b)−(v‾1+v‾2))b]\frac{d}{db}\left[\phi_1(b)\phi_2(b)\right] = \frac{d}{db}\left[\left(\phi_1(b) + \phi_2(b) - (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)\right)b\right]

Integrating both sides with respect to bb yields an exact algebraic conservation law connecting ϕ1(b)\phi_1(b) and ϕ2(b)\phi_2(b) at every bid b∈[b‾,bˉ]b \in [\underline{b}, \bar{b}] [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf):

ϕ1(b)ϕ2(b)=b(ϕ1(b)+ϕ2(b))−(v‾1+v‾2)b+c\phi_1(b)\phi_2(b) = b\left(\phi_1(b) + \phi_2(b)\right) - (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)b + c

where cc is the constant of integration determined by evaluating the conservation law at the known lower boundary b‾\underline{b} [20](https://search.proquest.com/openview/9376e16aa13b786e5d516ea2f5f04758/1.pdf?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=30972). Specifically, since ϕ1(b‾)=b‾\phi_1(\underline{b}) = \underline{b} and ϕ2(b‾)=max⁡{v‾2,m}\phi_2(\underline{b}) = \max\{\underline{v}_2, m\}, substituting b=b‾b = \underline{b} pins down cc across reserve-price regimes [15](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/figshare-production-eu-uoe-storage4174-eu-west-1/56686721/kaplan12.pdf?X-Amz-Algorithm=AWS4-HMAC-SHA256&X-Amz-Credential=AKIARRFKZQ25EXSLB6NE/20260926/eu-west-1/s3/aws4_request&X-Amz-Date=20260926T004917Z&X-Amz-Expires=86400&X-Amz-SignedHeaders=host&X-Amz-Signature=2fa9c9bffdf00b0d4b8c7709cb1230c91f9fecac6de143b2453f3b30699e5bae). When m≤v‾1+v‾22m \le \frac{\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2}{2} (so that b‾=v‾1+v‾22\underline{b} = \frac{\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2}{2} when v‾2≤v‾1+v‾22\underline{v}_2 \le \frac{\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2}{2}, or more generally when the interior lower bound binds), the constant evaluates to c=(v‾1+v‾2)b‾−b‾2=(v‾1+v‾22)2c = (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)\underline{b} - \underline{b}^2 = \left(\frac{\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2}{2}\right)^2 [20](https://search.proquest.com/openview/9376e16aa13b786e5d516ea2f5f04758/1.pdf?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=30972). When m>v‾1+v‾22m > \frac{\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2}{2} (so that b‾=m≥v‾2\underline{b} = m \ge \underline{v}_2), the constant becomes c=(v‾1+v‾2)m−m2c = (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)m - m^2 [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf).

This algebraic conservation law allows ϕ2(b)\phi_2(b) to be expressed purely as a rational function of ϕ1(b)\phi_1(b) and bb [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf):

ϕ2(b)=bϕ1(b)−(v‾1+v‾2)b+cϕ1(b)−b\phi_2(b) = \frac{b\phi_1(b) - (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)b + c}{\phi_1(b) - b}

Substituting this expression into Bidder 1's ODE eliminates ϕ2(b)\phi_2(b) completely, reducing the coupled system to a single decoupled first-order ODE [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf):

ϕ1′(b)(b2−(v‾1+v‾2)b+c)=(ϕ1(b)−v‾1)(ϕ1(b)−b)\phi_1'(b)\left(b^2 - (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)b + c\right) = \left(\phi_1(b) - \underline{v}_1\right)\left(\phi_1(b) - b\right)

This single Riccati/Bernoulli-type equation can be integrated in closed form across all uniform configurations. In the pure stretch configuration (v‾1=v‾2=0,m=0\underline{v}_1 = \underline{v}_2 = 0, m = 0) analyzed by Griesmer, Levitan, and Shubik (1967), b‾=0\underline{b} = 0 and c=0c = 0, reducing the single ODE to b2ϕ1′(b)=ϕ1(b)(ϕ1(b)−b)b^2 \phi_1'(b) = \phi_1(b)(\phi_1(b) - b) [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf). Setting V1∼U[0,ω1]V_1 \sim U[0, \omega_1] and V2∼U[0,ω2]V_2 \sim U[0, \omega_2] with ω1>ω2\omega_1 > \omega_2, the closed-form inverse bid functions are ϕ1(b)=2b1+kb2\phi_1(b) = \frac{2b}{1 + k b^2} and ϕ2(b)=2b1−kb2\phi_2(b) = \frac{2b}{1 - k b^2}, which invert to the explicit direct bidding strategies [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf):

β1(v)=1−1−kv2kv,β2(v)=1+kv2−1kv,where k=1ω22−1ω12>0\beta_1(v) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - k v^2}}{k v}, \qquad \beta_2(v) = \frac{\sqrt{1 + k v^2} - 1}{k v}, \qquad \text{where } k = \frac{1}{\omega_2^2} - \frac{1}{\omega_1^2} > 0

In the general shifted and stretched configuration (m≤v‾1+v‾22m \le \frac{\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2}{2}) solved by Kaplan and Zamir (2012), applying the variable substitution α≡v‾2−v‾1\alpha \equiv \underline{v}_2 - \underline{v}_1, x≡b−v‾1x \equiv b - \underline{v}_1, and ϕ1(b)=v‾1+α2D(x)\phi_1(b) = \underline{v}_1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{D(x)} linearizes the ODE with respect to D(x)D(x), yielding the closed-form inverse bid functions for i≠j∈{1,2}i \neq j \in \{1, 2\} [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf):

ϕi(b)=v‾i+(v‾2−v‾1)24(b−v‾j)+(2b−(v‾1+v‾2))ciexp⁡(v‾j−v‾i2(b−b‾))\phi_i(b) = \underline{v}_i + \frac{(\underline{v}_2 - \underline{v}_1)^2}{4(b - \underline{v}_j) + \left(2b - (\underline{v}_1 + \underline{v}_2)\right) c_i \exp\left(\frac{\underline{v}_j - \underline{v}_i}{2(b - \underline{b})}\right)}

where the constants c1,c2c_1, c_2 and the common maximum bid bˉ\bar{b} are pinned down algebraically by the upper boundary conditions ϕ1(bˉ)=vˉ1\phi_1(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_1 and ϕ2(bˉ)=vˉ2\phi_2(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_2 [22](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/workshops/micro/documents/March20.pdf). Answering a fifty-year-old open question posed by Vickrey (1961), Kaplan and Zamir (2012) also demonstrated that when a binding reserve price mm is present, equilibrium bid functions can intersect at an interior valuation—meaning neither bidder is uniformly more aggressive across the entire valuation support [20](https://search.proquest.com/openview/9376e16aa13b786e5d516ea2f5f04758/1.pdf?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=30972). Furthermore, in the degenerate known-valuation limit (v‾2=vˉ2=β\underline{v}_2 = \bar{v}_2 = \beta) studied by Vickrey (1961) and Kaplan and Zamir (2000), where V1∼U[0,1]V_1 \sim U[0, 1] and Buyer 2's valuation shrinks to a commonly known constant β∈(0,2)\beta \in (0, 2) (vˉ2−v‾2=ϵ→0\bar{v}_2 - \underline{v}_2 = \epsilon \to 0), Buyer 1 uses the pure inverse strategy ϕ1(b)=β24(β−b)\phi_1(b) = \frac{\beta^2}{4(\beta - b)}, while Buyer 2 plays a continuous mixed strategy over b∈[β2,β−β24]b \in \left[\frac{\beta}{2}, \beta - \frac{\beta^2}{4}\right] with CDF F2(b)=(2−β)β2(2b−β)exp⁡(2b−ββ−2)F_2(b) = \frac{(2 - \beta)\beta}{2(2b - \beta)} \exp\left(\frac{2b - \beta}{\beta - 2}\right) [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf).

### Power Distributions with Identical Exponents: Class PP (Plum 1992)

Plum (1992) extended the Griesmer–Levitan–Shubik stretch model to the two-parameter power-distribution family (Class PP), where both bidders share a common shape exponent a>0a > 0 over different upper supports ω1>ω2\omega_1 > \omega_2 [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf):

F1(x)=(xω1)a on [0,ω1],F2(x)=(xω2)a on [0,ω2]F_1(x) = \left(\frac{x}{\omega_1}\right)^a \text{ on } [0, \omega_1], \qquad F_2(x) = \left(\frac{x}{\omega_2}\right)^a \text{ on } [0, \omega_2]

Because Fi(ϕi)fi(ϕi)=ϕia\frac{F_i(\phi_i)}{f_i(\phi_i)} = \frac{\phi_i}{a}, the ODE system takes the scale-homogeneous form a(ϕ2(b)−b)ϕ1′(b)=ϕ1(b)a(\phi_2(b) - b)\phi_1'(b) = \phi_1(b) and a(ϕ1(b)−b)ϕ2′(b)=ϕ2(b)a(\phi_1(b) - b)\phi_2'(b) = \phi_2(b) [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). Integrating this homogeneous system yields closed-form expressions parameterized by k=1ω2a+1−1ω1a+1>0k = \frac{1}{\omega_2^{a+1}} - \frac{1}{\omega_1^{a+1}} > 0 [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). When a=1a = 1, Class PP reduces identically to the Griesmer, Levitan, and Shubik (1967) uniform stretch formulas [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf).

### Power Distributions with Distinct Exponents and Calibrated Supports: Classes C1C_1 and C2C_2 (Cheng 2006, 2010)

Cheng (2006) discovered that if the two bidders draw valuations from power distributions with different exponents a1>a2>0a_1 > a_2 > 0 [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf):

F_1(x) = \left(\frac{x}{\omega_1}\right)^{a_1} \text{ on } [0, \omega_1], \qquad F_2(x) = \left(\frac{x}{\omega_2}\right)^{a_2} \text{ on } [0, \omega_2]$$the ODE system $\phi_1'(b) = \frac{\phi_1(b)}{a_1(\phi_2(b) - b)}$ and $\phi_2'(b) = \frac{\phi_2(b)}{a_2(\phi_1(b) - b)}$ always admits the linear particular solution [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf):$$\phi_1(b) = \frac{a_2 + 1}{a_2} b, \qquad \phi_2(b) = \frac{a_1 + 1}{a_1} b

Verification is immediate: substituting ϕ1(b)=a2+1a2b\phi_1(b) = \frac{a_2 + 1}{a_2}b and ϕ2(b)=a1+1a1b\phi_2(b) = \frac{a_1 + 1}{a_1}b into the right-hand side of Bidder 1's ODE gives a2+1a2ba1(a1+1a1b−b)=a2+1a2ba1(1a1b)=a2+1a2=ϕ1′(b)\frac{\frac{a_2+1}{a_2}b}{a_1\left(\frac{a_1+1}{a_1}b - b\right)} = \frac{\frac{a_2+1}{a_2}b}{a_1\left(\frac{1}{a_1}b\right)} = \frac{a_2 + 1}{a_2} = \phi_1'(b) [35](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Revenue-and-efficiency-effects-of-resale-in-first-price-auctions.pdf). For this linear trajectory to satisfy the upper boundary conditions ϕ1(bˉ)=ω1\phi_1(\bar{b}) = \omega_1 and ϕ2(bˉ)=ω2\phi_2(\bar{b}) = \omega_2 at a single common maximum bid bˉ\bar{b}, the upper supports must satisfy the exact proportionality constraint defining Class C1C_1 [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf):

ω2=a2a2+1a1+1a1ω1⟺bˉ=a2a2+1ω1=a1a1+1ω2\omega_2 = \frac{a_2}{a_2 + 1}\frac{a_1 + 1}{a_1} \omega_1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \bar{b} = \frac{a_2}{a_2 + 1}\omega_1 = \frac{a_1}{a_1 + 1}\omega_2

Under this calibration, the unique BNE strategies are globally linear [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf):

β1(x)=a2a2+1x,β2(x)=a1a1+1x\beta_1(x) = \frac{a_2}{a_2 + 1}x, \qquad \beta_2(x) = \frac{a_1}{a_1 + 1}x

This result yields a striking economic insight: in Cheng's Class C1C_1, Bidder 1's equilibrium bid shading factor a2a2+1\frac{a_2}{a_2 + 1} is determined entirely by Bidder 2's curvature parameter a2a_2 (behaving exactly as if competing in a symmetric auction where the rival had exponent a2a_2), while Bidder 2's shading factor a1a1+1\frac{a_1}{a_1 + 1} is determined entirely by Bidder 1's parameter a1a_1 [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). Because a1>a2a_1 > a_2, the strong bidder (Bidder 1, who has both a higher exponent a1>a2a_1 > a_2 and a wider support ω1>ω2\omega_1 > \omega_2) bids a strictly smaller fraction of their valuation (a2a2+1<a1a1+1\frac{a_2}{a_2 + 1} < \frac{a_1}{a_1 + 1}) than the weak bidder across all positive valuations [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). In the concrete parameterization highlighted by Hafalir and Krishna (2008) where a1=3,a2=1,ω1=1.5,a_1 = 3, a_2 = 1, \omega_1 = 1.5, and ω2=1.0\omega_2 = 1.0 (so F1(x)=(x/1.5)3F_1(x) = (x/1.5)^3 on [0,1.5][0, 1.5] and F2(x)=xF_2(x) = x on [0,1][0, 1]), the strong bidder shades bids by 50%50\% (β1(x)=0.50x\beta_1(x) = 0.50x) whereas the weak bidder shades by only 25%25\% (β2(x)=0.75x\beta_2(x) = 0.75x), yet both strategies terminate at the exact same common maximum bid of bˉ=0.75\bar{b} = 0.75 [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). Cheng (2007, 2010) also extended this technique to shifted power distributions (Class C2C_2) and characterized all environments admitting a linear equilibrium [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf).

## General Numerical Solution Methods for Arbitrary Continuous Distributions

For general pairs of distributions (F1,F2)(F_1, F_2)—such as lognormal, Weibull, beta, or truncated normal distributions commonly encountered in empirical structural econometrics—the coupled ODE system is analytically intractable and must be solved numerically [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Over the past three decades, the numerical literature has evolved through three distinct generations of algorithms to address the twin challenges of the unknown upper bound bˉ\bar{b} and the lower-boundary singularity at b‾\underline{b} [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf).

### Backward Shooting and Recursive Taylor Series (Marshall et al. 1994; Gayle and Richard 2008)

Because the ODE system is singular at the lower boundary b=b‾b = \underline{b} (preventing naive forward integration from b=b‾b = \underline{b}), Marshall, Meurer, Richard, and Stromquist (1994) pioneered the backward-shooting method, which became the workhorse numerical algorithm in the literature (used by Maskin and Riley 2000; Fibich and Gavious 2003; Li and Riley 2007; Gayle and Richard 2008) [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf).

The backward-shooting algorithm exploits the fact that at any candidate upper bound bˉε∈(b‾,min⁡{vˉ1,vˉ2})\bar{b}_\varepsilon \in (\underline{b}, \min\{\bar{v}_1, \bar{v}_2\}), the terminal boundary conditions ϕ1(bˉε)=vˉ1\phi_1(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) = \bar{v}_1 and ϕ2(bˉε)=vˉ2\phi_2(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) = \bar{v}_2 are regular and free of singularities [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). The procedure operates as a nested loop that begins with an outer bisection search bracketing the unknown maximum winning bid bˉ\bar{b} within [LB,UB][\text{LB}, \text{UB}] and selecting a candidate guess bˉε\bar{b}_\varepsilon [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). In the inner backward integration step, the solver imposes the terminal conditions ϕ1(bˉε)=vˉ1\phi_1(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) = \bar{v}_1 and ϕ2(bˉε)=vˉ2\phi_2(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) = \bar{v}_2 at b=bˉεb = \bar{b}_\varepsilon as "initial" values and integrates the coupled ODEs backwards in bid space (b<bˉεb < \bar{b}_\varepsilon) down toward the lower support b‾\underline{b} [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). The algorithm then evaluates the stopping criterion by recording the implied smallest winning bid b‾(bˉε)\underline{b}(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) where a bidder's inverse valuation hits its lower support or violates ϕi(b)≥b\phi_i(b) \ge b [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Because b‾(bˉε)\underline{b}(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) varies monotonically with the guess bˉε\bar{b}_\varepsilon—for example, in Shen et al.'s benchmark, guessing bˉ=8.5\bar{b} = 8.5 terminates at b‾(bˉ)=0.35\underline{b}(\bar{b}) = 0.35, whereas guessing bˉ=9.5\bar{b} = 9.5 terminates at b‾(bˉ)=3.76\underline{b}(\bar{b}) = 3.76—a binary search updates bˉε\bar{b}_\varepsilon until ∣b‾(bˉε)−b‾∣|\underline{b}(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) - \underline{b}| falls below a specified convergence tolerance [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions).

To execute the backward integration with high precision and accommodate coalition or multi-bidder heterogeneity (k1,k2k_1, k_2 coalitions or heterogeneous risk aversion αi\alpha_i), Marshall et al. (1994) transformed the inverse bid functions into dimensionless variables δi(t)≡tϕi(t)\delta_i(t) \equiv \frac{t}{\phi_i(t)}, converting the ODEs into a Cauchy system [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf):

Expanding δ1(t)=∑j=0∞aj(t−t0)j\delta_1(t) = \sum_{j=0}^\infty a_j (t - t_0)^j and δ2(t)=∑j=0∞bj(t−t0)j\delta_2(t) = \sum_{j=0}^\infty b_j (t - t_0)^j around successive base points t0t_0 stepping backward from bˉε\bar{b}_\varepsilon toward 00, they derived exact algebraic recursion relations for the Taylor coefficients (an+1,bn+1)(a_{n+1}, b_{n+1}) [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Gayle and Richard (2008) generalized this backward recursive Taylor-series technique to arbitrary non-uniform distributions FiF_i with reserve prices by defining the cumulative probability functions li(t)≡Fi(ϕi(t))l_i(t) \equiv F_i(\phi_i(t)) [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Since li′(t)=fi(ϕi(t))ϕi′(t)l_i'(t) = f_i(\phi_i(t))\phi_i'(t), the general KK-group ODE system transforms into [17](https://d.repec.org/n?u=RePEc:sce:scecf5:472&r=):

which is solved backward step-by-step via power-series expansions of li(t)l_i(t), li′(t)/li(t)l_i'(t)/l_i(t), and Fi−1(li(t))−tF_i^{-1}(l_i(t)) - t around each grid point t0t_0 [17](https://d.repec.org/n?u=RePEc:sce:scecf5:472&r=).

### The Analytic Instability Theorem of Backward Shooting and Forward/BVP Remedies (Fibich and Gavish 2011, 2012)

Although backward shooting was used for nearly two decades as the standard numerical method, Fibich and Gavish (2011, 2012) proved a fundamental result: the backward-shooting method is inherently unstable, and this instability is an analytic property of the differential equations rather than a numerical rounding artifact [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

To demonstrate the exact mechanism of this instability, Fibich and Gavish (2011) analyzed the backward-shooting trajectory on the symmetric benchmark (Fi(v)=F(v)F_i(v) = F(v) on [0,1][0, 1]), which shares the exact same origin singularity structure as the asymmetric system but admits an explicit closed-form solution for every perturbed guess bˉε=bˉ+ε\bar{b}_\varepsilon = \bar{b} + \varepsilon [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). In the exact equilibrium (ε=0\varepsilon = 0), the bidding strategy is b(v)=v−∫0vFn−1(s)dsFn−1(v)b(v) = v - \frac{\int_0^v F^{n-1}(s)ds}{F^{n-1}(v)} with maximum bid bˉ=1−∫01Fn−1(s)ds\bar{b} = 1 - \int_0^1 F^{n-1}(s)ds [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). If the backward-shooting algorithm initializes at a slightly perturbed terminal value vε(bˉε)=1v_\varepsilon(\bar{b}_\varepsilon) = 1 where bˉε=bˉ+ε\bar{b}_\varepsilon = \bar{b} + \varepsilon (∣ε∣≪1\vert{}\varepsilon\vert{} \ll 1), integrating the ODE analytically yields the exact perturbed bidding curve [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf):

bε(v)=b(v)+εFn−1(v)b_\varepsilon(v) = b(v) + \frac{\varepsilon}{F^{n-1}(v)}

This closed-form perturbation equation explains why backward shooting breaks down near the lower boundary [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Because F(v)→0F(v) \to 0 as v→0+v \to 0^+, the perturbation term εFn−1(v)\frac{\varepsilon}{F^{n-1}(v)} blows up to ±∞\pm\infty as the backward trajectory approaches the origin, regardless of how small the initial guess error ∣ε∣>0|\varepsilon| > 0 is [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). When ε>0\varepsilon > 0 (overestimating bˉ\bar{b}), as bb decreases toward 00, bε(v)b_\varepsilon(v) bends upward and violates the no-overbidding condition bε(v)≤vb_\varepsilon(v) \le v as soon as ∫0vFn−1(s)ds=ε\int_0^v F^{n-1}(s)ds = \varepsilon, after which bε′(v)b_\varepsilon'(v) changes sign at ∫0vmin⁡Fn−1(s)ds=ε\int_0^{v_{\min}} F^{n-1}(s)ds = \varepsilon and the trajectory diverges to +∞+\infty [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Conversely, when ε<0\varepsilon < 0 (underestimating bˉ\bar{b}), as bb decreases toward 00, bε(v)b_\varepsilon(v) plummets precipitously to zero at a strictly positive valuation vmin⁡>F−1(∣ε∣1n−1)v_{\min} > F^{-1}\left(|\varepsilon|^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\right), terminating prematurely before reaching v=0v = 0 [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

In the two-bidder uniform benchmark (F(v)=vF(v) = v on [0,1][0, 1], where n=2n = 2 and the true maximum bid is bˉ=0.50\bar{b} = 0.50), the exact perturbed trajectory is bε(v)=v2+εvb_\varepsilon(v) = \frac{v}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{v} [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). An error of just ε=+0.01\varepsilon = +0.01 at the upper boundary (bˉε=0.51\bar{b}_\varepsilon = 0.51) causes the backward trajectory to reach a local minimum where bε′(v)=12−εv2=0b_\varepsilon'(v) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{\varepsilon}{v^2} = 0 at vmin⁡=2ε≈0.1414v_{\min} = \sqrt{2\varepsilon} \approx 0.1414 (crossing the truthful bidding bound b=vb = v at the exact same point ∫0vs ds=v22=ε ⟹ v≈0.1414\int_0^v s\,ds = \frac{v^2}{2} = \varepsilon \implies v \approx 0.1414) before diverging toward +∞+\infty, whereas an error of ε=−0.01\varepsilon = -0.01 (bˉε=0.49\bar{b}_\varepsilon = 0.49) causes the trajectory to crash into bε(v)=0b_\varepsilon(v) = 0 prematurely at v=2∣ε∣≈0.1414v = \sqrt{2|\varepsilon|} \approx 0.1414 [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Because the singular perturbation scales as F−(n−1)(v)F^{-(n-1)}(v), the domain over which backward shooting can even be integrated shrinks algebraically in ∣ε∣|\varepsilon|; for steeper distributions (such as F(v)=vaF(v) = v^a with large aa) or larger nn, machine precision (∣ε∣≈10−16\vert{}\varepsilon\vert{} \approx 10^{-16}) is exhausted well before the backward trajectory reaches the origin [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

To eliminate backward-shooting instability, Fibich and Gavish (2011, 2012) introduced two alternative paradigms [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). The first remedy is asymptotic forward shooting: by deriving the exact local asymptotic expansion of the inverse bid functions near the singular origin b=0b = 0 (which depends on a free asymptotic parameter governing the higher-order departure from the universal slope ϕi′(0)=2\phi_i'(0) = 2), the researcher steps slightly away from b=0b = 0 to b=δ>0b = \delta > 0 analytically and integrates forward toward the upper boundary, shooting for the regular target ϕ1(bˉ)=vˉ1,ϕ2(bˉ)=vˉ2\phi_1(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_1, \phi_2(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_2 [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). Because the dynamical system is stable in the forward direction (perturbations decay rather than amplify as bb increases), forward shooting accurately resolves power-law and steep distributions where backward shooting fails [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). The second remedy is a fixed-domain boundary-value transformation, which changes variables or reformulates the system as a coupled boundary-value problem solved via global iterative relaxation (such as damped Newton iteration over a discretized mesh bridging both boundaries simultaneously), thereby avoiding the directional blow-up of initial-value shooting altogether [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf).

### Global Shape-Preserving Polynomial Collocation (Hubbard, Kirkegaard, and Paarsch 2013; Hubbard and Paarsch 2014)

The modern state-of-the-art numerical methodology in empirical and computational economics is economic-theory-guided polynomial collocation, developed by Hubbard, Kirkegaard, and Paarsch (2013) and surveyed in Hubbard and Paarsch (2014) [11](https://scispace.com/pdf/using-economic-theory-to-guide-numerical-analysis-solving-42njwmy51p.pdf). Rather than treating the two-point BVP as an initial value problem and integrating sequentially in one direction (shooting), collocation approximates and solves the entire equilibrium trajectory globally [11](https://scispace.com/pdf/using-economic-theory-to-guide-numerical-analysis-solving-42njwmy51p.pdf).

In this framework, the unknown bid interval [b‾,bˉ][\underline{b}, \bar{b}] is mapped onto a fixed reference interval (such as [−1,1][-1, 1] or [0,1][0, 1]) where the upper bound bˉ\bar{b} is treated as an explicit scalar parameter alongside the basis coefficients, and each unknown inverse bid function ϕi(b)\phi_i(b) is represented as a finite linear combination of MM orthogonal polynomials (typically Chebyshev polynomials) or B-splines [11](https://scispace.com/pdf/using-economic-theory-to-guide-numerical-analysis-solving-42njwmy51p.pdf):

ϕ^i(b;ci,bˉ)=∑k=0M−1ci,kTk(2(b−b‾)bˉ−b‾−1)\hat{\phi}_i(b; \mathbf{c}_i, \bar{b}) = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} c_{i,k} T_k\left(\frac{2(b - \underline{b})}{\bar{b} - \underline{b}} - 1\right)

The solver then selects interior collocation nodes {bm}m=1M−2\{b_m\}_{m=1}^{M-2} (such as Chebyshev Gauss–Lobatto nodes, which cluster naturally near the boundaries b‾\underline{b} and bˉ\bar{b} to prevent Runge oscillations) and constructs a system of nonlinear algebraic equations enforcing boundary and interior conditions simultaneously [11](https://scispace.com/pdf/using-economic-theory-to-guide-numerical-analysis-solving-42njwmy51p.pdf). Specifically, the exact lower boundary conditions ϕ^i(b‾)=v‾i\hat{\phi}_i(\underline{b}) = \underline{v}_i and upper boundary conditions ϕ^i(bˉ)=vˉi\hat{\phi}_i(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_i are imposed at the endpoints, while at the lower boundary b‾\underline{b}, the analytically known L'Hôpital slope condition ϕ^i′(b‾)=2\hat{\phi}_i'(\underline{b}) = 2 (for two bidders with positive initial density) replaces the indeterminate 0/00/0 ODE evaluation [1](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/numericalanalysis.pdf). Simultaneously, at all interior collocation nodes bmb_m, the cleared-denominator ODE residuals are set to zero:

Ri(bm;c1,c2,bˉ)≡ϕ^i′(bm)fi(ϕ^i(bm))(ϕ^j(bm)−bm)−Fi(ϕ^i(bm))=0R_i(b_m; \mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2, \bar{b}) \equiv \hat{\phi}_i'(b_m) f_i(\hat{\phi}_i(b_m))\left(\hat{\phi}_j(b_m) - b_m\right) - F_i(\hat{\phi}_i(b_m)) = 0

Because unconstrained nonlinear solvers can wander into non-monotonic or economically infeasible regions (where ϕ^i(b)<b\hat{\phi}_i(b) < b or ϕ^i′(b)≤0\hat{\phi}_i'(b) \le 0), Hubbard, Kirkegaard, and Paarsch (2013) impose theoretical shape restrictions directly on the optimization problem, enforcing strict monotonicity (ϕ^i′(bm)>0\hat{\phi}_i'(b_m) > 0), individual rationality (ϕ^i(bm)>bm\hat{\phi}_i(b_m) > b_m), and stochastic dominance ordering (ϕ^S(bm)>ϕ^W(bm)\hat{\phi}_S(b_m) > \hat{\phi}_W(b_m) under conditional stochastic dominance) [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf). Because collocation solves for all polynomial coefficients (c1,c2)(\mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2) and the free boundary bˉ\bar{b} simultaneously via constrained nonlinear least-squares or Newton–Raphson root-finding, error cannot accumulate exponentially from bˉ\bar{b} toward b‾\underline{b} [11](https://scispace.com/pdf/using-economic-theory-to-guide-numerical-analysis-solving-42njwmy51p.pdf). Consequently, collocation achieves spectral accuracy with a modest number of basis functions and remains computationally fast enough to be embedded inside structural econometric estimation loops [11](https://scispace.com/pdf/using-economic-theory-to-guide-numerical-analysis-solving-42njwmy51p.pdf).

## Alternative General Paradigms: Perturbation, Discrete Solvers, Resale Symmetrization, and Bounds

When exact numerical curves are either unnecessary for theoretical comparative statics or inapplicable due to discrete valuations or post-auction market structure, four additional general methodologies provide exact or asymptotic solutions.

### Asymptotic Perturbation Analysis for Weakly Asymmetric Bidders (Fibich and Gavious 2003)

In many economic applications, bidders are nearly homogeneous save for a small asymmetry parameter ε≪1\varepsilon \ll 1 [24](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/moor.28.4.836.20510). Fibich and Gavious (2003) developed a general perturbation methodology by writing each bidder's valuation distribution as a perturbation around a common baseline distribution F(v)F(v) on [0,1][0, 1] [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf):

Fi(v)=F(v)+εHi(v),i∈{1,2}F_i(v) = F(v) + \varepsilon H_i(v), \qquad i \in \{1, 2\}

Expanding the equilibrium bidding strategies (or inverse strategies) as an asymptotic power series in ε\varepsilon:

βi(v)=β0(v)+εβi,1(v)+ε2βi,2(v)+O(ε3)\beta_i(v) = \beta_0(v) + \varepsilon \beta_{i,1}(v) + \varepsilon^2 \beta_{i,2}(v) + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^3)

transforms the nonlinear coupled ODE system into a hierarchy of linear first-order ODEs at each order O(εk)\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^k) [24](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/moor.28.4.836.20510). At leading order O(1)\mathcal{O}(1), β0(v)=v−∫0vF(s)dsF(v)\beta_0(v) = v - \frac{\int_0^v F(s)ds}{F(v)} is the explicit symmetric equilibrium [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). At first order O(ε)\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon), the linear ODEs for β1,1(v)\beta_{1,1}(v) and β2,1(v)\beta_{2,1}(v) can be solved explicitly via integrating factors for any smooth baseline F(v)F(v) and perturbation functions H1(v),H2(v)H_1(v), H_2(v) [24](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/moor.28.4.836.20510). Crucially, Fibich and Gavious (2003) proved that the first-order revenue correction O(ε)\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon) depends only on the average distribution H1(v)+H2(v)2\frac{H_1(v) + H_2(v)}{2} (so revenue equivalence holds up to O(ε)\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon) for zero-mean perturbations where H1(v)=−H2(v)H_1(v) = -H_2(v)), while true asymmetric revenue differences between FPA and SPA emerge at second order O(ε2)\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^2) [24](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/moor.28.4.836.20510).

### Discrete Valuation Distributions and Mixed-Strategy Mass Points (Maskin and Riley 1985; Shen et al. 2019)

When the two bidders draw independent private values from discrete supports rather than continuous intervals—an environment of major practical importance in real-time computational ad exchanges where bids or valuations are quantized—the differential equation framework no longer applies directly because pure-strategy equilibria fail to exist [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). Instead, as shown by Maskin and Riley (1985) and solved algorithmically by Shen et al. (2019) and recent discrete-type analyses (2024), the unique BNE exhibits a hybrid mixed-strategy structure [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). Although valuation supports are discrete, equilibrium bids are randomized continuously over contiguous bid intervals (bk,bk+1)(b_k, b_{k+1}) without interior atoms or gaps above the minimum winning bid [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). For instance, with two i.i.d. buyers drawing values from {1,2}\{1, 2\} with probability 0.50.5, a buyer with value 11 bids 11 with probability 11, whereas a buyer with value 22 randomizes continuously over b∈[1,1.5]b \in [1, 1.5] with density g(b)=1(2−b)2g(b) = \frac{1}{(2 - b)^2} [34](https://www.weiran-shen.info/page_files/bayesian_nash_equilibrium_in_first-price_auction_with_discrete_value_distributions.pdf).

A critical structural feature of discrete asymmetric auctions is the emergence of probability mass points (atoms) at the minimum bid vLv_L [43](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/research/math/papers/465.pdf). Consider two asymmetric bidders drawing values from a discrete ternary support {vL,vM,vH}\{v_L, v_M, v_H\} (where vM=vL+A,vH=vM+Av_M = v_L + A, v_H = v_M + A) with probabilities (θi,μi,1−θi−μi)(\theta_i, \mu_i, 1 - \theta_i - \mu_i) for i∈{1,2}i \in \{1, 2\} [43](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/research/math/papers/465.pdf). Both low types 1L1L and 2L2L bid vLv_L as a pure strategy (ρi≡Gi(vL)≥θi\rho_i \equiv G_i(v_L) \ge \theta_i), and both high types 1H1H and 2H2H share the same common maximum bid bˉH\bar{b}_H [43](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/research/math/papers/465.pdf). However, because asymmetry skews the winning probabilities at the bottom of the support, one of the middle types (1M1M or 2M2M, but never both simultaneously) places a strictly positive probability mass on the minimum bid vLv_L (ρi>θi\rho_i > \theta_i while ρj=θj\rho_j = \theta_j) [43](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/research/math/papers/465.pdf). In extreme asymmetry where θ1+μ1+μ2<θ2\theta_1 + \mu_1 + \mu_2 < \theta_2, type 1M1M bids vLv_L with probability 11, and even the high type 1H1H places a positive probability mass atom at vLv_L to preserve type 2H2H's indifference across its mixing support [43](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/research/math/papers/465.pdf).

Furthermore, under standard uniform random tie-breaking, discrete asymmetric settings can suffer from non-existence of BNE due to open-set best-response failures (for example, when Buyer 2 always bids 11 and Buyer 1 has value 22, Buyer 1 prefers to bid 1+ϵ1 + \epsilon for infinitesimally small ϵ>0\epsilon > 0, yielding no best response at ϵ=0\epsilon = 0) [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). Imposing Maskin and Riley's Vickrey tie-breaking rule (where ties are broken via a second-price auction among tied high bidders) restores exact BNE existence [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). Shen et al. (2019) provide a general discrete backward-shooting algorithm that tracks the active "bidding set" Λ(x)={i∣x∈Si}\Lambda(x) = \{i \mid x \in S_i\} and waiting list N∖Λ(x)N \setminus \Lambda(x), solving for the piece-wise differentiable mixed-strategy CDFs Gi(x)G_i(x) from a guessed bˉ\bar{b} down to b‾\underline{b} via binary search without requiring ODE integration [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions).

### Structural Symmetrization via Post-Auction Resale (Hafalir and Krishna 2008)

One of the most profound analytical discoveries in modern auction theory is that introducing post-auction resale completely eliminates the analytical intractability of the two-bidder asymmetric first-price auction [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf). Because the standard asymmetric FPA is allocatively inefficient—the weak bidder bids more aggressively (βW(v)>βS(v)\beta_W(v) > \beta_S(v)) and can win the object even when vW<vSv_W < v_S—there exist strict post-auction gains from trade whenever the weak bidder wins [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf).

In Hafalir and Krishna's (2008) First-Price Auction with Resale (FPAR) model, after the winning bid bb is announced, a winning weak bidder with value vWv_W inferentially knows that the losing strong bidder's value satisfies VS≤ϕS(b)V_S \le \phi_S(b) and makes an optimal take-it-or-leave-it resale offer p(b)p(b) to maximize expected resale profit [FS(ϕS(b))−FS(p)]p+FS(p)vW[F_S(\phi_S(b)) - F_S(p)]p + F_S(p)v_W [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf). Under regular distributions FS,FWF_S, F_W, the optimal monopoly resale price p(b)p(b) satisfies the conditional virtual-value equation [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf):

p(b)−FS(ϕS(b))−FS(p(b))fS(p(b))=ϕW(b)p(b) - \frac{F_S(\phi_S(b)) - F_S(p(b))}{f_S(p(b))} = \phi_W(b)

When evaluating the first-order condition in the initial auction, a remarkable symmetrization effect occurs: the marginal benefit of slightly increasing one's bid bb is identical for both the weak bidder and the strong bidder, and equals p(b)−bp(b) - b [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf). For the weak bidder (the potential reseller), p(b)−bp(b) - b is the net resale profit earned by winning at bb and reselling at p(b)p(b); for the strong bidder (the potential buyer), p(b)−bp(b) - b is the exact savings from winning the auction directly at price bb rather than losing and paying the retail resale price p(b)p(b) [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf). Consequently, the coupled ODE system collapses into two identical logarithmic derivatives [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf):

ddbln⁡FS(ϕS(b))=1p(b)−b=ddbln⁡FW(ϕW(b))\frac{d}{db} \ln F_S(\phi_S(b)) = \frac{1}{p(b) - b} = \frac{d}{db} \ln F_W(\phi_W(b))

Because both bidders share the same upper boundary condition FS(ϕS(bˉ))=FW(ϕW(bˉ))=1F_S(\phi_S(\bar{b})) = F_W(\phi_W(\bar{b})) = 1 at the maximum bid bˉ\bar{b}, integrating both equations immediately implies that the equilibrium bid distributions of the strong and weak bidders are identical across all b∈[0,bˉ]b \in [0, \bar{b}] [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf):

FS(ϕS(b))=FW(ϕW(b))F_S(\phi_S(b)) = F_W(\phi_W(b))

This identity allows the asymmetric FPAR to be solved in closed form for any regular distributions FS,FWF_S, F_W through a three-stage analytical construction [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf). First, the analyst constructs the unique symmetrized "effective" distribution F(p)F(p) (which lies strictly between FS(p)F_S(p) and FW(p)F_W(p)) directly from the algebraic equation [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf):

F(p)=FW(p−F(p)−FS(p)fS(p))F(p) = F_W\left(p - \frac{F(p) - F_S(p)}{f_S(p)}\right)

Second, the analyst computes the standard symmetric FPA bidding strategy β(p)=1F(p)∫0pyf(y)dy\beta(p) = \frac{1}{F(p)} \int_0^p y f(y) dy for two symmetric bidders drawing values from F(p)F(p) [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf). Third, the exact asymmetric inverse bidding strategies ϕS(b)\phi_S(b) and ϕW(b)\phi_W(b) are recovered by mapping FS(ϕS(b))=FW(ϕW(b))=F(β−1(b))F_S(\phi_S(b)) = F_W(\phi_W(b)) = F(\beta^{-1}(b)) [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf).

For example, when VS∼U[0,aS]V_S \sim U[0, a_S] and VW∼U[0,aW]V_W \sim U[0, a_W] (aS>aWa_S > a_W), whereas the standard FPA without resale requires the nonlinear Griesmer–Levitan–Shubik square-root strategies, the FPAR equilibrium inverse strategies are purely linear: ϕS(b)=4aSaS+aWb\phi_S(b) = \frac{4 a_S}{a_S + a_W}b and ϕW(b)=4aWaS+aWb\phi_W(b) = \frac{4 a_W}{a_S + a_W}b with maximum bid bˉ=aS+aW4\bar{b} = \frac{a_S + a_W}{4} [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf). Notably, whenever aS>3aWa_S > 3 a_W, ϕW(b)<b\phi_W(b) < b (βW(vW)>vW\beta_W(v_W) > v_W), meaning the weak bidder rationally bids above their own private valuation to capture speculative resale rents from the strong bidder [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf).

### Qualitative Comparative Statics and Distribution-Free Bounds Without Solving the ODEs

Finally, a substantial literature extracts rigorous structural predictions directly from the ODE system and best-response inequalities without computing ϕi(b)\phi_i(b) explicitly [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf). Suppose Bidder SS's distribution dominates Bidder WW's via Conditional Stochastic Dominance—defined by hazard-rate dominance ddv(FS(v)FW(v))>0\frac{d}{dv}\left(\frac{F_S(v)}{F_W(v)}\right) > 0, or equivalently fS(v)FS(v)>fW(v)FW(v)\frac{f_S(v)}{F_S(v)} > \frac{f_W(v)}{F_W(v)} for all v∈(v‾,vˉ)v \in (\underline{v}, \bar{v}) [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf). Using either boundary-value comparison arguments (Maskin and Riley 2000) or the ratio of winning probabilities R(v)=qW(v)qS(v)R(v) = \frac{q_W(v)}{q_S(v)} and payoff difference D(v)=EUS(v)−EUW(v)D(v) = EU_S(v) - EU_W(v) where D′(v)=qS(v)−qW(v)D'(v) = q_S(v) - q_W(v) (Kirkegaard 2009), three universal comparative static properties emerge directly from the ODEs [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf). First, the weak bidder bids strictly more aggressively for any given valuation (βW(v)>βS(v)\beta_W(v) > \beta_S(v), or ϕW(b)<ϕS(b)\phi_W(b) < \phi_S(b)) on the interior of the common support [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf). Second, despite shading more heavily, the strong bidder's equilibrium distribution of submitted bids first-order stochastically dominates the weak bidder's (FS(ϕS(b))<FW(ϕW(b))F_S(\phi_S(b)) < F_W(\phi_W(b)) for all b∈(b‾,bˉ)b \in (\underline{b}, \bar{b})) [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf). Third, because qW(v)>FS(v)q_W(v) > F_S(v) and qS(v)<FW(v)q_S(v) < F_W(v), the weak bidder wins more frequently in the FPA than in the efficient open/second-price auction (SPA), whereas the strong bidder wins less frequently; consequently, for n=2n = 2, the strong bidder strictly prefers the SPA, while the weak bidder strictly prefers the FPA [3](https://brocku.ca/repec/pdf/0504.pdf).

In parallel, researchers in algorithmic game theory bypass the analytical intractability of the asymmetric ODE system by bounding the worst-case ratio of BNE social welfare to optimal social welfare (competitive efficiency) across all independent prior distributions F=(F1,…,Fn)\mathbf{F} = (F_1, \dots, F_n) [21](http://samueltaggart.com/files/htw18.pdf). Syrgkanis and Tardos (2013) established a baseline smoothness welfare bound of 1−1e≈0.6321 - \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.632 [21](http://samueltaggart.com/files/htw18.pdf). By decomposing welfare into contributions from "rightful winners" (who win and possess the highest valuation) and "improper winners" (who win due to asymmetric bid shading despite having a lower valuation) and parameterizing best-response inequalities by distribution quantiles qi=Fi(vi)q_i = F_i(v_i), Hoy, Taggart, and Wang (2018) proved that any BNE of an asymmetric IPV first-price auction achieves at least 0.7430.743 (74.3%74.3\%) of the optimal social welfare [21](http://samueltaggart.com/files/htw18.pdf). Similarly, Bergemann, Brooks, and Morris (2017) derived tight lower bounds on winning bid distributions and seller revenue across all common-prior information structures [2](https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3456&context=cowles-discussion-paper-series).

## Methodological Synthesis and Practitioner Decision Protocol

When confronted with a specific two-bidder ex-ante asymmetric first-price auction, selecting the appropriate solution method follows a structured diagnostic hierarchy dictated by the analytical properties of the valuation priors (F1,F2)(F_1, F_2). The initial diagnostic step is to verify support overlap and determine the lower boundary b‾\underline{b} by ordering the bidders such that v‾1≤v‾2\underline{v}_1 \le \underline{v}_2 and computing the entry threshold b‾=max⁡{m,arg⁡max⁡b≥v‾2(v‾2−b)F1(b)}\underline{b} = \max\left\{m, \arg\max_{b \ge \underline{v}_2} (\underline{v}_2 - b)F_1(b)\right\} [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). If b‾≥vˉ1\underline{b} \ge \bar{v}_1 (such as when v‾2≥2vˉ1−v‾1\underline{v}_2 \ge 2\bar{v}_1 - \underline{v}_1 under uniform distributions), the interior differential equations do not apply; instead, the unique equilibrium is the corner pooling solution where Bidder 1 bids truthfully (β1(v1)=v1\beta_1(v_1) = v_1) and Bidder 2 pools at β2(v2)=vˉ1\beta_2(v_2) = \bar{v}_1 [5](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/riley/research/asyRES.PDF).

If b‾<vˉ1\underline{b} < \bar{v}_1 so that an interior equilibrium exists, the second step is to test for parametric first-integral solvability. Whenever both distributions are uniform on arbitrary intervals [v‾i,vˉi][\underline{v}_i, \bar{v}_i], the researcher should apply the Kaplan and Zamir (2012) conservation law ϕ1(b)ϕ2(b)=b(ϕ1(b)+ϕ2(b)−v‾1−v‾2)+c\phi_1(b)\phi_2(b) = b(\phi_1(b) + \phi_2(b) - \underline{v}_1 - \underline{v}_2) + c—or its special case, the Griesmer, Levitan, and Shubik (1967) formula when v‾1=v‾2=0\underline{v}_1 = \underline{v}_2 = 0—to obtain an exact closed-form solution [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf). Similarly, if both distributions belong to the power-law family Fi(x)=(x/ωi)aiF_i(x) = (x/\omega_i)^{a_i}, the analyst can apply Plum's (1992) Class PP closed-form solution when a1=a2a_1 = a_2, or Cheng's (2006) Class C1C_1 linear solution βi(x)=ajaj+1x\beta_i(x) = \frac{a_j}{a_j + 1}x when the supports satisfy ω2=a2(a1+1)a1(a2+1)ω1\omega_2 = \frac{a_2(a_1+1)}{a_1(a_2+1)}\omega_1 [8](https://capcp.la.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/Working%20Papers/2008/HafalirKrishna2008.pdf).

When (F1,F2)(F_1, F_2) are general non-uniform continuous distributions (such as lognormal, Weibull, or empirical kernel estimates) that do not admit a known first integral, the third step is to formulate the canonical inverse-bid ODE system ϕi′(b)=Fi(ϕi(b))fi(ϕi(b))(ϕj(b)−b)\phi_i'(b) = \frac{F_i(\phi_i(b))}{f_i(\phi_i(b))(\phi_j(b) - b)} on [b‾,bˉ][\underline{b}, \bar{b}] and apply a stable numerical BVP solver [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Practitioners should avoid naive backward shooting, which suffers from analytic blow-up proportional to ε/Fi(ϕi(b))\varepsilon / F_i(\phi_i(b)) near b‾\underline{b} [4](http://www.math.tau.ac.il/~fibich/Manuscripts/Numerical-simulations-of-asymmetric-first-price-auctions.pdf). Instead, the two-point free-boundary BVP should be solved globally using economic-theory-guided Chebyshev polynomial collocation (Hubbard, Kirkegaard, and Paarsch 2013)—enforcing ϕi′(b‾)=2\phi_i'(\underline{b}) = 2, ϕi(b‾)=v‾i\phi_i(\underline{b}) = \underline{v}_i, ϕi(bˉ)=vˉi\phi_i(\bar{b}) = \bar{v}_i, and ϕi′(b)>0\phi_i'(b) > 0 simultaneously—or via asymptotic forward shooting (Fibich and Gavish 2011) when operating within an ODE integration framework [11](https://scispace.com/pdf/using-economic-theory-to-guide-numerical-analysis-solving-42njwmy51p.pdf).

Finally, when specialized structural conditions characterize the environment, researchers can bypass full numerical BVP integration entirely. For small departures from symmetry (Fi=F+εHiF_i = F + \varepsilon H_i), Fibich and Gavious's (2003) second-order perturbation expansion yields closed-form comparative statics and revenue rankings [24](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/moor.28.4.836.20510). For discrete valuation grids, enforcing Vickrey tie-breaking and applying discrete bidding-set backward shooting (Shen et al. 2019) while accounting for mass points at vLv_L recovers the exact mixed-strategy BNE [18](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333993469_Bayesian_Nash_Equilibrium_in_First-Price_Auction_with_Discrete_Value_Distributions). And when post-auction resale is institutionally feasible, exploiting Hafalir and Krishna's (2008) symmetrization theorem (FS(ϕS(b))=FW(ϕW(b))F_S(\phi_S(b)) = F_W(\phi_W(b))) reduces the asymmetric two-bidder auction to an exact, analytically solvable symmetric equivalent [16](https://www.mdc.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/wp/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Asymmetric-Auctions-with-Resale.pdf).
