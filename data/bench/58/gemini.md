# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: Mechanistic Gateways, Adaptive Shortcuts, and Evolutionary Significance in Non-Microbial Systems

## The Eukaryotic Paradox: Why Horizontal Gene Transfer Transcends Microbial Boundaries

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)—also termed lateral gene transfer (LGT)—is the non-sexual movement of genetic information between distinct genomes across species boundaries [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf). In bacteria and archaea, HGT is a foundational driver of adaptive evolution, contributing 10% to 20% of the total gene repertoire across many prokaryotic genomes and facilitating the rapid dissemination of antibiotic resistance, virulence factors, nitrogen fixation, and metabolic versatility [2](https://www.abcam.com/en-us/knowledge-center/dna-and-rna/gene-transfer-types-mechanisms-and-methods). Prokaryotes maintain specialized molecular machinery dedicated to genetic exchange, including natural transformation (uptake of exogenous environmental DNA), viral transduction via bacteriophages, plasmid-mediated conjugation through cell-to-cell pili or Type IV secretion systems, and virus-like gene transfer agents (GTAs) encoded by alphaproteobacteria such as the Rhodobacterales [2](https://www.abcam.com/en-us/knowledge-center/dna-and-rna/gene-transfer-types-mechanisms-and-methods).

Historically, evolutionary biology operated under the assumption that once the primordial endosymbiotic mergers that founded the mitochondrion and plastid were completed, multicellular eukaryotes—specifically land plants (Embryophyta) and animals (Metazoa)—evolved almost exclusively through vertical parent-to-offspring inheritance, point mutation, gene duplication, and meiotic recombination [3](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article/58/1/1/515544). Even in prokaryotes, functional HGT is statistically rare relative to vertical inheritance because a foreign gene must navigate an improbable sequence of events: physical entry into the recipient cell, stable chromosomal integration, transcriptional activation by host polymerases, accurate translation and protein folding, and precise subcellular targeting [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf). In multicellular eukaryotes, these hurdles are compounded by chromatin packaging, spliceosomal intron processing, epigenetic silencing, and the physical segregation of reproductive cells (the Weismann barrier) [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf). Furthermore, unicellular eukaryotes (protists) and fungi exhibit substantially higher HGT frequencies than metazoans and vascular plants, largely because phagotrophic protists engulf microbial prey—releasing foreign DNA directly adjacent to the host nucleus via Doolittle's "you are what you eat" gene ratchet mechanism—and lack a sequestered germline [3](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article/58/1/1/515544). Even among phagotrophic protists, however, functional HGT typically accounts for less than 1% of the nuclear genome, because eukaryotic adaptation often selects for complex morphological and behavioral shifts—such as altering cytoskeletal architecture to engulf larger prey—that cannot be conferred by single foreign metabolic genes [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf).

Despite these formidable structural and selective filters, high-resolution comparative phylogenomics over the past fifteen years has overturned the dogma that plant and animal genomes are closed systems [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf). While HGT is quantitatively rare in plants and animals compared to microbes, its evolutionary impact is disproportionately profound [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf). Rather than occurring randomly across all functional categories, eukaryotic HGT acts as a high-leverage evolutionary shortcut [14](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Luke-Dunning). Because vertical evolution is constrained by standing genetic variation and the slow pace of stepwise mutational optimization, multicellular lineages facing acute ecological crises—such as toxic host-plant defenses, sub-zero marine environments, indestructible plant cell walls, or desiccation and canopy shading—occasionally capture pre-evolved, functionally optimized genes from cohabiting organisms [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). Once integrated into the germline, a single horizontally transferred gene (xenolog) can unlock an entirely novel ecological niche, catalyze adaptive radiation, or trigger massive post-transfer gene family amplification [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer).

## Mechanistic Gateways: Overcoming the Germline Barrier in Multicellular Systems

For a horizontal transfer event to persist across evolutionary time in a plant or animal, exogenous genetic material must exit the donor nucleus or cell, survive extracellular degradation, breach the recipient's cellular and nuclear membranes, integrate into host chromosomes, and enter the germline to be transmitted to subsequent generations [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf). While the isolation of germ cells in complex multicellular organisms acts as a stringent filter against somatic acquisitions, comparative genomic evidence demonstrates that no barrier to HGT is insurmountable [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf).

### The Weak-Link Model and Developmental Exposure

To explain how foreign genes systematically enter the chromosomes of complex eukaryotes, Jinling Huang formulated the **weak-link model**  of eukaryotic HGT [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf). The model recognizes that even the most complex multicellular plants and animals pass through unicellular or poorly protected early developmental bottlenecks—specifically gametes, spores, zygotes, and early-stage embryos—during which nuclear DNA is relatively exposed to the external environment or intracellular microbes [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf). If exogenous DNA integrates into a recipient chromosome during these weakly protected stages, subsequent mitotic cell divisions or fertilization automatically propagate the xenolog into both somatic tissues and the nascent germline of the resulting offspring [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf). Furthermore, in plants and basal metazoans capable of asexual propagation or vegetative regeneration, any somatic cell that incorporates foreign DNA and subsequently differentiates into a reproductive meristem or vegetative propagule transmits the foreign sequence directly to future generations [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf).

This developmental vulnerability explains why vertebrate-to-vertebrate HGT events are heavily concentrated in teleost fishes [5](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/42/5/msaf107/8133933). The aquatic environment combined with external fertilization creates an ideal physical conduit: during mass spawning events, naked fish eggs and sperm are bathed in water containing cell-free DNA, viral particles, and heterospecific sperm, facilitating sperm-mediated or viral-mediated gene transfer directly into the newly fertilized zygote [5](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/42/5/msaf107/8133933).

### Plant-Specific Gateways: Haustoria, Grafting, eccDNA, and Organellar Fusion

Plants possess distinct anatomical and developmental traits that create specialized conduits for interspecies genetic exchange [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). Because plant meristems retain pluripotency throughout the life of the organism and frequently differentiate into floral reproductive structures or wound-induced adventitious shoots ("suckers"), somatic DNA transfers in plants have a direct pathway into the germline [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil). In parasitic angiosperms—such as members of Orobanchaceae, Convolvulaceae, and Rafflesiaceae—this vulnerability is amplified by the haustorium, a specialized invasive organ that penetrates host tissues and establishes a direct physiological bridge connecting the xylem and/or phloem of the parasite to its host [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). This vascular continuity permits the massive, bidirectional trafficking of macromolecules, including genomic DNA fragments, viral RNA, and thousands of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) across the haustorial interface [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). While systemic movement of host mRNAs beyond the immediate haustorial contact zone is restricted, occasional reverse transcription of mobile mRNAs or direct uptake of genomic DNA near meristematic tissues results in permanent nuclear and mitochondrial HGT [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/).

In non-parasitic plants, mechanical contact between roots or stems of different species can result in natural grafting, defined by the physical fusion of vascular and cortical tissues [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). Experimental work in transgenic *Nicotiana*  (tobacco) lines demonstrates that nuclear DNA and entire organelles, such as plastids, move bidirectionally across graft junctions [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). While inter-familial grafting is physiologically incompatible, intra-familial root contact and illegitimate pollination (pollen tube growth into heterospecific styles) provide plausible routes for nuclear HGT among closely cohabiting plants such as grasses [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). Additionally, extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA)—which exits the donor nucleus and circulates with high structural stability—has been identified as a potent vehicle for transferring large genomic blocks between grass species [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). Soil bacteria of the genus *Agrobacterium*  (*A. tumefaciens*  and *A. rhizogenes* ) provide a further prokaryotic-to-plant vector, utilizing a conjugation-derived Type IV secretion system (VirB/VirD4) to inject transfer DNA (T-DNA) from their tumor-inducing (Ti) or root-inducing (Ri) plasmids directly into the plant host nucleus [2](https://www.abcam.com/en-us/knowledge-center/dna-and-rna/gene-transfer-types-mechanisms-and-methods). When transformed root or cambial cells spontaneously regenerate into whole plants, these bacterial T-DNA sequences become permanently fixed in the plant species [3](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article/58/1/1/515544).

Whereas plastid genomes (ptDNA) and animal mitochondrial genomes are virtually impervious to exogenous HGT, land plant mitochondrial genomes (mtDNA) exhibit extraordinary horizontal promiscuity [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil). Plant mitochondria possess an active macromolecular DNA uptake system and routinely undergo organelle fusion [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil). Under the **fusion-compatibility model**  proposed by Rice and Palmer, when foreign green plant cells (from epiphytic mosses, green algae, or parasitic plants) breach a host plant via wounding or haustoria, donor mitochondria are captured into the recipient cytoplasm and fuse with native mitochondria [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil). This fusion compatibility is mechanistically restricted to Viridiplantae (green plant) mitochondria, explaining why plant mitogenomes readily incorporate whole mitochondrial genomes from mosses, green algae, and other angiosperms, but never from fungi, bacteria, or animals [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil).

### Animal and Cross-Kingdom Gateways: Endosymbionts, Polydnaviruses, and Parasitic Vectors

In metazoans, early sequestration of the germline isolates reproductive tissues from the external environment, meaning that heritable HGT nearly always requires an intimate biological vector capable of penetrating internal tissues and reaching the gonads [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf). Arthropods and nematodes frequently harbor maternally transmitted intracellular bacteria—such as *Wolbachia* , *Hamiltonella defensa* , *Arsenophonus* , and *Portiera* —inside specialized cells (bacteriocytes) or directly within developing oocytes in the ovaries [26](https://www.maxapress.com/article/doi/10.48130/TP-2023-0003?viewType=HTML). Because these bacteria reside inside the germline across millions of host generations, lysed bacterial chromosomes or bacteriophage DNA (such as APSE phages infecting *H. defensa* ) are continually released adjacent to the host oocyte nucleus, facilitating frequent prokaryote-to-animal HGT [26](https://www.maxapress.com/article/doi/10.48130/TP-2023-0003?viewType=HTML).

Viruses also act as natural genomic shuttles across animal phyla [15](https://www.inverse.com/science/plant-dna-found-in-animal-dna-study). The most sophisticated viral HGT conduit in animals involves the *Polydnaviridae*  (PDVs), comprising Bracoviruses (BVs) and Ichnoviruses (IVs), which evolved convergently in endoparasitoid wasps [39](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstb/article/368/1626/20130051/30117/When-parasitic-wasps-hijacked-viruses-genomic-and). Approximately 100 million years ago, an ancestor of the microgastroid braconid wasp complex integrated a nudivirus genome into its chromosomes [25](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/7/evag150/8716239). The viral structural genes remain permanently fixed in the wasp genome and are expressed exclusively in specialized calyx cells of the wasp ovaries to build viral capsids [25](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/7/evag150/8716239). Rather than packaging the nudiviral structural genes, the wasp excises 25 to 35 dispersed proviral segments from its own chromosomes at Direct Repeat Junctions (DRJs), forming circular double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) molecules packed with wasp virulence genes [25](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/7/evag150/8716239). When the female wasp oviposits into a lepidopteran, orthopteran, or stick insect host, she injects these bracovirus particles [25](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/7/evag150/8716239). Although the virus cannot replicate in the caterpillar, its dsDNA circles actively integrate into the host insect's nuclear chromosomes via conserved **Host Integration Motifs (HIMs)** , utilizing double-strand breaks at specific Junction 1 (J1) and Junction 2 (J2) sites [25](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/7/evag150/8716239). When a parasitized host survives wasp attack and reproduces, these integrated wasp-viral segments become stably inherited in the host lineage [41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13315421/).

In addition to endosymbionts and viruses, blood-feeding and tissue-rasping parasites—most notably ixodid ticks, marine acorn worms, and trematode flatworms (*Schistosoma mansoni*  cycling between mammalian definitive hosts and freshwater snail intermediate hosts)—serve as mechanical and biological vectors that transfer transposable elements (TEs) between distantly related vertebrate and invertebrate phyla [21](https://www.inverse.com/science/46816-evolution-species-jumping-genes). At the cellular level within mammals, newly characterized intercellular mediators—including exosomes, extracellular vesicles (EVs), apoptotic bodies, and circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA)—transport genetic material horizontally between somatic cells, driving tumor microenvironment remodeling, drug resistance, and genomic instability [29](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cell-and-developmental-biology/articles/10.3389/fcell.2020.00229/full).

## Landmark Empirical Cases in Plants: Metabolic Shortcuts, Organellar Chimerae, and Natural Transgenesis

### Evolutionary Shortcuts to C4\text{C}_4 Photosynthesis and Genomic Blocks in Grasses (*Poaceae* )

Outside of parasitic plant systems, over 95% of all documented plant-to-plant nuclear HGT events occur within the grass family (*Poaceae* ) [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12448838/). The premier model system illuminating this phenomenon is the paleotropical grass *Alloteropsis semialata* , the only known angiosperm species that encompasses C3\text{C}_3, C3–C4\text{C}_3\text{–C}_4 intermediate, and full C4\text{C}_4 photosynthetic populations within a single species [13](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1810031116).

Evolving C4\text{C}_4 photosynthesis—a complex biochemical and anatomical carbon-concentrating adaptation to hot, dry, and low-CO2\text{CO}_2 environments—typically requires both developmental leaf modifications (such as high bundle sheath tissue proportions >15%>15\% and increased minor vein density) and the step-wise duplication, upregulation, and kinetic optimization of core metabolic enzymes [14](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Luke-Dunning). Rather than evolving these biochemical kinetics entirely from scratch via vertical point mutations, separate populations of *A. semialata*  horizontally acquired three distinct genes encoding the core C4\text{C}_4 enzymes **phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC)**  and **phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK)**  from distantly related C4\text{C}_4 grass clades (including *Themeda triandra*  and panicoid lineages that diverged from *Alloteropsis*  20 to 40 million years ago) [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). Because these donor genes had already undergone millions of years of positive selection for optimal C4\text{C}_4 catalytic efficiency and high expression levels in older C4\text{C}_4 lineages, their horizontal acquisition provided *A. semialata*  with an immediate biochemical shortcut and a measurable demographic advantage across warm, arid landscapes [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383).

To determine whether these photosynthetic transfers were isolated anomalies or part of a broader genomic process, Dunning et al. assembled a chromosome-level genome for *A. semialata*  and screened it against genomic data from 146 other grass species [13](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1810031116). The analysis uncovered 59 protein-coding LGTs in the reference genome clustered within 23 large, laterally acquired genomic fragments measuring up to 170 kb in length, originating from at least nine different grass donor species [13](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1810031116). Because these foreign fragments carry multiple physically linked genes, functionally neutral or non-essential genes frequently "hitchhike" into the recipient population alongside a strongly selected beneficial gene, subsequently evolving novel functions in their new genomic context [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf).

Recent pan-genomic, transcriptomic, and bisulfite sequencing studies across five *A. semialata*  reference genomes—encompassing 55 Illumina RNA-Seq datasets across leaf base, leaf tip, leaf sheath, and root tissues—have revealed the molecular fate of these foreign blocks after integration [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). Out of 282 laterally acquired genes identified across the five accessions, 208 (73.8%, with a per-genome mean of 77.5%±8.3%77.5\% \pm 8.3\%) are transcriptionally expressed at ≥0.5 TPM\ge 0.5\text{ TPM} in at least one tissue [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). Rather than replacing native genes via homologous recombination, 72.1% (±10.7%\pm 10.7\%) of laterally acquired genes coexist in the genome alongside their vertically inherited homologs at entirely non-syntenic chromosomal locations, and only 10.3% (±9.1%\pm 9.1\%) of laterally acquired genes show recent duplication compared to a significantly higher rate in vertical homologs (P=0.008P = 0.008, Wilcoxon rank sum test) [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). Furthermore, the presence of a native vertical homolog has no suppressive effect on whether the foreign gene is expressed (P=0.830P = 0.830, Pearson χ2\chi^2 test) [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383).

A critical question in plant genomics is how host genomes regulate sudden influxes of foreign DNA [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). Bisulfite sequencing of *A. semialata*  leaf tissue demonstrated that laterally acquired genes exhibit a statistically significant **2.7% mean increase in CpG cytosine methylation**  across the gene body (P=0.001P = 0.001), exons (P=0.004P = 0.004), and 1 kb downstream regions (P=0.026P = 0.026) compared to their vertically inherited homologs [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). This confirms that plant epigenetic machinery subjects natural HGT insertions to the same methylation-mediated transcriptional dampening observed in engineered transgenic crops, explaining why most newly acquired LGTs are expressed at relatively low baseline levels unless positive selection favors high-expression variants [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383).

Similar post-transfer evolutionary dynamics occur across other grass lineages. Screening chromosome-level assemblies of 21 diploid wild barley (*Hordeum* ) species and 77 Panicoideae accessions revealed that a papain-like cysteine peptidase gene was transferred as part of a panicoid DNA segment from *Panicum bergii*  into *Hordeum*  section *Stenostachys*  [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520124/). Following integration, the foreign locus amplified into 1 to 8 copies per species, accumulating transposable element insertions and coding variants [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520124/). While most duplicates degraded into non-catalytic pseudopeptidases, a subset retained an intact catalytic triad, underwent episodic positive selection, and maintained active transcription across tissues—demonstrating how a single HGT block can seed a diversifying, regulated multigene family [19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520124/). Additionally, systematic scans of Nuclear Plastid DNA (NUPTs) in *A. semialata*  identified foreign chloroplast fragments transferred laterally from donors such as *Eragrostis attenuata*  (100% bootstrap support), establishing NUPTs as high-resolution forensic markers for tracing grass-to-grass HGT donors [14](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Luke-Dunning).

### Organellar Promiscuity and Chimeric Recombination in *Amborella trichopoda*

While grass HGT operates primarily on nuclear genomic blocks, the most extreme accumulation of horizontally transferred DNA in any eukaryotic organelle occurs in the mitochondrial genome of *Amborella trichopoda* , an understory shrub endemic to the cloud forests of New Caledonia that represents the sister lineage to all other extant flowering plants (diverging ~200 million years ago) [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil).

Early PCR surveys of the 40 ancestral angiosperm mitochondrial protein genes recovered 31 genes from *Amborella* , 20 of which possessed one or more foreign HGT copies alongside *Amborella* 's native vertically inherited copies (amounting to 26 foreign protein genes in that initial screen) [33](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article-pdf/58/1/1/1382263/erl148.pdf). Subsequent complete sequencing of the *Amborella*  mitogenome revealed a colossal **3.9 Mb circular-mapping genome containing roughly six complete mitochondrial genome equivalents of foreign DNA**  acquired from three distinct taxonomic groups: three whole-genome equivalents from epiphytic green algae, two from other angiosperms (primarily core eudicots), and one from bryophytes (mosses) [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil).

Detailed structural analysis of the "Moss-in-*Amborella* " DNA illustrates the exact molecular mechanics of organellar HGT [34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4664403/). The moss DNA is partitioned into four widely separated tracts—designated *MoAm1*  (48.1 kb), *MoAm2*  (40.0 kb), *MoAm3*  (9.3 kb), and *MoAm4*  (4.4 kb)—which together sum to 102 kb [46](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137532). When aligned against the highly conserved, perfectly syntenic 104 kb mitochondrial genome of the moss *Anomodon rugelii* , the four *Amborella*  tracts reconstruct an almost complete moss mitogenome composed of seven perfectly syntenic segments, missing only four tRNA genes [46](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0137532).

Crucially, expanded phylogenetic sampling resolved a paradox regarding the donor's identity: rather than originating from a single moss species, the 102 kb moss mitogenome inside *Amborella*  is a **chimera derived from two distinct orders of crown pleurocarpous mosses, the *Hookeriales*  and *Ptychomniales***  [34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4664403/). Following whole-mitochondrion capture and fusion, just eight recombination events (r1–r8r_1\text{–}r_8) account for all observed dispersions, deletions, and duplications of the moss DNA within *Amborella*  [34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4664403/). Five of these eight rearrangements occurred at identifiable sequence repeats: three occurred across very short repeats (10–13 bp) via **microhomology-mediated break-induced replication (MMBIR)** —producing duplicative, non-reciprocal crossovers—while two occurred across longer repeats (84 bp and 295 bp) via reciprocal homologous recombination [34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4664403/).

Unlike the adaptive nuclear transfers in *Alloteropsis* , virtually all of the moss-derived protein-coding genes in *Amborella*  have degenerated into non-functional pseudogenes, while *Amborella* 's native vertically inherited mitochondrial genes remain intact and functional [33](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article-pdf/58/1/1/1382263/erl148.pdf). *Amborella*  acts as a genomic "hoarder" due to three converging biological factors: its cloud-forest bark is densely colonized by epiphytic mosses, hepatics, and green algae; it responds to physical bark wounding by generating new meristematic germlines ("suckers") directly at the wound site where foreign epiphytic mitochondria are trapped; and its mitochondrial genome exhibits an exceptionally low rate of DNA deletion, preserving neutral foreign insertions over tens of millions of years [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil).

### Parasitic Plant Genomic Theft, Natural GMOs, and Canopy Photoreceptors

Where *Amborella*  retains foreign mitochondrial DNA neutrally, other land plants have co-opted horizontally transferred genes for critical physiological innovations. The Southeast Asian holoparasite *Rafflesia cantleyi* —famous for producing massive 50 cm rotting-flesh-scented flowers while lacking leaves, stems, roots, and a recognizable chloroplast genome—survives entirely embedded within the tissues of its obligate vine host, *Tetrastigma rafflesiae*  [30](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120608100846.htm). Transcriptomic phylogenomics revealed that *R. cantleyi*  has hijacked dozens of actively transcribed nuclear genes, mitochondrial genes such as *matR* , and roughly **33% of its remnant plastid-derived genes**  directly from *Tetrastigma*  via its haustorium [30](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120608100846.htm). These stolen genes encode essential respiration and core metabolic functions, in several instances completely replacing the parasite's native vertical homologs [30](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120608100846.htm). Even more remarkably, approximately one-third of *Rafflesia* 's own native vertically inherited genes have undergone convergent sequence evolution to resemble *Tetrastigma*  sequences—a strategy of "genomic deception" that likely enables the endoparasite to evade host immune recognition [30](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120608100846.htm).

Bacterial-to-plant HGT has similarly reshaped human agriculture. In 2015, Kyndt et al. screened 291 cultivars and wild relatives of sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas* ) and discovered that every single cultivated variety carries two distinct regions of *Agrobacterium*  T-DNA (*IbT-DNA1*  and *IbT-DNA2* ) stably integrated into its nuclear chromosomes [32](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150421084204.htm). These bacterial sequences contain actively expressed genes encoding auxin biosynthesis and opine synthesis enzymes that alter root architecture [32](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150421084204.htm). Because *IbT-DNA1*  is ubiquitous across all domesticated sweet potatoes but absent in most wild relatives, early human farmers inadvertently selected for the agronomic traits conferred by this natural bacterial HGT event during crop domestication thousands of years ago—making one of humanity's most important staple crops a "natural GMO" [32](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150421084204.htm).

Cross-lineage HGT between non-vascular and vascular plants has also triggered major macroevolutionary radiations. During the Cretaceous rise of angiosperm-dominated forests, ferns faced a severe ecological crisis as dense flowering-plant canopies filtered out high-intensity light [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24733898/). Modern polypod ferns overcame this shading by horizontally acquiring the gene encoding **neochrome** —a specialized chimeric photoreceptor that fuses a red/far-red-sensing phytochrome domain with a blue-light-sensing phototropin domain into a single polypeptide—directly from hornworts (bryophytes) [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24733898/). This single HGT acquisition dramatically enhanced fern light sensitivity, optimizing phototropism and chloroplast movement in dim understory environments and fueling the Cenozoic diversification of modern ferns [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24733898/). Similarly, at the dawn of land plant evolution, the common ancestor of charophycean green algae and embryophytes acquired glycosyl hydrolases from bacteria and fungi, aquaglyceroporins from eubacteria (~1,200 million years ago), and glutathione biosynthesis genes from alphaproteobacteria to survive terrestrial stresses [3](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article/58/1/1/515544).

## Landmark Empirical Cases in Animals: Co-Evolutionary Arms Races and Niche Expansion

Although metazoan genomes were once considered impenetrable to functional HGT due to early germline specification, intensive sequencing of invertebrates and lower vertebrates has revealed hundreds of validated xenologs [5](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/42/5/msaf107/8133933). In animals, functional HGT is overwhelmingly concentrated at the front lines of biological conflict: herbivore-plant chemical warfare, parasite-host immune evasion, and extreme environmental adaptation [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf).

### Weaponizing Plant Defenses: The Silverleaf Whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci* )

The sweet potato or silverleaf whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci* ), a cosmopolitan hemipteran agricultural pest and viral vector, provides the most striking documented example of functional **plant-to-animal horizontal gene transfer**  [15](https://www.inverse.com/science/plant-dna-found-in-animal-dna-study). Plants defend their phloem sap against herbivores by synthesizing toxic secondary metabolites, particularly phenolic glycosides [15](https://www.inverse.com/science/plant-dna-found-in-animal-dna-study). To prevent self-toxicity, plants use internal malonyltransferase enzymes to conjugate malonyl groups onto phenolic glycosides, storing them safely in nontoxic form until tissue damage triggers their breakdown [15](https://www.inverse.com/science/plant-dna-found-in-animal-dna-study).

Roughly 35 million years ago, an ancestor of *B. tabaci*  horizontally acquired a plant phenolic glucoside malonyltransferase gene—designated ***BtPMaT1*** —most likely via a plant virus vector [15](https://www.inverse.com/science/plant-dna-found-in-animal-dna-study). Highly expressed in the whitefly's gut tissues, the BtPMaT1 enzyme malonylates ingested plant phenolic glycosides, neutralizing the host plant's chemical shield using the plant's own biochemical detoxification mechanism [15](https://www.inverse.com/science/plant-dna-found-in-animal-dna-study). The adaptive necessity of this single xenolog was proved experimentally by Xia et al. (2021): when tomato plants were genetically engineered to express double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) targeting *BtPMaT1*  via host-induced RNA interference (RNAi), whiteflies feeding on the transgenic plants suffered **100% mortality** , whereas non-target insects lacking the plant-derived gene experienced zero mortality [15](https://www.inverse.com/science/plant-dna-found-in-animal-dna-study).

Subsequent genome-wide phylogenomic screens of *B. tabaci*  cryptic species (MEAM1, MED, and SSA-ECA) demonstrated that *BtPMaT1*  is part of an extraordinary repertoire of horizontally acquired genes [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11800479/). Screening the 15,662 predicted proteins of *B. tabaci*  MEAM1 (which has 95.6% arthropod BUSCO completeness) using the *AvP*  (Automatic Phylogenetic Detection) pipeline identified 686 proteins with an Alien Hit Score (AHS\text{AHS}) >0> 0, of which **255 were rigorously validated as HGT acquisitions corresponding to at least 136 independent HGT events**  from bacteria, fungi, and plants (Viridiplantae) [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11800479/). Every validated event was conserved across multiple *B. tabaci*  cryptic species and exhibited positive local synteny scores (>0>0), ruling out sequencing contamination [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11800479/). Among the plant-derived transfers in *B. tabaci*  are functional Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes (CAZymes) acting as glucanases that degrade plant phloem callose defenses, a sterol Δ5\Delta 5-desaturase (*BtSC5DL* ) essential for steroid metabolism, and plant ribotoxin-coding genes [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11800479/).

### Metabolic Hijacking in Phytophagous Beetles, Aphids, and Nematodes

Across Ecdysozoa (insects, mites, and nematodes), transitioning from a microbial or predatory diet to obligate herbivory requires overcoming two biochemical hurdles: digesting recalcitrant plant cell wall polysaccharides (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin) and neutralizing or synthesizing specialized metabolites [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer). Rather than evolving these enzymatic pathways de novo, numerous invertebrate lineages imported them directly from bacteria and fungi. In coleopterans, the scolytine coffee berry borer beetle (*Hypothenemus hampei* ) feeds and reproduces exclusively inside coffee seeds (beans), whose dry weight consists of 60% carbohydrates—predominantly the hard storage polysaccharide galactomannan—alongside 10% proteins, 12% lipids, and 1% toxic caffeine [20](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1121190109). Screening the midgut secretome of *H. hampei*  revealed ***HhMAN1*** , a glycosyl hydrolase family 5 (GH5) endo-β\beta-mannanase gene nested phylogenetically within the bacterial *Bacillus*  clade [20](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1121190109). *HhMAN1*  was acquired from gut bacteria prior to the beetle's global invasion from West Africa to Asia and South America, and is present in all geographic isolates of *H. hampei*  [20](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1121190109). Crucially, *HhMAN1*  is completely absent in its sibling species *Hypothenemus obscurus*  (the tropical nut borer or "false berry borer"), which cannot digest galactomannan or colonize coffee beans—demonstrating that a single bacterial HGT event enabled *H. hampei*  to monopolize a globally cultivated agricultural niche [20](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1121190109). Similarly, phytophagous spider mites and butterflies/moths (Lepidoptera) independently acquired a bacterial **β\beta-cyanoalanine synthase (cysteine synthase)**  gene via HGT, enabling them to detoxify hydrogen cyanide released when feeding on cyanogenic glucoside-producing host plants [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer).

In hemipterans, HGT overturned a foundational biochemical rule of animal metabolism. Until 2010, it was assumed that animals cannot synthesize carotenoids de novo and must obtain these essential antioxidants and visual/signaling pigments from their diet or intracellular endosymbionts (such as *Portiera*  in whiteflies) [35](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/04/100429141417.htm). Sequencing the pea aphid (*Acyrthosiphon pisum* ) and potato aphid (*Myzus persicae* ) genomes revealed **seven carotenoid biosynthesis genes nested phylogenetically within the kingdom Fungi**  [61](https://arthropoda.wordpress.com/2010/04/30/aphid-adornment-lateral-gene-transfer-from-fungi-to-aphids/). An ancient aphid ancestor became infected with a fungus and integrated a fungal carotenoid synthesis cluster into its chromosomes, followed by gene duplication [60](https://scienceblogs.com/myrmecos/2010/04/30/how-the-aphid-got-its-pink). Beyond providing antioxidant protection, this fungal transfer governs a critical ecological polymorphism: red pea aphids carry a specific 30 kb genomic region encoding a single fungal carotenoid desaturase that synthesizes the red carotenoid **torulene** , whereas green aphids lack this 30 kb locus (and a single amino acid replacement in this desaturase converts red 5A aphids into the yellow 5AY mutant strain) [35](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/04/100429141417.htm). In the field, this HGT locus is maintained under frequency-dependent **balancing selection** : visual predators (ladybird beetles) preferentially attack conspicuous red aphids on green foliage, whereas endoparasitoid wasps preferentially oviposit into green aphids [60](https://scienceblogs.com/myrmecos/2010/04/30/how-the-aphid-got-its-pink).

A remarkably parallel pattern of microbe-to-animal metabolic transfer underpins the evolution of plant-parasitic nematodes (PPNs). PPNs—which inflict $100 billion to $173 billion in annual global crop losses—are not monophyletic; plant parasitism evolved independently at least four times across the phylum Nematoda (in Clade 1 Triplonchidae, Clade 2 Longidoridae, Clade 10b Aphelenchoididae/Parasitaphelenchidae, and Clade 12 Tylenchida) from free-living bacterivorous or fungivorous ancestors [37](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/7/9/2727/592822). Across these independent origins, the evolutionary leap into plant parasitism was catalyzed by the horizontal acquisition of a prokaryotic and fungal "cell-wall-degrading arsenal" followed by massive tandem gene duplication [17](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1008486107). Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne incognita* ) and cyst nematodes (*Globodera rostochiensis* ) in Clade 12 acquired **GH5 β\beta-1,4-endoglucanases (cellulases)** , **pectate lyases**  (cleaving α\alpha-1,4 pectate linkages), **expansin-like proteins**  (also shared with *Xiphinema index*  in Clade 2), and nine vitamin biosynthesis/salvage genes from soil bacteria, as well as **GH28 polygalacturonases**  transferred directly from the cohabiting soil plant pathogen *Ralstonia solanacearum*  [17](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1008486107). Conversely, pine wood nematodes (*Bursaphelenchus*  in Clade 10b) acquired **GH16 and GH45 cellulases from fungi** , while necromenic *Pristionchus*  nematodes acquired GH5 cellulases from *Amoeba*  to digest bacterial biofilms [57](https://apsjournals.apsnet.org/doi/pdf/10.1094/MPMI-03-11-0055).

### Tri-Trophic Immune Arms Races: Bacteriophages, Wasps, and Caterpillars

Horizontal gene transfer also drives reciprocal weapon acquisitions across multiple trophic levels in insect-parasitoid interactions. Many sap-feeding and drosophilid insects rely on facultative bacterial endosymbionts such as *Hamiltonella defensa*  and *Arsenophonus*  spp. for protection against parasitoid wasps; this protection is actually encoded by bacteriophages (specifically **APSE-2, APSE-6, and APSE-7 phages** ) integrated into the endosymbiont genome [31](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2218334120). Remarkably, ancestral insects across five orders—Diptera (including the *Drosophila ananassae*  subgroup ~21 million years ago), Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera, and Thysanoptera—bypassed the need to maintain the bacterial symbiont by horizontally transferring two syntenic phage toxin genes—**cytolethal distending toxin B (*cdtB* )**  and the B domain of **apoptosis-inducing protein 56 (*aip56* )** —directly from APSE phages into the insect nuclear genome [31](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2218334120). In *Drosophila* , these captured phage toxins are expressed in the embryonic serosa and strongly upregulated upon wasp attack, functioning as an innate eukaryotic immune weapon that kills developing parasitoid wasp embryos [31](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2218334120).

In the reverse direction, when braconid wasps inject polydnavirus (BV) dsDNA circles into caterpillars, orthopterans, or stick insects, those circles integrate into host chromosomes via HIM J1/J2 motifs [25](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/7/evag150/8716239). When caterpillars survive parasitism, these integrated bracovirus genes—unambiguously identifiable because they retain non-autonomous wasp ovarian DRJ regulatory signals—are occasionally domesticated by the lepidopteran genome [42](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1005470). Functional assays demonstrate that at least two bracovirus genes horizontally acquired by Lepidoptera (along with PDV-derived C-type lectins, CTLs) have been co-opted to protect the caterpillars against lethal **baculovirus**  infections in the wild [42](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1005470). Thus, a viral genome captured 100 million years ago by a wasp to suppress caterpillar immunity was subsequently captured by the caterpillar from the wasp to fight off a third-party viral pathogen [25](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/7/evag150/8716239).

### Direct Vertebrate-to-Vertebrate Transfer and Transposon Pandemics

While functional protein-coding HGT is rarer in vertebrates than in arthropods, two major phenomena demonstrate that vertebrate genomes are actively shaped by horizontal DNA movement. During Cenozoic polar cooling, marine teleost fishes faced lethal freezing in icy shallow seawater, driving the independent evolution of distinct classes of antifreeze proteins (AFPs: alanine-rich α\alpha-helical Type I, lectin-like Type II, β\beta-clip Type III, and antifreeze glycoproteins, AFGPs) [68](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1139/bcb-2022-0029). However, the Ca2+\text{Ca}^{2+}-dependent, lectin-like **Type II AFP**  exhibits a bafflingly disjunct phylogenetic distribution, appearing in only three widely diverged teleost orders: Atlantic herring (*Clupea harengus* ), smelt (*Osmerus mordax* ), and sea raven [16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2440524/). Whereas all other vertebrate and invertebrate lectins possess four to eight cysteines and share <40%<40\% amino acid identity with Type II AFPs (and model genomes such as *Danio rerio* , *Oryzias latipes* , *Fugu rubripes* , and *Tetraodon nigroviridis*  lack any homologs or pseudogenes), the Type II AFPs of herring, smelt, and sea raven share an identical 10-cysteine framework [16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2440524/). Crucially, the herring and rainbow smelt Type II AFP genes share **>85%>85\% amino acid identity and over 90% intron sequence similarity**  [16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2440524/). Access to the chromosome-scale Atlantic herring genome confirmed that smelt acquired its Type II AFP gene (along with flanking non-coding sequences) via direct horizontal transfer from herring—representing a validated vertebrate-to-vertebrate HGT of a functional adaptive gene that instantly conferred sub-zero survival [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer).

Beyond single-copy protein genes, the most massive structural impact of HGT in vertebrates involves non-LTR retrotransposons [21](https://www.inverse.com/science/46816-evolution-species-jumping-genes). By screening over 500 to 750 eukaryotic genomes, Ivancevic et al. (2018) reconstructed the cross-species leaps of two major retrotransposons: ***BovB***  (a 3.2 kb RTE-clade LINE) and ***L1***  (LINE-1) [70](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/07/180709101216.htm). *BovB*  displays a patchy, cross-class distribution across sea urchins, marine acorn worms, ticks, squamate reptiles (snakes and lizards), marsupials, bats, afrotherians (where *BovB*  reverse transcriptase mobilized *AfroSINE*  elements), and ruminants [21](https://www.inverse.com/science/46816-evolution-species-jumping-genes). Phylogenomic clustering of Reverse Transcriptase (RT) domains confirmed that blood-feeding parasitic ticks with broad ancestral host ranges vectored *BovB*  between reptiles and mammals [24](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/5/evag107/8673164). Once *BovB*  jumped into a naive ruminant ancestor, it escaped host epigenetic repression and underwent an explosive "copy-and-paste" amplification burst—ultimately expanding to occupy **~25% of the entire domestic cow (*Bos taurus* ) genome**  [21](https://www.inverse.com/science/46816-evolution-species-jumping-genes). Similarly, *L1* —the only currently active autonomous transposon in the human genome, implicated in promoter activation, genomic rearrangement, and disease—was completely absent in early mammals until it entered the therian ancestor (marsupials and placentals) via a single ancient HGT event after the divergence of monotremes, leaving the platypus genome entirely free of *L1*  [21](https://www.inverse.com/science/46816-evolution-species-jumping-genes). Parallel transposon jumps have been documented between filarial nematodes and seven tropical bird lineages (*AviRTE* , transferred 25–22 Ma and 20–17 Ma) and between the human blood fluke *Schistosoma mansoni*  and its intermediate freshwater snail hosts (*Perere-3*  and *Sr3* ) [24](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/5/evag107/8673164).

## Bioinformatic Detection, Quantitative Metrics, and the Contamination Crisis

As high-throughput shotgun sequencing expanded across non-model plants and animals, claims of massive eukaryotic HGT surged—only for several high-profile reports to be exposed as bioinformatic artifacts caused by microbial sequencing contamination, differential gene loss, or evolutionary rate heterogeneity [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer). Consequently, modern evolutionary genomics relies on a strict quantitative and phylogenetic validation framework to separate bona fide xenologs from bacterial cobionts [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664098/).

### Parametric Signatures and Sequence-Similarity Indices

Inferring HGT begins with high-throughput screening of whole proteomes using two complementary approaches: **parametric (intrinsic) methods** , which search for atypical nucleotide composition, GC content, or synonymous codon usage within a single genome; and **phylogenetic (extrinsic) methods** , which detect topological discordance between a gene tree and the established species tree [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer). For parametric evaluation, researchers calculate the **Codon Adaptation Index (CAI\text{CAI})** —ranging from 00 to 11—and compare it against an expected threshold (e-CAI\text{e-CAI} at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05 using Markov models, Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, and χ2\chi^2 goodness-of-fit tests) to determine whether a putative xenolog has ameliorated to match the host genome's codon bias [75](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7145615/).

However, because ancient HGTs evolve to match host GC and codon profiles over millions of years, sequence-similarity indices provide far higher sensitivity for initial proteome screening [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664098/). Originally formulated by Gladyshev et al. (2008) to quantify massive HGT in asexual bdelloid rotifers and subsequently standardized in the *Alienness*  and *HGTphyloDetect*  pipelines, the **Alien Index (AIAI)**  measures the logarithmic magnitude difference between the best BLAST hit in a taxonomically close ingroup (bbhG\text{bbh}_G, recipient clade) and a distant outgroup (bbhO\text{bbh}_O, putative donor clade) [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664098/):

AI=ln⁡(bbhG+1×10−200)−ln⁡(bbhO+1×10−200)AI = \ln\left(\text{bbh}_G + 1 \times 10^{-200}\right) - \ln\left(\text{bbh}_O + 1 \times 10^{-200}\right)

Any gene yielding AI>0AI > 0 shares greater sequence similarity with a distant foreign taxon than with its own phylum or kingdom [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664098/). Calibration studies across plant-parasitic nematodes (*Meloidogyne incognita*  and *Globodera rostochiensis* ) established precise operational cutoffs: **AI>14AI > 14**  eliminates automatic phylogenetic false positives while capturing 84% to 92% of true HGTs; **AI>26AI > 26**  isolates candidates with robust maximum-likelihood tree support; and **AI≥45AI \ge 45** —combined with an outgroup hit proportion out_pct=noutside kingdomntotal hits≥90%\text{out\_pct} = \frac{n_{\text{outside kingdom}}}{n_{\text{total hits}}} \ge 90\%—serves as Gladyshev's stringent threshold for unambiguous foreign origin [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664098/).

Because classical AIAI relies solely on a single best ingroup and outgroup BLAST hit, a trace contaminant sequence in public databases can distort the score [74](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.23.497291v1.full.pdf). To overcome this limitation, Koutsovoulos et al. (2022) developed the **Alien Hit Score (AHS\text{AHS})**  within the *AvP*  software package, which calculates the difference between the sum of all normalized donor bitscores and all normalized ingroup bitscores using an exponential decay weighting relative to the highest bitscore (HBitscore\text{HBitscore}) [74](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.23.497291v1.full.pdf):

BitscoreN=Bitscore⋅e−10⋅HBitscore−BitscoreBitscore\text{Bitscore}_N = \text{Bitscore} \cdot e^{-10 \cdot \frac{\text{HBitscore} - \text{Bitscore}}{\text{Bitscore}}}

Crucially, *AvP*  pairs AHS\text{AHS} with a genomic **HGT Local Score**  ranging from −1-1 to +1+1, which evaluates the taxonomic classification of neighboring genes along the same assembly contig or scaffold [74](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.23.497291v1.full.pdf). A Local Score <0< 0 (approaching −1-1) indicates that the candidate gene is surrounded by other foreign/bacterial genes—flagging a contaminant bacterial contig—whereas a Local Score >0> 0 (approaching +1+1) proves that the foreign gene is physically embedded within a chromosome flanked by native host genes [74](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.23.497291v1.full.pdf). Alongside these topological metrics, Ku and Martin's **"70% Rule"**  dictates that in prokaryote-to-eukaryote HGT analyses, any candidate protein or nucleotide sequence sharing **≥70%\ge 70\% sequence identity**  with a known bacterial genome must be categorized as a probable sequencing contaminant unless proven otherwise by physical chromosomal linkage, the acquisition of spliceosomal introns, eukaryotic secretion signal peptides, and multi-tissue RNA expression [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664098/).

### Cautionary Tales: The Tardigrade and Human Genome Controversies

The necessity of these forensic filters was dramatically illustrated by the 2015–2016 controversy surrounding the tardigrade *Hypsibius dujardini*  [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113). Tardigrades ("water bears") are microscopic ecdysozoans renowned for surviving extreme desiccation (anhydrobiosis), freezing, and cosmic radiation [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113). In late 2015, Boothby et al. published a draft genome assembly of *H. dujardini*  (the UNC assembly, spanning 212 Mb with 38,145 predicted genes) and concluded that **roughly one-sixth (~16.7%, or >6,000 genes) of the tardigrade gene repertoire originated via functional HGT**  primarily from bacteria—proposing that drying-induced DNA fragmentation made tardigrades uniquely receptive to foreign genes [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113).

Within months, independent genome sequencing by Koutsovoulos et al. (2016, the Edinburgh *nHd.2.3*  assembly) and targeted metagenomic re-analyses by Delmont and Eren proved that the claim of extensive HGT in *H. dujardini*  was an artifact of **undetected bacterial contamination**  [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113). Because an adult *H. dujardini*  consists of only ∼103\sim 10^3 cells, even trace quantities of microbiome bacteria remaining after washing contribute DNA mass equivalent to the animal itself during whole-organism sequencing [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113). When evaluated using metagenomic "blobplots"—which plot each assembled scaffold by its GC content, sequencing read coverage depth, and taxonomic similarity—the UNC assembly was found to contain **68.9 Mb of obvious bacterial contaminant scaffolds encoding 9,872 predicted protein loci**  [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113). Closer inspection of the largest UNC contigs revealed intact bacterial chromosomes (such as *Chitinophagaceae*  species) containing up to 4,783 bacterial genes on a single contig with narrow prokaryotic intergenic spacing and zero physical linkage to tardigrade chromosomes [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113). When contaminant reads were removed using PacBio long-read and cleaned Illumina assemblies, the true *H. dujardini*  genome span contracted to **135 Mb** , and bona fide functional HGT was shown to account for **at most 1% to 2% of tardigrade genes**  (with only 58 of the highlighted UNC candidate loci confirmed in the Edinburgh reads and 49 showing transcriptional expression) [22](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1600338113).

A similar debate unfolded regarding the human genome: in 2015, Crisp et al. reported phylogenetic evidence that 145 foreign genes (largely from bacteria, fungi, and protists) had entered the human lineage via HGT [29](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cell-and-developmental-biology/articles/10.3389/fcell.2020.00229/full). However, subsequent re-evaluations by Salzberg (2017) and other evolutionary biologists demonstrated that—outside of ancient mitochondrial-to-nuclear intracellular gene transfers (IGT), endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), and transposable elements (*L1* )—almost all putative bacterial protein-coding HGT candidates in humans can be explained by differential gene loss across ancestral eukaryotic branches, lineage-specific evolutionary rate variations, or assembly contamination [3](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article/58/1/1/515544).

## Synthesis and Evolutionary Significance: Macroevolutionary and Translational Implications

### Ecological Intimacy Versus Selective Fixation

Evaluating the empirical record across plants and animals reveals that horizontal gene transfer is neither a ubiquitous prokaryotic free-for-all nor a negligible biological curiosity, but rather a targeted evolutionary mechanism governed by distinct structural and selective filters [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf). The probability of physical DNA transfer across species boundaries is dictated by **ecological intimacy and developmental architecture** : endoparasitic plants connected via haustoria (*Rafflesia* , Orobanchaceae), epiphytes growing on wound-prone shrubs (*Amborella* ), sap-sucking and root-invading herbivores harboring intracellular symbionts (*Bemisia tabaci* , aphids, plant-parasitic nematodes), and externally fertilizing marine teleosts (herring and smelt) experience orders-of-magnitude higher exposure to exogenous DNA in or near their germ cells than free-living terrestrial vertebrates [8](https://scispace.com/papers/horizontal-transfer-of-entire-genomes-via-mitochondrial-4h122jvnil). However, as demonstrated by the contrast between *Amborella*  (which retains six foreign mitochondrial genomes mostly as neutral pseudogenes) and *Alloteropsis semialata*  or *Hypothenemus hampei*  (which selectively express and duplicate foreign C4\text{C}_4 and mannanase enzymes), **physical entry is decoupled from functional fixation**  [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf). In nuclear genomes, host epigenetic defenses—such as the +2.7% CpG gene body hypermethylation documented in *A. semialata* —actively suppress newly integrated foreign DNA [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383). Only when a foreign gene provides a high-fitness solution to an otherwise insurmountable selective bottleneck does natural selection drive its transcriptional activation, intron acquisition, and fixation across populations [1](https://www3.botany.ubc.ca/keeling/PDF/24HGT.pdf).

### Bypassing Epistatic Valley Constraints via Pre-Optimized Modules

In classical vertical evolution, evolving a complex novel enzyme—such as a 10-cysteine lectin-like antifreeze protein, a chimeric red/blue *neochrome*  photoreceptor, or a high-efficiency C4\text{C}_4 PEPC carboxylase—requires accumulating numerous sequential point mutations and gene duplications [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24733898/). If intermediate mutational steps confer neutral or deleterious fitness, a lineage cannot cross the adaptive valley rapidly enough to survive sudden environmental shifts such as Cenozoic marine glaciation or Cretaceous angiosperm canopy closure [14](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Luke-Dunning). Horizontal gene transfer circumvents this evolutionary constraint by importing **complete, pre-tested genetic modules**  that have already been refined by millions of years of positive selection in the donor lineage [6](https://www.scienceopen.com/document_file/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee/PubMedCentral/7dc2a545-dfc9-44d1-a377-5e681c60e6ee.pdf). Once captured, these single-gene modules frequently undergo tandem cis-duplication in the recipient genome—as seen in the multigene cellulase and pectate lyase families of plant-parasitic nematodes and the cysteine peptidase family in wild *Hordeum* —generating a permanent genomic reservoir for neo-functionalization [17](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1008486107).

### Translational Applications in Biocontrol, Forensic Ecology, and Crop Engineering

Beyond redefining the eukaryotic tree of life as an interconnected reticulate web, eukaryotic HGT discoveries yield immediate practical applications across agriculture, evolutionary ecology, and biotechnology [41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13315421/). In agricultural pest management, because xenologs acquired by pests from plants, bacteria, or fungi (such as *BtPMaT1*  and *BtSC5DL*  in *Bemisia tabaci*  or *HhMAN1*  in *Hypothenemus hampei* ) are completely absent from beneficial insects, pollinators, and vertebrates, they represent ideal "Achilles' heel" molecular targets [20](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1121190109). Engineering host crops or foliar sprays with dsRNA targeting pest-specific HGT genes achieves up to 100% pest mortality with zero off-target ecological toxicity [53](https://www.sci.news/biology/silverleaf-whiteflies-plant-derived-detoxification-gene-09490.html).

In evolutionary ecology, because HGT requires intimate physical contact between donor and recipient, horizontally transferred transposable elements (*BovB* , *AviRTE* , *Perere-3* /*Sr3* ), nuclear plastid fragments (NUPTs), and integrated polydnavirus segments (HIM junctions) serve as permanent genomic fossils [24](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/5/evag107/8673164). Sequencing these insertions allows researchers to reconstruct ancient host-parasitoid ranges—such as uncovering previously unknown braconid wasp attacks on stick insects and orthopterans—and trace historical trophic networks across deep time [24](https://academic.oup.com/gbe/article/18/5/evag107/8673164). Finally, in agricultural biotechnology, the discovery that globally consumed food crops like sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas* ) are naturally transgenic via ancient *Agrobacterium*  T-DNA insertions—and that wild grasses routinely exchange up to 170 kb blocks of functional nuclear DNA to accelerate C4\text{C}_4 evolution—demonstrates that transgenic modification is an intrinsic mechanism of natural plant evolution [13](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.1810031116). Understanding how recipient grass genomes epigenetically methylate, regulate, and domesticate foreign C4\text{C}_4 gene blocks provides a direct blueprint for synthetic biologists working to engineer C4\text{C}_4 photosynthetic efficiency into C3\text{C}_3 staple crops such as rice and wheat [9](https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/43/2/msag042/8500383).
