# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: How Common Is It, and Does It Matter?

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) into plants and animals is no longer controversial in principle, and it is not vanishingly rare. Careful genome-scale surveys find it in most lineages examined. But it typically supplies only a tiny fraction of genes: about 0.05% of insect genes, and up to roughly 0.3% of grass genes. The striking exceptions are bdelloid rotifers (about 8–10%)\[1\] and parasitic plants. A small but well-validated subset of these transfers clearly confers adaptive benefits: detoxification, digestion, defence, pigmentation, cold tolerance and light sensing. The best overall reading is that eukaryotic HGT is a rare-per-gene but recurrent, ecologically channelled process. Most transfers are probably neutral, transient or selfish (especially transposons and endosymbiont DNA). A minority are domesticated into key innovations, and these explain the dramatic case studies.

## TL;DR

- **How common:** The best genome-scale surveys put it at about 1,410 foreign genes from 741 transfers across 218 insect genomes. That is 192 of 218 species, but only about 0.05% of the genes screened. In grasses, lateral transfers were found in 13 of 17 grasses, and 0.03–0.3% of genes in a 2025 survey of 122 grass genomes. About 108 transfers were found in dodder (*Cuscuta*), and 8–10% of genes are foreign in bdelloid rotifers.\[2\] Headline claims of very high rates were largely artefacts: 17.5% in a tardigrade, and 145 "foreign" genes in humans.
- **Does it confer benefits:** Yes, in a small set of cases with experimental support. These include the whitefly *BtPMaT1* (RNAi knockdown raises mortality on host-plant toxins), the moth courtship gene (CRISPR knockout reduces courtship), the coffee berry borer mannanase, the fern neochrome, the herring-to-smelt antifreeze gene and grass-to-grass stress and photosynthesis genes.\[3\]\[4\] For most detected transfers, benefit is inferred, not shown.
- **Why it is rarer than in microbes, and why plants differ:** Animals sequester the germline early, and both kingdoms have nuclear, chromatin, intron and silencing barriers. Transfers therefore concentrate where intimacy and germline access coincide. Examples are parasites and haustoria, endosymbionts, rhizomes and clonal growth, external fertilisation in fish, asexual desiccation-tolerant rotifers, and viral or tick vectors. Plants, with no early germline segregation, show more plant-to-plant transfer than animals show animal-to-animal gene transfer.

## Key Findings

1. **HGT in multicellular eukaryotes is widespread across species but sparse within genomes.** In the most rigorous animal survey (Li et al. 2022, *Cell*), 88% of 218 insect species carried at least one foreign gene.\[5\] Yet the 1,410 HGT-acquired genes came from 2,806,851 genes screened.\[6\] About 80% of donors were bacteria, 11% fungi and 4% plants.\[7\] Lepidoptera had the most foreign genes, Diptera the fewest.\[8\]
2. **Plants show more eukaryote-to-eukaryote transfer than animals.** Grass-to-grass transfer is documented repeatedly. *Alloteropsis semialata* carries 59 laterally acquired genes in 23 fragments of up to 170 kb, from at least nine donors.\[9\] Parasitic plants acquire host nuclear and mitochondrial genes.\[10\] *Amborella* holds six genome-equivalents of foreign mitochondrial DNA.\[11\] In animals, eukaryote-to-eukaryote *gene* transfer remains anecdotal: herring-to-smelt antifreeze, plant-to-whitefly *BtPMaT1*, and fungus-to-aphid carotenoid genes. Animal-to-animal transfer is overwhelmingly of transposable elements (TEs), not host genes.
3. **Transposons are the most mobile eukaryotic DNA.** A 2020 survey of 307 vertebrate genomes found at least 975 horizontal TE transfers, almost 94% involving ray-finned fishes and fewer than 3% involving birds or mammals.\[3\] BovB and L1 retrotransposons have moved between snakes, ruminants, marsupials and arthropods. BovB plus L1 make up more than 17% of the cow genome.\[12\] By sequence volume, this probably dwarfs adaptive gene transfer.
4. **Ancient "pulses" of HGT coincide with major transitions,** especially plants colonising land. Ma et al. (2022) inferred 593 streptophyte gene families of HGT origin and two major episodes (177 and 107 families).\[13\]\[14\] Cheng et al. (2019) traced GRAS and PYR/PYL/RCAR abscisic-acid receptor families to soil bacteria about 580 million years ago.\[15\] These are the hardest claims to prove and remain partly debated.\[16\]
5. **Retention follows opportunity and compatibility.** Rhizomatous grasses acquire more genes, and transfers succeed more often between closer relatives.\[17\] Lineages with intimate ecological contact are HGT hotspots: parasites, herbivores feeding on symbiont-rich diets, and hosts of endosymbionts.
6. **Contamination is the field's defining failure mode.** The tardigrade claim fell from 17.5% to well under 2%.\[18\]\[19\] Most human HGT claims evaporated on re-analysis, twice: in 2001 and again in 2015–2017.\[19\]\[20\] Even celebrated adaptive cases warrant scrutiny. *Fhb7*'s fungal origin is plausible, but its resistance function is disputed in both directions: Guo et al. (2023, *Plant Biotechnology Journal*) found the GST-encoding *Fhb7* "failed to confer wheat FHB resistance," whereas Zhao et al. (2024, *Phytopathology*, USDA-ARS) found a gene-edited GST knockout increased susceptibility and "confirmed GST as the causal gene of Fhb7 for FHB resistance."

## Background: Microbial HGT versus Eukaryotic HGT

In bacteria and archaea, HGT is a primary engine of genome evolution. Transformation, transduction and conjugation move genes routinely. One widely cited estimate holds that, on average, 81% of genes in bacteria were involved in HGT at some point in their history (Dagan et al. 2008, quoted in Li et al. 2026).\[21\] Because DNA in a bacterium lies in the cytoplasm, a fragment that crosses the envelope faces few barriers to integration.\[3\]

Eukaryotes differ in four ways:
- the genome sits inside a nucleus and is packed into chromatin;
- genes need eukaryotic promoters and often introns and splicing signals;
- many lineages have RNAi and other genome-defence systems that silence foreign sequences;
- in multicellular organisms a transfer counts evolutionarily only if it reaches the germline and then spreads through a population.

The field also carries a history of over-claiming. The 2001 human genome paper's claims of bacterial genes were quickly refuted as gene loss in intermediate lineages (Salzberg et al. 2001, *Science*).\[3\]\[20\] That history made reviewers sceptical for two decades. Laurie Graham's herring–smelt antifreeze paper, for instance, was rejected by "journal after journal" before appearing in *PLoS ONE* in 2008.\[3\]

By the late 2010s, the field had shifted from "does it happen?" to "how much, and does it matter?" Van Etten & Bhattacharya's 2020 *Trends in Genetics* opinion was titled "Horizontal Gene Transfer in Eukaryotes: Not if, but How Much?".\[22\] Patrick Keeling's 2024 *Nature Reviews Genetics* synthesis, "Horizontal gene transfer in eukaryotes: aligning theory with data", offers the most useful current framing. Phagocytosis and endosymbiosis do raise the *chance* of acquiring genes, but this is offset by a reduced *need* for them.\[23\] Selection in most eukaryotes acts on variation that HGT does not readily generate, such as regulatory changes, duplications and alternative splicing. This explains why foreign genes are found almost everywhere yet make up such a small share of genomes.

Note also that the boundary between HGT and "introgression" is blurring. A 2025 preprint on grasses coined the term "remote introgression" for transfers between moderately distant grass lineages. It argues that some of these may reflect ancient hybridisation across incomplete reproductive barriers rather than true asexual transfer.\[24\] This matters for how grass-to-grass "HGT" counts are interpreted.

## Why HGT Is Rarer in Multicellular Eukaryotes

**Barriers common to all eukaryotes:**
- **Nuclear envelope and chromatin.** Foreign DNA must reach the nucleus, typically during mitosis or after DNA damage, and integrate via non-homologous end-joining or a transposon or viral integrase.
- **Expression incompatibility.** Bacterial genes lack eukaryotic promoters, polyadenylation signals and introns, and often have divergent codon usage and GC content. Foreign genes that persist usually show signs of "domestication": acquired introns, shifted codon usage, and sometimes duplication. Li et al. (2022) found that intron gains from the native insect genomes were "likely involved" in adapting HGT-acquired genes.\[25\]
- **Genome defences.** RNAi, DNA methylation and piRNA systems silence repetitive or foreign sequence. This may explain why many inserts, such as Wolbachia DNA in flies, are transcribed only at low levels.\[26\]\[27\]
- **Lack of dedicated machinery.** Eukaryotes have no natural competence or conjugation systems comparable to bacteria. The possible exceptions are sperm-mediated uptake, vesicle traffic and viral transduction.

**The germline (Weismann) barrier in animals.** Most animals set aside the germline early in development. A gene entering a gut or skin cell is an evolutionary dead end. Animals that are HGT hotspots tend to weaken this barrier in some way:
- **Bdelloid rotifers** are asexual. They repeatedly desiccate, which breaks DNA, and then repair it, which may allow foreign DNA to be captured during repair.
- **Insects with heritable endosymbionts** (e.g., Wolbachia) carry bacteria directly in the germline.
- **Externally fertilising fish** expose gametes to DNA-rich water. Graham suggests that sperm-mediated uptake during mass herring spawning could have moved the antifreeze gene into smelt.\[3\]

**Why plants are more permeable:**
- Plants lack early germline sequestration. Meristems that later produce flowers can be exposed to foreign DNA throughout life.
- Plants can regenerate from somatic tissue.
- Clonal and vegetative reproduction lets a transformed somatic sector found a new lineage. Hibdige et al. (2021) found that rhizomatous grasses acquired statistically more genes, "probably because this growth habit boosts opportunities for transfer into the germline."\[28\]
- Parasitic haustoria create vascular continuity with hosts, through which mRNA, proteins and DNA move.\[29\]
- Natural grafting, root contact and Agrobacterium infection offer further routes.
- Plant mitochondria can fuse and recombine, uniquely allowing whole-genome capture (see *Amborella*).\[30\]\[31\]

**Theoretical models.** The "weak-link" model (Huang 2013) proposes that foreign DNA enters at unprotected, transformable stages: single-celled, early developmental or meristematic stages. It fits plants, clonal animals and unicellular-like life stages well. It predicts that taxa with exposed or long-lived germline precursors should accumulate more HGT, which matches the rhizome correlation in grasses. The "you are what you eat" ratchet (Doolittle 1998) argues that phagotrophs continually ingest DNA and occasionally fix genes from their food. It explains much of protist HGT. However, Keeling (2024) argues that the expected excess of HGT in phagotrophs is not clearly borne out by data.\[32\] For plants and most animals, which do not phagocytose prey, the ratchet applies only loosely, via gut symbionts, parasites and diet-associated microbes. The whitefly's acquisition of a host-plant gene is a rare animal case consistent with a diet-linked route, though the authors favour a plant virus as vector.\[33\]

## Mechanisms and Vectors

- **Viruses.** Polydnaviruses carried by braconid wasps (bracoviruses) integrate into the DNA of the caterpillars they parasitise.\[34\] Gasmi et al. (2015, *PLoS Genetics*) showed that bracovirus sequences have been inserted repeatedly into lepidopteran genomes, including monarch, silkworm and *Spodoptera* armyworms, so the viral DNA can reach the germline. Some of these sequences are wasp genes rather than viral genes. Functional assays suggest two of the domesticated genes, including *gasmin* (BV2-5), partially protect caterpillars against baculoviruses. Selection on the monarch *Ben* genes is mainly conservative (purifying). European *S. exigua* populations carry a truncated, non-functional *gasmin*, which shows that such genes can be lost. The whitefly team proposed that a plant virus may have ferried *BtPMaT1* into the insect.\[33\] Irwin et al. (2022, *Nature Microbiology*) systematically evaluated gene exchange between eukaryotes and viruses. They showed viruses are frequent recipients and occasional donors of eukaryotic genes, including a viral cellulose synthase linked to the streptophyte cell wall.\[35\]
- **Transposable elements.** TEs carry their own integration machinery, making them the most transferable eukaryotic DNA. Ivancevic et al. (2018, *Genome Biology*) analysed 759 plant, fungal and animal genomes. They described extensive BovB transfer and the first evidence that L1 elements also transfer horizontally. Ticks and bedbugs carry closely related BovBs and are proposed vectors. Kambayashi, Kurabayashi et al. (2022, *Molecular Biology and Evolution*, "Geography-Dependent Horizontal Gene Transfer from Vertebrate Predators to Their Prey") found BovB "originated in frogs by HT from snakes only 27.7 Ma" and now makes up 0.53% of the genome of the mantellid frog *Boophis goudotii*, with Madagascar a hotspot and parasites the likely vectors. Mosquito-borne filarial worms have also been implicated as TE vectors.
- **Endosymbionts.** Dunning Hotopp et al. (2007, *Science*) found Wolbachia DNA in the nuclear genomes of 4 insect and 4 nematode species. Inserts ranged "from nearly the entire Wolbachia genome (>1 megabase) to short (<500 base pairs)" fragments.\[36\] In *Drosophila ananassae* the insert spans about 1.4 Mb. At least 28 of its genes are transcribed, "albeit at low levels."\[26\]\[27\] Later work (Klasson et al. 2014, *BMC Genomics*) found multiple Wolbachia genome copies on chromosome 4, making up more than 2% of the fly genome. That work concluded the insert "may not currently provide any function."\[37\]\[38\] This is the archetype of large-scale but probably non-adaptive transfer.
- **Parasitism.** Parasitic plants acquire host genes through haustoria.\[24\]\[39\] Examples are *Striga hermonthica* from sorghum (Yoshida et al. 2010, *Science*), *Rafflesia* from its *Tetrastigma* host, Orobanchaceae, and dodder (*Cuscuta*). Yang et al. (2016, *PNAS*) found that HGT is more frequent with increasing heterotrophy.\[40\] Yang et al. (2019, *Nature Plants*) identified 108 transcribed, probably functional HGT events in *Cuscuta campestris*.\[41\] Analysis of intron and flanking structure supported transfer of genomic DNA rather than retroprocessed mRNA.\[42\] Eighteen *Cuscuta* transfers involved the same gene families acquired independently by Orobanchaceae, mostly highly expressed in haustoria. This convergence strongly suggests adaptive retention.\[43\]
- **Agrobacterium T-DNA.** Agrobacterium naturally inserts T-DNA into plant genomes. All 291 cultivated sweet potato accessions tested contained one or more T-DNA sequences (Kyndt et al. 2015, *PNAS*). One T-DNA is in all cultivated clones but not in close wild relatives, suggesting selection during domestication. Similar "cellular T-DNAs" occur in *Nicotiana*, *Linaria* and others, notably catalogued by Tatiana Matveeva's group, and recently in dodder.
- **Mitochondrial fusion.** Rice et al. (2013, *Science*) found that the 3.9-Mb *Amborella trichopoda* mitochondrial genome contains six genome-equivalents of foreign mitochondrial DNA. It came from green algae, mosses and other angiosperms, including whole mitochondrial genomes from three green algae and one moss.\[11\] A 2015 re-analysis found the moss portion derives from two separate transfers.\[44\] The authors' model has Amborella capturing whole mitochondria, which fuse and recombine with its own. This is aided by its dense epiphyte load, wound-induced suckering and a low rate of mtDNA loss.\[45\]
- **Ecological intimacy.** Across mechanisms, the common factor is sustained physical contact. Examples are host–parasite, plant–endophyte (Epichloë–grass), insect–symbiont, sympatric co-occurrence among grasses, and shared spawning grounds among fish.

**What the numbers mean.**
- Per genome, typical animals and plants carry tens to perhaps a couple of hundred well-supported foreign genes. That is a per-mille-level contribution, not the per-cent level of bacteria.
- The variance between lineages is huge and ecologically structured. Lepidoptera have far more foreign genes than Diptera, and rhizomatous grasses more than non-rhizomatous ones. Bdelloids reach about 10%, and parasitic plants are elevated.
- Detected counts are lower bounds. Old transfers decay beyond recognition, conservative pipelines discard ambiguous cases, and transfers between close relatives look like ordinary inheritance. Detected counts are also upper-bound-prone where contamination or poor taxon sampling inflates them. Both biases are real.
- **Plants vs. animals.** Plants clearly show more *eukaryote-to-eukaryote* gene transfer than animals: plant-to-plant via haustoria, rhizomes and mitochondrial fusion. Prokaryote/fungus-to-host rates are broadly comparable, at small fractions of a percent. The difference follows directly from germline biology and physical-contact opportunities.
- A 2026 *Scientific Data* compendium of metazoan HGT (Spaulding & Fierst) lists the top recipient phyla as Rotifera, Arthropoda, Nematoda, Chordata, Porifera, Echinodermata and Mollusca. This ranking partly reflects research effort.

## Case Studies: Animals

**Whitefly *BtPMaT1* (Xia et al. 2021, *Cell*). Plant to insect; strongest functional case.**
- *Bemisia tabaci* carries a plant-type phenolic glucoside malonyltransferase. It was acquired roughly 35 million years ago and is found in no other insect.\[33\]
- The authors ruled out contamination; for example, the gene is expressed even in eggs.\[46\]
- RNAi and virus-induced silencing increased whitefly mortality on phenolic glycosides and on tomato plants. Plant-mediated silencing of *BtPMaT1* gave tomato "full resistance" to whiteflies.\[47\]\[48\]
- *Evidence: very strong. Benefit: demonstrated.* It is one of the clearest cases of a host's own defence gene being turned against it.

**Lepidopteran courtship gene *LOC105383139* (Li et al. 2022, *Cell*). Bacterium to insect.**
- The gene is present across Lepidoptera and of bacterial (*Listeria*-related) origin.\[5\]\[24\]
- CRISPR knockout in diamondback moth caused no morphological defects, but knockout males courted less vigorously and produced fewer offspring.\[5\]\[49\]
- *Evidence: strong. Benefit: demonstrated for reproductive fitness.* The selective history behind this function is unknown.

**Pea aphid carotenoid genes (Moran & Jarvik 2010, *Science*; the paper is often mis-cited as "Moran & Jarvela"). Fungus to insect.**
- The aphid genome encodes fungal-derived carotenoid biosynthesis genes (CrtYB-like lycopene cyclase/phytoene synthase, CrtI-like desaturases), integrated and duplicated. "Aphids are animals that make their own carotenoids."\[50\]\[51\]
- Red aphids carry a 30-kb region with an extra desaturase that green aphids lack. A point mutation abolishes torulene and red colour.\[50\]
- Ladybirds tend to prey on red morphs and parasitoid wasps on green, so the colour polymorphism is under balancing ecological selection.\[52\]
- Related fungal-derived genes occur in adelgids and phylloxerids (a single ancient transfer), gall midges and spider mites.\[53\]\[54\]
- *Evidence: very strong (insect flanking genes, multiple species). Benefit: strongly supported via colour polymorphism.*\[55\]

**Coffee berry borer *HhMAN1* (Acuña et al. 2012, *PNAS*). Bacterium to beetle.**
- The beetle has a mannanase, unknown elsewhere in insects, that hydrolyses coffee-bean galactomannan.\[56\]\[57\]
- The gene is present in all 37 accessions tested worldwide but absent from a non-coffee-feeding congener. It is flanked by transposon sequences.\[58\]\[59\]
- *Evidence: strong. Benefit: well inferred* (enzymology plus host specificity), not tested by fitness assay.

**Herbivore cell-wall enzymes.** Plant-cell-wall-degrading enzymes (cellulases, pectinases) were acquired from bacteria and fungi by plant-parasitic nematodes and several beetle lineages. They are a recurring theme of "HGT enables herbivory", and Li et al. (2022) and related reviews list them among insect adaptations. *Evidence: strong for several lineages; benefit: well inferred, with knockdown support in some nematodes.*

**Spider mites and other arthropods.** Spider mites (*Tetranychus*) carry fungal-type carotenoid genes.\[51\] Wybouw and colleagues showed that a bacterial-derived β-cyanoalanine synthase lets mites and some Lepidoptera detoxify host-plant cyanide. *Evidence: strong; benefit: functionally supported (enzymology).*

**Bdelloid rotifers. Asexual animal hotspot.**
- About 10% of transcripts in *Adineta ricciae* are foreign. Roughly 80% of foreign genes encode enzymes, making up 39% of enzymes in identified pathways (Boschetti et al. 2012, *PLoS Genetics*). Linkage to metazoan genes rules out contamination.
- Eyres et al. (2015) found nearly half of foreign genes were acquired before bdelloid families diverged over 60 million years ago. Transfer is ongoing, and more frequent in species from desiccating habitats.
- Nowell, Wilson, Barraclough et al. (2024, *Nature Communications*) found HGT genes were "over twice as likely to be upregulated as other genes" under attack by the fungal pathogen *Rotiferophthora*, making up about 23–32% of pathogen-upregulated genes versus about 14% of desiccation-responsive genes. Upregulation of horizontally acquired NRPS/PKS biosynthetic clusters was "nearly ten times stronger" in the resistant species, so HGT-derived biosynthetic genes are deployed against pathogens.
- Trehalose-6-phosphate synthase and trehalase genes of foreign origin are upregulated during desiccation.
- *Evidence: very strong. Benefit: increasingly supported* (expression, pathogen defence); causal links to asexuality remain hypotheses.

**Herring-to-smelt antifreeze protein (Graham et al. 2008, 2012; Graham & Davies 2021, *Trends in Genetics*). Vertebrate to vertebrate.**
- The type II lectin-like antifreeze gene in rainbow smelt is nearly identical to herring's, including introns that are more than 90–95% identical. The two lineages diverged over 200–250 million years ago.
- Flanking genes in smelt are smelt-like, and the gene is absent from relatives.
- Herring-specific transposons flank the smelt gene in the same order as in herring, which Graham calls "definitive proof."
- *Evidence: very strong. Benefit: compelling* (freeze avoidance in icy water); the proposed sperm-mediated mechanism is speculative.

**Tardigrades. The cautionary tale.**
- Boothby et al. (2015, *PNAS*) claimed 17.5% of *Hypsibius dujardini* genes (6,663) were foreign and speculated about links to cryptobiosis.\[60\]
- Koutsovoulos et al. (2016, *PNAS*) and Delmont & Eren (2016) showed the assembly was heavily contaminated by bacteria from non-axenic cultures. An independent assembly was 135 Mb rather than 252 Mb. Re-analysis suggests about 0.4–2% of genes are of foreign origin.\[18\]\[61\]\[62\]
- *Lesson:* claims of extraordinary HGT need independent assemblies, long reads, coverage and GC checks, and linkage to host genes.

**Humans and vertebrates in general.** Crisp et al. (2015, *Genome Biology*) reported 145 foreign genes in humans and "tens or hundreds" of active foreign genes across animals.\[63\]\[64\] Salzberg (2017) re-examined the highest-confidence genes and found "little or no evidence" for HGT. He attributed them to contamination, mitochondrial origin or failure to meet minimal standards.\[19\]\[65\] A 2026 *BMC Genomics* study again reports 10–237 HGT regions per species, including human, validated by long reads. Given the history, this deserves independent replication. Outside TEs and the antifreeze gene, confirmed adaptive gene HGT into vertebrates remains very scarce.

**Syncytins and endogenous retroviruses.** Syncytin genes are retroviral envelope genes captured independently in several mammal lineages. They mediate trophoblast cell fusion in the placenta: a demonstrated, essential function. They are horizontally *derived*, from infectious viruses, but entered by germline infection and endogenisation rather than transfer between cellular species. Most authors treat them as "virus-to-host HGT" or "gene co-option." They show that foreign sequence can underpin a major innovation even in mammals, which are otherwise refractory to HGT.

**Wolbachia insertions.** Wolbachia insertions are large, widespread and lowly expressed, as described above. They are the best example of transfer without demonstrated benefit. Most are probably neutral or decaying.

## Case Studies: Plants

**Fern neochrome (Li et al. 2014, *PNAS*). Hornwort to fern.**
- Neochrome is a chimeric red/blue photoreceptor (phytochrome plus phototropin). Fern neochromes nest within hornwort neochromes.
- The two diverged about 179 million years ago, long after the fern–hornwort split of at least 400 million years ago.
- The authors link it to the Cretaceous radiation of ferns in the shade of angiosperm canopies.
- *Evidence: strong (phylogeny plus dating). Benefit: plausible* through the photoreceptor's known function; the macroevolutionary link to diversification is correlational.

**Grass-to-grass transfers.** *Alloteropsis semialata* is the flagship case:
- It carries 59 laterally acquired genes in 23 fragments up to 170 kb, from at least nine donors. Most are expressed, with functions in photosynthesis, disease resistance and abiotic stress (Dunning et al. 2019, *PNAS*).\[9\]
- Earlier work (Christin et al. 2012) showed C4 photosynthesis genes, notably a phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, were acquired from distantly related C4 grasses.
- Population studies show selection acting on standing variation generated by these transfers in particular populations (Olofsson et al. 2019, *Current Biology*).\[66\]\[67\]
- Across grasses (Hibdige et al. 2021), LGT occurred in 13 of 17 species. Its frequency was predicted by number of genes tested, rhizomes and ploidy, and it rises with phylogenetic relatedness. Most acquired genes (79.4%) sit alongside the native ortholog rather than replacing it.\[17\]\[28\]\[68\]
- Specific gene clusters have moved between grass lineages: benzoxazinoid (*Bx*) defence genes into Triticeae, momilactone biosynthesis genes into rice, and a *Panicum*-derived stress-gene segment into *Hordeum*.\[24\]
- The 2025 preprint adds a 30-kb Triticeae-derived segment in the drought-tolerant *Cleistogenes songorica*. It also reports evidence that gramine biosynthetic clusters spread by inter-subfamily transfer.\[24\] Both claims are preprint-level.
- *Evidence: strong. Benefit: supported for specific genes; inferred broadly.*

**Fhb7 in *Thinopyrum* and wheat (Wang et al. 2020, *Science*). Fungus to grass.**
- *Fhb7* encodes a glutathione S-transferase that detoxifies trichothecene mycotoxins by de-epoxidation. It reportedly confers broad resistance to Fusarium head blight and crown rot without yield penalty.\[69\]\[70\]
- Its homologs are absent in plants but present in Epichloë endophytes; the protein is 97% identical. This suggests a fungus-to-grass transfer.\[71\]\[72\]
- **Caveats:** Guo et al. (2022, *Plants*) found *Fhb7* homologs in *Elymus*, *Leymus*, *Roegneria* and *Pseudoroegneria*, with proteins "at least 94% identical," suggesting the transfer "occurred before Triticeae differentiation." The resistance function is disputed in both directions: Guo et al. (2023, *Plant Biotechnology Journal*) reported that the GST-encoding *Fhb7* "failed to confer wheat FHB resistance," whereas Zhao et al. (2024, *Phytopathology*) found a gene-edited GST knockout raised susceptibility and "confirmed GST as the causal gene of Fhb7 for FHB resistance."
- *Evidence for HGT: strong. Evidence for the claimed resistance benefit: contested.* This is a good illustration that even high-profile functional claims need replication.

**Land-plant terrestrialisation genes.**
- Cheng et al. (2019, *Cell*) sequenced the subaerial algae *Spirogloea* and *Mesotaenium*. They argued that GRAS transcription factors and PYR/PYL/RCAR abscisic-acid receptors were gained from soil bacteria about 580 million years ago. These genes boost tolerance of desiccation and other stresses.\[15\]
- Ma et al. (2022, *Molecular Plant*) found two major HGT episodes, of 177 and 107 gene families, at the origin of streptophytes and of land plants. Of the 284 genes from these episodes, 63% were retained in seed plants.\[13\]\[14\]
- Critics note that ancient gene loss and older transfers are hard to exclude, and that functional tests are lacking.\[16\]\[73\]
- *Evidence: moderate. Benefit: plausible, inferred.*

**Parasitic plants.**
- *Striga hermonthica* acquired a sorghum gene (Yoshida et al. 2010).\[24\] *Rafflesia* carries numerous host-derived transcripts, and Orobanchaceae have many host genes.
- *Cuscuta* carries 108 transfers, expressed in haustoria. Some generate microRNAs that may target host defence genes.\[41\]\[74\]
- In *Cuscuta* mitochondria, a host-derived *atp1* has functionally replaced the native copy and is under purifying selection.\[10\]
- *Evidence: strong. Benefit: inferred from expression, convergence and selection signatures; rarely tested directly.*

**Naturally transgenic plants.** All 291 cultivated sweet potato accessions tested contained Agrobacterium T-DNA. One insert is fixed in cultivated clones but absent from close wild relatives, consistent with selection during domestication. Similar insertions occur in tobacco, *Linaria* and others. *Evidence: very strong for transfer; benefit: speculative* (possibly altered root or hormone traits).

**Mitochondrial-to-mitochondrial transfer.** *Amborella*'s six foreign mitochondrial genome-equivalents are the most spectacular case.\[75\] Plant mitochondrial HGT is widespread, especially into and out of parasitic plants. *Evidence: very strong; benefit: mostly none* (a non-adaptive by-product of mitochondrial fusion and low DNA loss).

## Evolutionary Significance: A Critical Synthesis

**HGT vs. other sources of novelty.** In plants and animals, gene duplication remains the dominant source of new genes. De novo gene birth from non-coding DNA and endosymbiotic gene transfer from mitochondria and plastids are each, in aggregate, far larger contributors than ongoing HGT. HGT is distinctive not in volume but in *kind*. It delivers fully formed, often biochemically novel functions that the recipient lineage could not easily evolve from its own genes:
- mannanases in a beetle;
- carotenoid synthesis in an animal;
- a plant detoxification enzyme in an insect;
- a chimeric photoreceptor in ferns;
- trichothecene detoxification in grasses.

This is "evolution by acquisition", a shortcut to traits that would otherwise require long mutational paths.

**Where HGT clusters in major transitions:**
- Colonisation of land by plants: GRAS and PYL genes, and the Ma et al. episodes.
- Herbivory and specialised diets: cell-wall enzymes, mannanase, *BtPMaT1*, cyanide detoxification.
- Parasitism: haustorial genes in parasitic plants.
- Extremophily and anhydrobiosis: bdelloids, but *not* tardigrades.
- Pigmentation: aphids and mites.
- Cold tolerance: smelt antifreeze.
- Light capture: neochrome.

The pattern is that HGT matters most where an organism enters a new ecological niche dominated by a chemical or physical challenge that a donor lineage had already solved. Often the donor shares the niche: soil bacteria for early land plants, host plants for whiteflies, endophytes for grasses.

**Domestication of foreign genes.** Successful transfers show a consistent signature:
- intron gain: Li et al. found insect-native intron sequences in HGT-acquired genes;
- shifts toward host codon usage and GC content;
- tissue-specific regulation, e.g., *BtPMaT1* in the gut and Cuscuta genes in haustoria;
- frequent duplication and expansion after arrival: aphid carotenoid genes, and the duplicated HGT regions reported in 2026.

Bracovirus genes are a special case. They were "pre-adapted" for expression in lepidopteran tissue, which may explain their repeated domestication.

**Neutral, selfish or adaptive?** Most horizontally transferred DNA in multicellular eukaryotes is probably *not* adaptive:
- Transposons (BovB, L1, the TE transfers in fishes) dominate by sequence volume. They are selfish elements whose spread reflects their own replication, although they later supply raw material such as regulatory sequences and co-opted genes.
- Wolbachia insertions and nuclear copies of mitochondrial DNA (numts) are mostly decaying passengers.
- Plant mitochondrial captures, as in *Amborella*, are by-products of fusion.
- Even among protein-coding HGT genes, many are lowly expressed or found only in single species, consistent with recent, possibly transient insertions.

The adaptive cases are a filtered minority. They are filtered by selection, which retains them, and by researchers, who preferentially study them. A fair statement is that most transfers are neutral or transient, while a minority become fixed and functionally central.

**Tree of life vs. web of life.** Soucy, Huang & Gogarten (2015, *Nature Reviews Genetics*) argued HGT "builds the web of life." For plants and animals, the data support a **tree with sparse reticulations** rather than a web. Species trees remain recoverable, and foreign genes are a per-mille-level overlay. Introgression and "remote introgression" in grasses add further reticulation at shallower depths. The web metaphor fits prokaryotes and many protists far better.

**Implications for crop breeding and biotechnology:**
- Naturally transgenic crops such as sweet potato undermine the claim that gene transfer across species barriers is intrinsically "unnatural." Kyndt et al. explicitly noted this may "change the paradigm governing the 'unnatural' status of transgenic crops." This is a philosophical point about naturalness, not a safety argument in itself: safety depends on the specific trait.
- Grass-to-grass transfer in crops raises questions about transgene escape. The Sheffield group has explicitly framed it as relevant to gene flow from GM crops.\[76\]
- HGT-derived genes are practical breeding targets: *Fhb7*, if its function is confirmed, and RNAi against *BtPMaT1* as a whitefly control.\[70\]\[77\]

## Controversies, Open Questions and Methodological Pitfalls

1. **Contamination and assembly artefacts.** These are the most common source of false HGT. Examples include the tardigrade case, the 2001 human genome claims, and the Wolbachia insert that the original *D. ananassae* assembly discarded as contamination (an error in the opposite direction).\[27\]\[78\] Safeguards:
   - independent assemblies and long-read sequencing;
   - coverage and GC "blob" plots;
   - PCR or long-read evidence linking the foreign gene to flanking host genes;
   - expression in axenic or germline tissue (e.g., *BtPMaT1* in eggs).
2. **Phylogenetic error.** Long-branch attraction, model misspecification, paralog confusion and compositional bias can all nest a host gene inside a foreign clade. Topology tests (AU, SH), multiple alignment and trimming schemes, and codon-position analyses are standard mitigations.
3. **Incomplete taxon sampling and differential gene loss.** A gene present in a species and in bacteria, but missing from sampled relatives, may reflect ancient presence plus repeated loss. This caveat applies most to ancient plant episodes and the GRAS and PYL claims. Dating, as in the neochrome case where divergence (179 Mya) is far younger than the lineage split (≥400 Mya), is one of the strongest discriminators.
4. **Incomplete lineage sorting and hybridisation.** Between plant relatives, incongruent gene trees may reflect incomplete lineage sorting or introgression rather than HGT. Grass studies now apply coalescent and branch-length tests to address this.\[24\]
5. **Reproducibility and selective reporting.** The field is now accumulating large automated surveys. Examples are the 2026 *BMC Genomics* report of 10–237 HGT regions per species, including humans, and the 2026 metazoan compendium. Their per-gene evidence varies, and the compendium assigns confidence scores. Independent re-analysis should be expected before such numbers are cited as settled.
6. **Best-practice standards.** Keeling & Palmer (2008, *Nature Reviews Genetics*) and Husnik & McCutcheon (2018, *Nature Reviews Microbiology*) emphasise a convergence of evidence:
   - robust phylogenetic incongruence with strong support;
   - a patchy distribution consistent with transfer rather than loss;
   - physical linkage to host genes in a high-quality assembly;
   - eukaryotic gene features (introns, host-like codon usage and polyadenylation);
   - presence across populations and related species;
   - expression in the host;
   - ideally, functional tests: knockdown, knockout or complementation.

   Irwin et al. (2022) extended systematic standards to eukaryote–virus exchanges. Only a handful of animal and plant cases meet all criteria: *BtPMaT1*, the lepidopteran courtship gene, aphid carotenoids, and smelt antifreeze.
7. **Open questions:**
   - The actual mechanisms of entry in vertebrates and non-parasitic plants: sperm-mediated uptake, vesicles, tick or mite vectors, root grafts or pollen?
   - Is the somatic HGT rate high but invisible? David Adelson (University of Adelaide) told *Quanta Magazine* in 2021: "How many times have we all been bitten by mosquitoes? My view is this happens at an astounding rate… I bet we're constantly being bombarded."
   - How often does HGT supply adaptive variation compared with standing variation and duplication, measured in an unbiased, genome-wide way?

## Concluding Assessment

**Eukaryotic HGT is best understood as both things at once, on different axes.** Measured by the *number of transfers and amount of DNA moved*, it is more common than once believed and largely neutral or selfish. Transposons hop between vertebrates, Wolbachia genomes lodge in insect chromosomes, and whole mitochondrial genomes fuse into *Amborella*. Measured by *contribution to adaptation*, it is a rare-but-important source of novelty. Tens of validated cases show genes conferring traits that would be hard to evolve otherwise, concentrated at ecological transitions such as herbivory, parasitism, terrestrialisation and extreme environments.

The premise in the original question needs one correction. HGT is not rare in plants and animals *because* it is special. It is rare because the barriers are high. Where it persists, it does so because selection retained a gene that solved a problem the donor had already solved, often in the same niche. The "interesting reason" behind most famous cases is ecological intimacy plus a germline loophole.

**My position:**
- Plants show substantially more eukaryote-to-eukaryote gene transfer than animals.
- Bdelloid rotifers and parasitic plants are genuine outliers.
- Headline per-genome percentages above a few tenths of a percent in typical animals should be treated with suspicion until replicated.

**What would resolve the remaining uncertainty:**
1. Standardised, contamination-robust pipelines applied uniformly across thousands of telomere-to-telomere genomes, with fixed criteria so that rates are comparable across plants and animals.
2. Population-genomic data, as in *Alloteropsis*, to measure how often new transfers arise, are lost or sweep to fixation. This would separate neutral from adaptive retention.
3. Routine functional tests (CRISPR knockouts, RNAi, complementation) on random samples of HGT genes, not only on attractive candidates, to estimate the true adaptive fraction.
4. Direct experimental detection of transfer in real time, e.g., through grafts, haustoria, tick bites or fish spawning, using long-read and single-cell sequencing to establish mechanisms and germline entry rates.
