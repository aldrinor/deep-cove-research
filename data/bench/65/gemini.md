# Control-Theoretic Modeling, Active Perception, and Computational Geometry for High-Throughput 3D Crop Grain Phenotyping

## 1. System Architecture and Digital Twin Maturity in Micro-Scale Grain Phenomics

Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction and phenotypic analysis of harvested crop organs—specifically cereal caryopses and legume seeds such as wheat (*Triticum aestivum* ), maize (*Zea mays* ), rice (*Oryza sativa* ), and oats (*Avena sativa* )—represent a foundational pillar of modern quantitative genetics, precision breeding, and post-harvest quality assessment [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Unlike whole-plant canopy phenotyping, which operates at centimeter-level tolerances in natural light [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/), single-grain micro-phenotyping demands micron-scale spatial resolution to resolve subtle morphological boundaries, embryonic structures, and surface concavities [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Traditional two-dimensional (2D) silhouette and reflection-based commercial seed analyzers suffer from inherent projection ambiguities that distort volumetric and gravimetric predictions [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Empirical evaluations comparing manual ground-truth measurements against automated 2D and mirror-assisted platforms demonstrate statistically significant systematic biases in Thousand Grain Weight (TGW) and axial length distributions [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30). Specifically, the MARVIN optical analyzer systematically overestimates plant-level TGW by a mean difference of +1.5 g+1.5\text{ g} (Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test, p≤0.0001p \le 0.0001) and skews grain length distributions toward elongated outliers [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30). Conversely, artificial neural network predictions from the Cgrain platform (TGWANN\text{TGW}_{\text{ANN}}) underestimate TGW by −2.7 g-2.7\text{ g} (p<0.0001p < 0.0001) while truncating oat grain length distributions at a strict ceiling of 19.2 mm19.2\text{ mm} (p<0.0001p < 0.0001), whereas only geometric volume-derived calculations (TGWCalc\text{TGW}_{\text{Calc}}) achieve statistical parity with manual weighing (p=0.22p = 0.22) [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30). Furthermore, 2D optical silhouettes are mathematically incapable of observing re-entrant surface concavities such as the wheat ventral sulcus (crease), a critical morphological determinant of endosperm-to-bran ratio and industrial flour extraction yield [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

To justify the computational and mechatronic overhead of 3D reconstruction over simpler 2D or spectral baselines, the system architecture must be structured around a formal Reconstruction–Trait–Task–Maturity Framework [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/15/13/1992). Within this systems engineering paradigm, high-fidelity geometric capture is decoupled from downstream biological decision support across four hierarchical maturity tiers [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/15/13/1992):

| Digital Twin Maturity Level | Classification | Mathematical & Operational Definition in Grain Phenomics | Primary Output & Decision Coupling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Level 0** | Static 3D Model | Uncalibrated multi-view point cloud or polygon mesh representation lacking absolute physical scale or semantic organ partitioning [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/15/13/1992). | Visual inspection and qualitative morphological rendering [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/15/13/1992). |
| **Level 1** | Metric Geometric Twin | Isotropically scaled, coordinate-normalized watertight mesh or dense point cloud verified against physical reference standards via Hausdorff distance metrics [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/15/13/1992). | Metric surface geometry in canonical Euclidean coordinates (mm,mm2,mm3\text{mm}, \text{mm}^2, \text{mm}^3) [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/15/13/1992). |
| **Level 2** | Validated Phenotypic Twin | Automated extraction and statistical validation (R2,MAPE,RMSER^2, \text{MAPE}, \text{RMSE}) of primary and derived morphological parameters, including occluded ventral sulcus traits [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). | High-dimensional trait vectors (e.g., 32-parameter wheat kernel matrices) for QTL mapping [12](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.840908/full). |
| **Level 3** | Process-Coupled Functional Twin | Explicit dynamical coupling of validated 3D seed geometry with physiological mass-transfer models, milling fracture mechanics, or ensemble gravimetric predictors [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). | Closed-loop breeding selection, automated seed sorting, and flour yield optimization [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). |

Transitioning a seed phenotyping platform from Level 0 to Level 3 requires treating data acquisition, optical projection, surface reconstruction, and trait extraction as an interconnected, closed-loop dynamical system rather than a sequence of isolated software scripts [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). In a high-throughput rotary and robotic scanning configuration, mechanical vibrations of the seed stage, velocity tracking lags in camera positioning, optical diffraction limits, and point-cloud density variations directly propagate into volumetric integration errors and crease depth miscalculations [15](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/13/11/2232). Consequently, modern control theory—encompassing state-space disturbance rejection, sliding mode control with explicit vibration compensation, model predictive trajectory shaping, and second-order image-based visual servoing—serves as the mathematical backbone ensuring that physical acquisition satisfies the strict boundary conditions of computational geometry [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

## 2. Opto-Mechanical Coupling: Diffraction Limits, Motion Blur Kinematics, and State Constraints

A foundational design error in agricultural imaging systems is treating optical sensor specification and mechanical motion control as independent problems [21](https://www.vision-doctor.com/en/camera/exposure-time-area-scan-camera.html). In micro-phenotyping of crop grains, the optical physics of macro magnification directly dictates the permissible state-space bounds of the mechanical positioning stage [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). Typical cereal grains span 4 mm4\text{ mm} to 19 mm19\text{ mm} in length, requiring a tight horizontal field of view (FOVwidth\text{FOV}_{\text{width}}) between 15 mm15\text{ mm} and 60 mm60\text{ mm} to resolve fine morphological structures such as the wheat brush, germ, and ventral crease [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30).

### Spatial Sampling, Nyquist Ceilings, and Working Diffraction Limits

Given an area-scan global-shutter sensor with horizontal pixel count HpixelsH_{\text{pixels}} and physical pixel pitch ppitch (mm)p_{\text{pitch}}\text{ (mm)}, the object-space sampling resolution Rs (mm/pixel)R_s\text{ (mm/pixel)} and the sensor Nyquist spatial frequency limit fNyquist (line pairs per mm, lp/mm)f_{\text{Nyquist}}\text{ (line pairs per mm, lp/mm)} are governed by [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision):

Rs=FOVwidthHpixels,fNyquist=12ppitchR_s = \frac{\text{FOV}_{\text{width}}}{H_{\text{pixels}}}, \quad f_{\text{Nyquist}} = \frac{1}{2 p_{\text{pitch}}}

While the theoretical sampling floor requires a minimum of 2 pixels2\text{ pixels} across a target feature under ideal contrast, real-world optical Modulation Transfer Function (MTF) roll-off, sensor readout noise, and low-contrast seed coat textures necessitate a 2×2\times to 3×3\times engineering design margin, establishing a practical requirement of 5 pixels5\text{ pixels} across the smallest phenotypic feature of interest [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). For instance, resolving a 0.2 mm0.2\text{ mm} micro-fissure or crease boundary margin across 5 pixels5\text{ pixels} requires an object-space resolution of Rs=0.04 mm/pixelR_s = 0.04\text{ mm/pixel} (40 μm/pixel40\,\mu\text{m/pixel}) [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision).

However, shrinking the pixel pitch ppitchp_{\text{pitch}} to increase HpixelsH_{\text{pixels}} introduces a severe optical constraint governed by wave diffraction [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). Any macro lens stops down light into an Airy diffraction pattern whose central bright disk (containing approximately 84%84\% of the radiant energy) has a physical diameter dAiryd_{\text{Airy}} defined in close-up macro conjugates by [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision):

dAiry=2.44λNw,Nw=N(1+∣m∣p)d_{\text{Airy}} = 2.44 \lambda N_w, \quad N_w = N \left( 1 + \frac{|m|}{p} \right)

where λ\lambda is the illumination wavelength (typically 550 nm550\text{ nm} for broadband visible center or 450 nm450\text{ nm} for blue structured light), NN is the nominal lens f-number, mm is the optical magnification ratio, and pp is the pupil magnification factor [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). At far conjugates (λ=550 nm\lambda = 550\text{ nm}), closing the aperture from F/4F/4 to F/16F/16 expands dAiryd_{\text{Airy}} from 5.4 μm5.4\,\mu\text{m} to 21.5 μm21.5\,\mu\text{m}, matching the pixel pitch marker near F/5.1F/5.1 for 3.45 μm3.45\,\mu\text{m} sensors, F/4.1F/4.1 for 2.74 μm2.74\,\mu\text{m} sensors, and F/2.3F/2.3 for 1.55 μm1.55\,\mu\text{m} sensors [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). In macro grain imaging where magnification ∣m∣∈[0.5,2.0]|m| \in [0.5, 2.0] is required, increasing NN to expand the depth of field across a curved seed surface rapidly inflates the working f-number NwN_w, causing the Airy disk to spill across multiple pixels and degrading high-frequency MTF prior to point cloud triangulation [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision).

### Continuous-Time Motion Blur Formulation and Differentiable Velocity Bounds

Because macro imaging demands moderately stopped-down apertures to maintain depth of field across the seed volume, photon irradiance at the sensor plane is restricted, requiring finite effective camera exposure intervals t0∈[0.1 ms,20 ms]t_0 \in [0.1\text{ ms}, 20\text{ ms}] unless high-intensity strobed illumination is deployed [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). Furthermore, rolling-shutter CMOS sensors must be strictly excluded from dynamic or high-throughput rotary and conveyor grain phenotyping; sequential row exposure during object motion induces non-affine geometric shear that corrupts multi-view epipolar geometry [23](https://www.machine-vision-shop.com/blog/machine-vision-exposure-time-calculation). Even with a global-shutter sensor, any relative motion between the grain and the optical axis during the effective integration window t∈[−t0/2,t0/2]t \in [-t_0/2, t_0/2] smears the radiant irradiance across adjacent pixels [21](https://www.vision-doctor.com/en/camera/exposure-time-area-scan-camera.html).

Let I(p)I(\mathbf{p}) represent the ideal, unblurred 2D continuous spatial image at pixel coordinate p=[px,py]⊤\mathbf{p} = [p_x, p_y]^\top, and let rp(t)=[xp(t),yp(t)]⊤\mathbf{r}_p(t) = [x_p(t), y_p(t)]^\top denote the projected trajectory of a grain surface point across the sensor plane during exposure period t0t_0 [25](http://www.eecs.northwestern.edu/~sda690/MfB/Motion_CVPR08.pdf). The captured blurred image Ib(p)I_b(\mathbf{p}) is given by the temporal integral [25](http://www.eecs.northwestern.edu/~sda690/MfB/Motion_CVPR08.pdf):

Ib(p)=1t0∫−t0/2t0/2I(rp(t)) dt≈g(1N∑i=1NIiν)I_b(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{1}{t_0} \int_{-t_0/2}^{t_0/2} I(\mathbf{r}_p(t))\,dt \approx g\left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N I_i^\nu \right)

where g(⋅)g(\cdot) denotes the camera radiometric response function over NN discretized virtual sub-frames IiνI_i^\nu [25](http://www.eecs.northwestern.edu/~sda690/MfB/Motion_CVPR08.pdf). For space-invariant linear motion with blur vector b=[u,v]⊤=[lcos⁡θ,lsin⁡θ]⊤\mathbf{b} = [u, v]^\top = [l\cos\theta, l\sin\theta]^\top, the image formation simplifies to the convolution Ib=I∗hI_b = I * h, which obeys the fundamental directional gradient constraint ∇Ib∣p⋅b=I(p+b/2)−I(p−b/2)\nabla I_b|_{\mathbf{p}} \cdot \mathbf{b} = I(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{b}/2) - I(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{b}/2) [25](http://www.eecs.northwestern.edu/~sda690/MfB/Motion_CVPR08.pdf). Decomposing the foreground seed from the background stage via an alpha matte channel α∈[0,1]\alpha \in [0, 1] such that I=αF+(1−α)BI = \alpha F + (1 - \alpha)B yields the blurred alpha boundary constraint ∇αb⋅b=α(p+b/2)−α(p−b/2)∈{0,±1}\nabla \alpha_b \cdot \mathbf{b} = \alpha(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{b}/2) - \alpha(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{b}/2) \in \{0, \pm 1\} [25](http://www.eecs.northwestern.edu/~sda690/MfB/Motion_CVPR08.pdf). Crucially, this proves mathematically that motion blur directly widens the alpha transition band along the perimeter of the seed silhouette, eroding boundary sharpness and corrupting 3D hull reconstruction [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/).

When a grain undergoes 3D rotation and translation on a motorized stage, the pixel smear vector F=[Fx,Fy]⊤\mathbf{F} = [F_x, F_y]^\top across exposure duration t0t_0 is space-variant and governed by the 3D motion field equations mapping the relative camera-object translational displacement t=[tx,ty,tz]⊤∈R3\mathbf{t} = [t_x, t_y, t_z]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3 and angular rotation θ=[θx,θy,θz]⊤∈R3\boldsymbol{\theta} = [\theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3 at scene depth dd and focal length ff [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17358v3):

[FxFy]=[−fd0pxdpxpyf−px2+f2fpy0−fdpydpy2+f2f−pxpyf−px][txtytzθxθyθz]=A(p,d)x\begin{bmatrix} F_x \\ F_y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{f}{d} & 0 & \frac{p_x}{d} & \frac{p_x p_y}{f} & -\frac{p_x^2 + f^2}{f} & p_y \\ 0 & -\frac{f}{d} & \frac{p_y}{d} & \frac{p_y^2 + f^2}{f} & -\frac{p_x p_y}{f} & -p_x \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} t_x \\ t_y \\ t_z \\ \theta_x \\ \theta_y \\ \theta_z \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{p}, d)\mathbf{x}

While a blur of 0.50.5 to 2.0 pixels2.0\text{ pixels} is often imperceptible to human visual inspection, smear exceeding 0.5 pixels0.5\text{ pixels} measurably degrades sub-pixel feature matching in Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and blurs high-frequency covariance splats in 3D Gaussian Splatting [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). Enforcing the strict metrological threshold ∥F∥∞≤bmax⁡=0.5 pixels\|\mathbf{F}\|_\infty \le b_{\max} = 0.5\text{ pixels} defines the maximum allowable projected object velocity vproj,max⁡v_{\text{proj},\max} (or residual structural vibration velocity during stop-and-shoot capture) as a direct function of object-space resolution RsR_s and effective exposure t0t_0 [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision):

For a micro-phenotyping system operating at Rs=0.025 mm/pixelR_s = 0.025\text{ mm/pixel} (25 μm/pixel25\,\mu\text{m/pixel}) with an effective exposure of t0=1.0 mst_0 = 1.0\text{ ms}, the instantaneous relative velocity between the grain surface and the camera frame must not exceed vproj,max⁡=12.5 mm/sv_{\text{proj},\max} = 12.5\text{ mm/s} [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). On a rotary turntable of radius r=25 mmr = 25\text{ mm}, this restricts residual angular velocity during exposure to ωmax⁡=vproj,max⁡/r=0.5 rad/s\omega_{\max} = v_{\text{proj},\max} / r = 0.5\text{ rad/s}, establishing a hard state-space boundary condition for the mechanical controller [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision).

## 3. Modern Control Theory for High-Throughput Mechatronic Acquisition

High-throughput 3D seed phenotyping platforms operate in one of two mechatronic regimes: indexed step-and-shoot rotary acquisition, where a turntable rotates the specimen through discrete angular increments (empirically optimized at Δθ=30∘\Delta\theta = 30^\circ per step with a 37∘37^\circ camera elevation angle over a matte black stage for wheat grains) [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/), or continuous-velocity synchronized scanning along a linear conveyor or continuous rotary stage [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0825/12/1/31). In both regimes, rapid acceleration and deceleration excite structural elastic modes in the stage and camera gantry, while bearing friction, cogging torque, and payload variations introduce nonlinear uncertainties [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19).

### State-Space Formulation of the Coupled Electro-Mechanical Stage

Consider a direct-drive rotary turntable or Permanent Magnet Linear Synchronous Motor (PMLSM) positioning stage coupled to an elastic structural frame [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19). Let q=[q1,q2]⊤\mathbf{q} = [q_1, q_2]^\top represent the generalized displacement coordinates (e.g., rotary stage angle θ\theta and radial/axial structural deflection, or dual-axis translational coordinates), and let qd(t)\mathbf{q}_d(t) denote the desired reference trajectory [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19). In continuous-time state-space form, the nominal plant dynamics with coupled structural mass M\mathbf{M}, damping C\mathbf{C}, stiffness K\mathbf{K}, nonlinear friction Ff(q˙)\mathbf{F}_f(\dot{\mathbf{q}}), and external disturbance d(t)\mathbf{d}(t) are expressed as [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19):

Mq¨(t)+Cq˙(t)+Kq(t)+Ff(q˙)=Buu(t)+d(t)\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t) + \mathbf{C}\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t) + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}(t) + \mathbf{F}_f(\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = \mathbf{B}_u \mathbf{u}(t) + \mathbf{d}(t)

Defining the state vector x=[x1⊤,x2⊤]⊤=[q⊤,q˙⊤]⊤∈R2n\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{x}_1^\top, \mathbf{x}_2^\top]^\top = [\mathbf{q}^\top, \dot{\mathbf{q}}^\top]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{2n}, the state-space dynamical system takes the canonical nonlinear affine form [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0825/12/1/31):

x˙(t)=f(x,t)+B(x,t)u(t)+d~(x,t)\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, t) + \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}, t)\mathbf{u}(t) + \tilde{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{x}, t)

where u(t)∈Rm\mathbf{u}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m is the actuator control input (motor current or voltage) and d~(x,t)\tilde{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{x}, t) represents the lumped parametric uncertainties and external disturbances, which are physically bounded under admissible operating conditions such that ∥d~(x,t)∥≤D<∞\|\tilde{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{x}, t)\| \le D < \infty [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0825/12/1/31).

### Sliding Mode Control with Explicit Vibration Model Compensation (SMC-VMC)

Sliding Mode Control (SMC) is highly attractive for phenotyping stage regulation due to its finite-time convergence and theoretical invariance to matched uncertainties once the system trajectory is confined to a prescribed sliding manifold [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0825/12/1/31). Defining the tracking position error e(t)=q(t)−qd(t)\mathbf{e}(t) = \mathbf{q}(t) - \mathbf{q}_d(t) and velocity error e˙(t)=q˙(t)−q˙d(t)\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t) = \dot{\mathbf{q}}(t) - \dot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t), the linear sliding surface vector s(x)∈Rm\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^m is formulated as [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0825/12/1/31):

s(x)=e˙(t)+Λe(t),Λ=diag(λ1,…,λm)>0\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}) = \dot{\mathbf{e}}(t) + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{e}(t), \quad \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m) > 0

When the reaching condition drives the state trajectory onto the sliding manifold s(x)=0\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0} in finite time, the restricted error dynamics satisfy e˙(t)=−Λe(t)\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t) = -\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{e}(t), guaranteeing exponential asymptotic convergence of both position and velocity errors toward the origin [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0825/12/1/31).

However, a critical engineering flaw arises when applying baseline uncompensated SMC to optical phenotyping stages [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19). In standard SMC formulations, structural vibration forces (Fvib=Cq˙+Kq\mathbf{F}_{\text{vib}} = \mathbf{C}\dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}) are omitted from the nominal model and treated as matched external disturbances to be overpowered by the discontinuous switching law usw=−Kssgn(s)\mathbf{u}_{\text{sw}} = -\mathbf{K}_s \text{sgn}(\mathbf{s}) [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19):

uuncomp(t)=B^u−1M^(q¨d−Λe˙)+B^u−1F^f(q˙)−Ks,high sgn(s)\mathbf{u}_{\text{uncomp}}(t) = \hat{\mathbf{B}}_u^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{M}}\left( \ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d - \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\dot{\mathbf{e}} \right) + \hat{\mathbf{B}}_u^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_f(\dot{\mathbf{q}}) - \mathbf{K}_{s,\text{high}}\,\text{sgn}(\mathbf{s})

Because the switching gain matrix Ks,high=diag(ks1,…,ksm)\mathbf{K}_{s,\text{high}} = \text{diag}(k_{s1}, \dots, k_{sm}) must strictly exceed the upper bound of the unmodeled elastic restoring and damping forces (ksi>∥Fvib,i+di∥∞k_{si} > \Vert{}\mathbf{F}_{\text{vib},i} + d_i\Vert{}_\infty), the controller injects aggressive high-frequency switching chatter into the actuator [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19). In an optical phenotyping setup, this control chattering excites unmodeled high-frequency resonant modes in the camera mount and seed stage, directly violating the sub-pixel velocity bound vproj,max⁡v_{\text{proj},\max} and causing severe image smear [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19). Attempting to suppress chattering purely by replacing sgn(s)\text{sgn}(\mathbf{s}) with a continuous saturation boundary layer sat(s/ϕ)\text{sat}(\mathbf{s}/\boldsymbol{\phi}) degrades positioning precision, as the states only converge to a residual neighborhood of width ϕ\boldsymbol{\phi} rather than the exact angular indexing target [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19).

To resolve this fundamental trade-off, the stage controller must implement Sliding Mode Control with Explicit Vibration Model Compensation (SMC-VMC) [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19). Rather than treating structural vibrations as unknown disturbances or smoothing the switching function, SMC-VMC incorporates the identified mass-spring-damper modal parameters (C^,K^\hat{\mathbf{C}}, \hat{\mathbf{K}}) directly as feedforward cancellation terms inside the equivalent control law ueq(t)\mathbf{u}_{\text{eq}}(t) [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19):

uSMC-VMC(t)=B^u−1[M^(q¨d−Λe˙)+C^q˙(t)+K^q(t)+F^f(q˙)]⏟Vibration-Compensated Equivalent Control ueq(t)−Ks,low sgn(s(t))⏟Reduced-Gain Switching usw(t)\mathbf{u}_{\text{SMC-VMC}}(t) = \underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{B}}_u^{-1}\left[ \hat{\mathbf{M}}\left(\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d - \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\dot{\mathbf{e}}\right) + \hat{\mathbf{C}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t) + \hat{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{q}(t) + \hat{\mathbf{F}}_f(\dot{\mathbf{q}}) \right]}_{\text{Vibration-Compensated Equivalent Control } \mathbf{u}_{\text{eq}}(t)} - \underbrace{\mathbf{K}_{s,\text{low}}\,\text{sgn}(\mathbf{s}(t))}_{\text{Reduced-Gain Switching } \mathbf{u}_{\text{sw}}(t)}

To verify stability, consider the candidate Lyapunov function V(s)=12s⊤Ms>0V(\mathbf{s}) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{s}^\top \mathbf{M} \mathbf{s} > 0 [32](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/image-based-visual-servoing-ibvs). Differentiating V(s)V(\mathbf{s}) along the system trajectories and substituting uSMC-VMC(t)\mathbf{u}_{\text{SMC-VMC}}(t) yields:

V˙(s)=s⊤(Δ~param(x)+d(t)−BuKs,low sgn(s))≤−∑i=1mηi∣si∣<0(∀s≠0)\dot{V}(\mathbf{s}) = \mathbf{s}^\top \left( \tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\text{param}}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{d}(t) - \mathbf{B}_u \mathbf{K}_{s,\text{low}}\,\text{sgn}(\mathbf{s}) \right) \le -\sum_{i=1}^m \eta_i |s_i| < 0 \quad (\forall \mathbf{s} \neq \mathbf{0})

Because the feedforward terms C^q˙\hat{\mathbf{C}}\dot{\mathbf{q}} and K^q\hat{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{q} explicitly cancel the dominant elastic and damping forces, the switching gain Ks,low\mathbf{K}_{s,\text{low}} only needs to bound the small parametric estimation residual Δ~param(x)\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\text{param}}(\mathbf{x}) rather than the full vibration magnitude [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19). ## 4. Active Perception and Second-Order Image-Based Visual Servoing (IBVS)

When phenotyping asymmetric crop seeds—or when scanning intact spikes, panicles, and multi-grain trays—fixed-trajectory camera paths frequently suffer from self-occlusion, depth-of-field defocus, or incomplete coverage of the ventral sulcus [14](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Correction-principle-of-the-fisheye-image-based-on-the-virtual-SF-camera_fig1_236599226). Integrating a robotic Image-Based Visual Servoing (IBVS) subsystem enables closed-loop camera pose regulation directly in the 2D image feature space without requiring complete *a priori*  3D scene reconstruction [14](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Correction-principle-of-the-fisheye-image-based-on-the-virtual-SF-camera_fig1_236599226).

### Kinematic Interaction Matrix and Second-Order Dynamic Visual Servoing

Let a set of nn salient 3D morphological landmarks on the grain surface have coordinates Pi=[Xi,Yi,Zi]⊤\mathbf{P}_i = [X_i, Y_i, Z_i]^\top in the instantaneous camera frame FC\mathcal{F}_C, projecting to normalized perspective image coordinates si=[xi,yi]⊤=[Xi/Zi,Yi/Zi]⊤\mathbf{s}_i = [x_i, y_i]^\top = [X_i/Z_i, Y_i/Z_i]^\top [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/). Stacking nn point features yields the visual feature vector s∈R2n\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{2n}, whose first-order time derivative relates to the relative camera-object spatial velocity screw (twist) τ=[vc⊤,ωc⊤]⊤=[vx,vy,vz,ωx,ωy,ωz]⊤∈R6\boldsymbol{\tau} = [\mathbf{v}_c^\top, \boldsymbol{\omega}_c^\top]^\top = [v_x, v_y, v_z, \omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^6 via the analytical interaction matrix (image Jacobian) Ls(s,Z)∈R2n×6\mathbf{L}_s(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{Z}) \in \mathbb{R}^{2n \times 6} [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/):

s˙=Ls(s,Z)τ,Ls,i(xi,yi,Zi)=[−1Zi0xiZixiyi−(1+xi2)yi0−1ZiyiZi1+yi2−xiyi−xi]\dot{\mathbf{s}} = \mathbf{L}_s(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{Z})\boldsymbol{\tau}, \quad \mathbf{L}_{s,i}(x_i, y_i, Z_i) = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{1}{Z_i} & 0 & \frac{x_i}{Z_i} & x_i y_i & -(1 + x_i^2) & y_i \\ 0 & -\frac{1}{Z_i} & \frac{y_i}{Z_i} & 1 + y_i^2 & -x_i y_i & -x_i \end{bmatrix}

In classical kinematic IBVS, imposing an exponentially decaying visual feature error e˙s=−λes=−λ(s(t)−s∗)\dot{\mathbf{e}}_s = -\lambda \mathbf{e}_s = -\lambda(\mathbf{s}(t) - \mathbf{s}^*) yields the proportional Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse velocity command τref=−λLs+(s(t)−s∗)\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ref}} = -\lambda \mathbf{L}_s^+ (\mathbf{s}(t) - \mathbf{s}^*) [17](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/14/4626). However, classical first-order IBVS relies on the idealized assumption that the robotic manipulator tracks velocity commands instantaneously as a unit step response (Vˉe(n)=Ve,ref(n)\bar{\mathbf{V}}_e(n) = \mathbf{V}_{e,\text{ref}}(n)) [17](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/14/4626). In physical agricultural robotic manipulators, structural link flexibility and actuator bandwidth limitations introduce discrete-time velocity dynamics of the form [17](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/14/4626):

Vˉe(n)=αVe(n−1)+βVe,ref(n),α∈(−1,1)\bar{\mathbf{V}}_e(n) = \alpha \mathbf{V}_e(n-1) + \beta \mathbf{V}_{e,\text{ref}}(n), \quad \alpha \in (-1, 1)

where α≠0\alpha \neq 0 induces phase lag between the computed visual velocity and the true end-effector velocity [17](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/14/4626). Furthermore, when the grain is simultaneously rotating on a non-inertial turntable, first-order kinematic mapping fails to account for relative accelerations and Coriolis couplings [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/).

To achieve high-speed, zero-lag active alignment between the optical head and the rotating seed, the control architecture must utilize the Second-Order Visual Servoing Interaction Model [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/). Differentiating s˙=Lsτ\dot{\mathbf{s}} = \mathbf{L}_s \boldsymbol{\tau} with respect to time in a non-inertial observation frame where ddtτ=a+[vc×ωc0]\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{a} + \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_c \times \boldsymbol{\omega}_c \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} yields the exact second-order feature acceleration dynamics [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/):

s¨=Ls(s,Z)a+Ls(s,Z)[vc×ωc0]+Lv(s,τ,Z),Lv,i=[τ⊤Ωx,i(si,Zi)ττ⊤Ωy,i(si,Zi)τ]\ddot{\mathbf{s}} = \mathbf{L}_s(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{Z})\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{L}_s(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{Z})\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_c \times \boldsymbol{\omega}_c \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} + \mathbf{L}_v(\mathbf{s}, \boldsymbol{\tau}, \mathbf{Z}), \quad \mathbf{L}_{v,i} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\tau}^\top \boldsymbol{\Omega}_{x,i}(\mathbf{s}_i, Z_i) \boldsymbol{\tau} \\ \boldsymbol{\tau}^\top \boldsymbol{\Omega}_{y,i}(\mathbf{s}_i, Z_i) \boldsymbol{\tau} \end{bmatrix}

where a∈R6\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^6 is the relative spatial camera-object acceleration screw and Lv\mathbf{L}_v encapsulates the quadratic velocity terms arising from the time derivative of the interaction matrix L˙sτ\dot{\mathbf{L}}_s \boldsymbol{\tau} and Coriolis acceleration [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/). By feeding s¨\ddot{\mathbf{s}} directly into the torque/acceleration-level SMC-VMC loop of the manipulator, the system compensates for both robot velocity lag and turntable centripetal acceleration [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19).

### Feature Conditioning, Singularity Avoidance, and Active View Selection

The choice of visual features and depth estimation architecture profoundly impacts closed-loop stability during seed inspection [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/). While planar image moments (mijm_{ij} of order i+ji+j) provide analytical interaction matrices Lmij\mathbf{L}_{m_{ij}} that represent global seed silhouettes, empirical evaluations under identical sensor noise demonstrate that normalized image point visual servoing achieves 2×2\times to 3×3\times higher position and force tracking accuracy than image moment visual servoing [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/). Consequently, tracking four or more non-collinear anatomical key points—such as the grain apex, embryo tip, and lateral crease extrema—is strongly preferred over silhouette moments for fine pose alignment [17](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/14/4626).

A second critical structural challenge stems from the conditioning of the interaction matrix Ls,i\mathbf{L}_{s,i}, whose translational columns scale inversely with vision depth ZiZ_i, whereas its rotational columns depend solely on normalized image coordinates (xi,yi)(x_i, y_i) [20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9198459/). During large angular reorientations around a grain, condition-number degradation in Ls\mathbf{L}_s causes the pseudoinverse controller to command spurious optical-axis translations (vz→∞v_z \to \infty, known as camera retreat) or become trapped in local minima lying in the nullspace Null(Ls+)\text{Null}(\mathbf{L}_s^+) [32](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/image-based-visual-servoing-ibvs). This pathology is eliminated through three complementary mechanisms: deploying a stereo macro rig or structured-light depth map to update ZiZ_i online [32](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/image-based-visual-servoing-ibvs); utilizing a depth-independent interaction matrix that decouples depth nonlinearity from rotational control [17](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/14/4626); and partitioning large rotational trajectories (such as transitions between 30∘30^\circ viewing stations) into smaller optimal sub-trajectories via Dynamic Programming while enforcing field-of-view visibility bounds via Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) [12](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.840908/full).

Furthermore, when reconstructing complex seed coats or multi-grain clusters via 3D Gaussian Splatting, uniform angular stepping can be refined by Residual-Driven Gaussian Uncertainty Active View Selection (RGU-AVS) or Gaussian Splatting Shannon Mutual Information (GauSS-MI) [40](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/20/4582). By projecting the 2D elliptical footprint of each 3D Gaussian primitive onto the image plane and intersecting it with a high-error rendering residual mask, the supervisory controller identifies under-reconstructed spatial regions—such as a shadowed ventral sulcus—and commands the IBVS loop to steer the camera directly toward the next-best-view pose that maximizes expected information gain [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329).

## 5. 3D Reconstruction Pipelines: Structured Light, SfM-MVS, and 3D Gaussian Splatting

Once multi-view image sequences or active structured-light fringe patterns are acquired under stabilized mechatronic control, the raw optical observations must be transformed into a metric 3D geometric representation (Level 1 Twin) [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Contemporary grain phenotyping relies on three primary reconstruction paradigms: Active Structured Light Scanning, Structure-from-Motion with Multi-View Stereo (SfM-MVS), and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/).

### Structured Light Triangulation vs. Photogrammetric SfM-MVS

Active structured light scanning projects known spatially modulated patterns (lines, grids, or phase-shifted sinusoidal stripes) onto the grain surface and triangulates 3D surface coordinates from the pattern deformation captured by a calibrated camera [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Because cereal kernels frequently exhibit low-texture pericarps that challenge passive feature matchers, structured light provides immediate, dense metric point clouds without requiring surface texture [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). However, ambient specular reflections from glossy seed coats (such as flint maize or polished rice) and background scattering can corrupt fringe decoding [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). Orthogonal experimental design across three factors—stage background color (black, red, white), turntable rotation step (30∘,45∘,60∘30^\circ, 45^\circ, 60^\circ), and scanner elevation angle (30∘,37∘,45∘30^\circ, 37^\circ, 45^\circ)—proves that structured-light wheat grain reconstruction achieves peak metrological accuracy using a matte black object stage, a 30∘30^\circ rotation angle increment (12 views per revolution12\text{ views per revolution}), and a 37∘37^\circ scanning angle [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). Validated against industrial X-ray Computed Tomography (CT) and manual micrometer measurements across 125 wheat grains125\text{ wheat grains}, this optimal structured-light configuration matches X-ray CT precision across length, width, thickness, and ventral sulcus depth while eliminating ionizing radiation, biological seed damage, and prohibitive CT scanning times [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

### Differentiable 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) and Adaptive Density Control

While passive SfM-MVS pipelines provide low-cost multi-view reconstruction, they frequently suffer from surface fragmentation, high computational latency, and loss of fine morphological detail on small seeds [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Recent breakthroughs in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) overcome these limitations by representing the seed surface as an explicit, continuously differentiable field of anisotropic 3D Gaussian ellipsoids initialized from a sparse SfM point cloud [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329).

In the initialization stage, multi-view seed images undergo feature extraction, RANSAC-based outlier rejection, sparse triangulation, and global Bundle Adjustment to optimize camera intrinsic and extrinsic matrices [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). Each sparse 3D point initializes a 3D Gaussian primitive parameterized by its world-coordinate mean position μ=[x,y,z]⊤∈R3\boldsymbol{\mu} = [x, y, z]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3, a 3×33 \times 3 spatial covariance matrix Σ∈R3×3\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}, an opacity scalar Oi∈[0,1]O_i \in [0, 1], and view-dependent Spherical Harmonics (SH) color coefficients ci\mathbf{c}_i [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329):

G(x)=exp⁡(−12(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ))G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right)

To guarantee that Σ\boldsymbol{\Sigma} remains positive semi-definite throughout gradient-based optimization, the covariance matrix is factorized into a 3D rotation matrix R\mathbf{R} (parameterized as a unit quaternion) and a diagonal scaling matrix S=diag(sx,sy,sz)\mathbf{S} = \text{diag}(s_x, s_y, s_z) [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329):

Σ=RSS⊤R⊤\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top

To synthesize a 2D image from a camera viewing transformation W\mathbf{W}, the 3D covariance Σ\boldsymbol{\Sigma} is projected onto the 2D image plane via the affine-approximated projective Jacobian matrix J\mathbf{J}, yielding the 2D splat covariance Σ′=JWΣW⊤J⊤\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{J}^\top [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). For each pixel, the set N\mathcal{N} of overlapping depth-ordered Gaussian splats is alpha-composited front-to-back to compute the rendered pixel color C\mathbf{C} [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329):

C=∑i∈Nciαi∏j=1i−1(1−αj),αi=Oiexp⁡(−12(xi′−μi′)⊤(Σ′)−1(xi′−μi′))\mathbf{C} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbf{c}_i \alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad \alpha_i = O_i \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}'_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}'_i)^\top (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}')^{-1} (\mathbf{x}'_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}'_i) \right)

Because the entire rasterization pipeline is differentiable, the Gaussian parameters {μi,Ri,Si,Oi,ci}\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{S}_i, O_i, \mathbf{c}_i\} are iteratively updated via Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) to minimize a composite photometric (L1\mathcal{L}_1) and structural similarity (LSSIM\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}) loss against the captured training views [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329):

L=(1−λ)L1+λLSSIM,λ=0.2\mathcal{L} = (1 - \lambda)\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}, \quad \lambda = 0.2

Crucially, from a control-theoretic perspective, 3DGS achieves superior micro-morphological fidelity through an internal Adaptive Density Control feedback heuristic that dynamically regulates the population of Gaussian primitives during training [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). In regions suffering from under-reconstruction—where existing 3D Gaussian primitives are insufficient to cover the seed surface and exhibit large view-space positional gradients ∇μ′L\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}'} \mathcal{L} paired with small Gaussian scaling ∥S∥≤τscale\|\mathbf{S}\| \le \tau_{\text{scale}}—the controller clones the existing primitives, duplicating their parameters and optimizing their positions to fill sparse volumetric voids [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). Conversely, in regions exhibiting over-reconstruction—where large Gaussian ellipsoids bridge across intricate features such as the wheat ventral crease or maize dent cavity without capturing fine detail—the solver segments and subdivides the oversized primitives into smaller Gaussians to maintain a balanced density distribution and resolve high-frequency surface curvature [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). Simultaneously, primitives whose opacity falls below an operational threshold or whose 2D elliptical footprint exhibits poor alignment with object masks are eliminated via adaptive ellipsoid deletion, while sparse-view self-supervised denoising (Denoise-GS) and 2D Gaussian Splatting object-aware mask losses enforce watertight surface geometry [40](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/20/4582). Quantitative evaluations across 150 seeds150\text{ seeds} (50 seeds50\text{ seeds} each of maize, rice, and wheat) confirm that 3DGS produces significantly denser, cleaner point clouds and achieves statistically superior phenotypic measurement accuracy compared to conventional SfM-MVS (p<0.05p < 0.05) [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329).

## 6. Point Cloud Preprocessing, Coordinate Normalization, and Deep Segmentation

Before geometric traits can be extracted from a reconstructed point cloud Praw={p1,…,pN}⊂R3\mathcal{P}_{\text{raw}} = \{\mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_N\} \subset \mathbb{R}^3, the system must isolate the target seed from the mounting stage, eliminate optical scattering artifacts, normalize the coordinate frame to a canonical metric basis, and segment individual organs or kernels [10](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2026.1883203/full).

### Multi-Stage Filtering and PCA Canonical Coordinate Normalization

A rigorous four-stage preprocessing pipeline is executed sequentially on raw point clouds to guarantee metric consistency [10](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2026.1883203/full). First, coarse background clutter is stripped via pass-through conditional filtering by enforcing axis-aligned bounding box inequalities Xd<x<XuX_d < x < X_u, Yd<y<YuY_d < y < Y_u, and Zd<z<ZuZ_d < z < Z_u around the calibrated rotary stage workspace [35](https://www.sciopen.com/local/article_pdf/10.25165/j.ijabe.20251804.9169.pdf). Second, to separate the grain point cloud from residual stage supports and line-of-sight occlusion artifacts, a KD-Tree nearest-neighbor search accelerates Euclidean clustering, partitioning P\mathcal{P} into discrete spatial clusters where inter-point Euclidean distances satisfy ∥pi−pj∥2<dth\Vert{}\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\Vert{}_2 < d_{\text{th}} [42](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.974339/full). Chromatic thresholding then removes boundary halo noise along the seed edges, followed by Statistical Outlier Removal (SOR) and voxel grid downsampling to eliminate spatially dispersed anomalies and standardize local point density [35](https://www.sciopen.com/local/article_pdf/10.25165/j.ijabe.20251804.9169.pdf).

Third, because monocular photogrammetric and 3DGS reconstructions recover geometry only up to an arbitrary similarity scale factor, an external geometric reference target of known physical dimensions is segmented within the scene [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Comparing the reconstructed horizontal span against the known physical standard determines a scene-specific isotropic scale factor smetrics_{\text{metric}}, which is multiplied uniformly across (X,Y,Z)(X, Y, Z) coordinates to convert the cloud into physical millimeters while avoiding anisotropic deformation [10](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2026.1883203/full). Fourth, because seeds rest at arbitrary orientations on the scanning stage, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) pose normalization is applied prior to bounding-box interrogation [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). Letting A=1M∑i=1Mpi∈R3\mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3 denote the centroid of the segmented single-grain point cloud T0∈R3×M\mathbf{T}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times M} [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/), the algorithm constructs the empirical spatial covariance matrix CP=1M∑i=1M(pi−A)(pi−A)⊤\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{P}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M (\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{A})(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{A})^\top and solves the eigenvalue decomposition CPvk=λkvk\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{v}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{v}_k (λ1≥λ2≥λ3>0\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3 > 0) to form the orthogonal rotation matrix MT=[v1,v2,v3]∈SO(3)\mathbf{M}_T = [\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3] \in SO(3) [10](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2026.1883203/full). Applying the rigid affine transformation [12](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.840908/full):

TA=MT⊤×(T0−A)\mathbf{T}_A = \mathbf{M}_T^\top \times (\mathbf{T}_0 - \mathbf{A})

translates the grain centroid to the origin (0,0,0)(0,0,0) and aligns the grain's major physiological length axis with the canonical XX-axis (PCA1\text{PCA}_1), its width axis with the YY-axis (PCA2\text{PCA}_2), and its thickness axis with the ZZ-axis (PCA3\text{PCA}_3) [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

### Deep Point Cloud Segmentation Networks and Mathematical Invariances

When phenotyping whole cereal spikes (wheat ears, rice panicles) or dense multi-seed trays, individual kernels must be segmented directly in 3D space or classified into filled versus unfilled grain categories [47](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Liang-Gong-6). Unlike 2D pixel grids or 3D voxel lattices, a point cloud P={p1,…,pM}\mathcal{P} = \{\mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_M\} (typically standardized to M=16,384 pointsM = 16,384\text{ points} for dense organ models) is an irregular, unordered set [48](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8025700/). Consequently, any deep 3D segmentation architecture F(P)→{γ1,…,γM}F(\mathcal{P}) \to \{\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_M\} deployed in the pipeline must strictly satisfy four foundational mathematical invariance axioms [49](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.608732/full):

| Network Invariance Axiom | Mathematical Formulation | Structural & Phenotypic Requirement in 3D Grain Analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Property I: Permutation Invariance** | [γi,γj]=[γj,γi], ∀i,j∈{1,…,M}[\gamma_i, \gamma_j] = [\gamma_j, \gamma_i], \; \forall i, j \in \{1, \dots, M\}[cite: 49] | Unordered 3D point sets lack raster indexing; semantic labels must remain invariant to point array ordering via symmetric pooling functions [49](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.608732/full). |
| **Property II: Transformation Invariance** | Γ(r[p1,…,pM]+t)=Γ[p1,…,pM]\Gamma(\mathbf{r}[\mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_M] + \mathbf{t}) = \Gamma[\mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_M][cite: 49] | Arbitrary camera viewpoints (rotations r\mathbf{r}), stage translations (t\mathbf{t}), and developmental scaling must not alter semantic organ partitioning [49](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.608732/full). |

Across agricultural phenotyping benchmarks, hierarchical point-set architectures satisfying these four axioms achieve exceptional segmentation fidelity: PointNet++ reaches a mean F1-score of 99.66%99.66\% on individual maize samples and 98.38%98.38\% mean precision (>96.00%>96.00\% F1-score) across multi-species evaluations, while also powering filled/unfilled rice grain classification from structured-light clouds [50](https://mail.ajaronline.com/index.php/AJAR/article/view/2003); PointNeXt incorporates Inverted Residual MLPs to accelerate multi-scale feature extraction on maize and sugarcane [53](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12709989/); PSegNet achieves 89.9%89.9\% mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) and 90.15%90.15\% mean recall [54](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12894392/); and PTV-SegCo integrates spatial structure and chromatic features to resolve ambiguous fine-organ boundaries [10](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2026.1883203/full).

## 7. Computational Geometry and the 32-Trait Grain & Ventral Sulcus Model

Once a single grain point cloud is segmented and aligned into its canonical PCA coordinate system TA\mathbf{T}_A, the pipeline transitions from Level 1 geometric representation to Level 2 validated phenotypic extraction and Level 3 gravimetric prediction [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

### Surface Triangulation: Convex Hulls vs. 3D Alpha Shapes

To compute volumetric and surface-area parameters, the discrete point set TA\mathbf{T}_A must be converted into a closed polyhedral surface mesh W\mathcal{W} consisting of kk triangular facets [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). A critical design distinction must be maintained between 3D Convex Hull (Quickhull / QHull) reconstruction and 3D Alpha Shape triangulation [15](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/13/11/2232). The 3D Convex Hull (Hconv\mathcal{H}_{\text{conv}}) computes the minimal convex polyhedron enclosing all points in TA\mathbf{T}_A [15](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/13/11/2232); while computationally rapid and useful for outer envelope estimation, a convex hull mathematically bridges over all re-entrant concavities, completely sealing over the wheat ventral sulcus and maize dent depression, which artificially inflates true seed volume VV and underestimates true surface area SaS_a [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/). Conversely, 3D Alpha Shapes (Aα\mathcal{A}_\alpha) and manifold Delaunay triangulation generalize the convex hull by introducing a pre-specified spatial size-criterion parameter α>0\alpha > 0 (where α→∞\alpha \to \infty recovers the convex hull) that controls the level of detail in the achieved triangulation by restricting simplex facet formation to local neighborhoods [15](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/13/11/2232). Because the alpha shape method is sensitive to the applied point density, coupling voxel-normalized 3DGS or structured-light clouds with an appropriately tuned α\alpha parameter produces watertight, high-resolution meshes that carve accurately into the ventral sulcus cavity [1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030611/).

To verify that the triangulated mesh W\mathcal{W} faithfully represents the underlying point cloud P\mathcal{P} without topological smoothing or hole-filling artifacts, the system evaluates the Hausdorff Distance dH(P,W)d_H(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{W}) [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/14/3/391):

dH(P,W)=max⁡{sup⁡p∈Pinf⁡w∈W∥p−w∥2, sup⁡w∈Winf⁡p∈P∥p−w∥2}d_H(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{W}) = \max \left\{ \sup_{\mathbf{p} \in \mathcal{P}} \inf_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}} \Vert{}\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{w}\Vert{}_2, \; \sup_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}} \inf_{\mathbf{p} \in \mathcal{P}} \Vert{}\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{w}\Vert{}_2 \right\}

which bounds the worst-case maximum deviation between the measured point set and the reconstructed triangular facets [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/14/3/391).

### Analytical Formulation of the 32 Wheat Grain and Ventral Sulcus Traits

From the PCA-normalized point cloud TA\mathbf{T}_A and its validated kk-facet watertight mesh W\mathcal{W}, the phenotypic extraction engine computes a comprehensive 32-dimensional trait vector comprising 28 whole-grain 3D morphological traits and 4 specialized ventral sulcus (crease) parameters (partitioned into 16 primary basic parameters and 16 derived allometric ratios) [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

#### Canonical Axial Dimensions and Oriented Bounding Box (VobbV_{\text{obb}})

From the coordinate extrema (xmax⁡′,xmin⁡′,ymax⁡′,ymin⁡′,zmax⁡′,zmin⁡′)(x'_{\max}, x'_{\min}, y'_{\max}, y'_{\min}, z'_{\max}, z'_{\min}) of the PCA-transformed grain cloud TA\mathbf{T}_A, the fundamental orthogonal dimensions and oriented bounding box volume are extracted directly [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/):

l=xmax⁡′−xmin⁡′,w=ymax⁡′−ymin⁡′,h=zmax⁡′−zmin⁡′,Vobb=l⋅w⋅hl = x'_{\max} - x'_{\min}, \quad w = y'_{\max} - y'_{\min}, \quad h = z'_{\max} - z'_{\min}, \quad V_{\text{obb}} = l \cdot w \cdot h

#### Surface Area (SaS_a), Mesh Volume (V0V_0), and 3D Sphericity (EE)

For each of the kk triangular facets on the grain surface mesh with edge lengths (ai,bi,ci)(a_i, b_i, c_i) and semi-perimeter pi=12(ai+bi+ci)p_i = \frac{1}{2}(a_i + b_i + c_i), the facet area sis_i is computed via Heron's formula and summed to yield total grain surface area SaS_a [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329):

Sa=∑i=1ksi=∑i=1kpi(pi−ai)(pi−bi)(pi−ci)S_a = \sum_{i=1}^k s_i = \sum_{i=1}^k \sqrt{p_i(p_i - a_i)(p_i - b_i)(p_i - c_i)}

Selecting an interior reference point (the centroid origin 0\mathbf{0}) forms kk elementary tetrahedra with the surface facets; summing the signed tetrahedral volumes with perpendicular altitude hih_i yields the exact grain volume V0V_0 [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329):

V0=∑i=1kVi=∑i=1k13sihiV_0 = \sum_{i=1}^k V_i = \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{3} s_i h_i

From SaS_a and V0V_0, the true 3D Sphericity Index (EE)—defined as the ratio of the surface area of an equivalent-volume sphere (SeS_e) to the actual grain surface area (SaS_a)—and Compactness / Specific Surface Area are formulated as [12](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.840908/full):

E=SeSa=π1/3(6V0)2/3Sa,Specific Surface Area=SaV0E = \frac{S_e}{S_a} = \frac{\pi^{1/3}(6 V_0)^{2/3}}{S_a}, \quad \text{Specific Surface Area} = \frac{S_a}{V_0}

#### Ventral Sulcus (Crease) Depth (DD), Area (SsulcusS_{\text{sulcus}}), and Planar Sections

To quantify the re-entrant ventral crease without destructive sectioning, the algorithm extracts a central transverse cross-sectional slice in the YZYZ-plane (perpendicular to the length axis XX) and a longitudinal slice in the XZXZ-plane [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). Ordering the 2D boundary vertices {q1,…,qm}\{\mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_m\} angularly around the transverse slice centroid qc\mathbf{q}_c, the cross-sectional perimeter CyzC_{yz} is summed along the shared boundary edges, and the concave cross-sectional slice area SyzS_{yz} (SsliceS_{\text{slice}}) is integrated via Heron's theorem across all adjacent vertex-centroid triangles △(qc,qj,qj+1)\triangle(\mathbf{q}_c, \mathbf{q}_j, \mathbf{q}_{j+1}) [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

Computing the 2D convex hull polygon over the same transverse slice vertices yields an outer convex envelope that bridges straight across the top of the ventral groove with area Sconvex_hull,yzS_{\text{convex\_hull}, yz} [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). The Ventral Sulcus Area (SsulcusS_{\text{sulcus}}), Ventral Sulcus Depth (DD), and 2D Roundness Index (cc) are then isolated analytically [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/):

Ssulcus=Sconvex_hull,yz−Syz,D=max⁡qj∈Γgroovedist(qj,qh,1qh,2‾),c=4πSyzCyz2S_{\text{sulcus}} = S_{\text{convex\_hull}, yz} - S_{yz}, \quad D = \max_{\mathbf{q}_j \in \Gamma_{\text{groove}}} \text{dist}\left(\mathbf{q}_j, \overline{\mathbf{q}_{h,1}\mathbf{q}_{h,2}}\right), \quad c = \frac{4\pi S_{yz}}{C_{yz}^2}

where qh,1qh,2‾\overline{\mathbf{q}_{h,1}\mathbf{q}_{h,2}} is the convex hull bridge segment spanning the ventral cleft and Γgroove\Gamma_{\text{groove}} is the set of interior boundary vertices lying along the sulcus floor [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

### Level 3 Functional Coupling: Machine Learning Gravimetric Prediction

To elevate the platform to a Level 3 Functional Twin, the 32-dimensional geometric and ventral sulcus trait vector z∈R32\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{32} is coupled to supervised regression models to predict individual grain weight (WiW_i) and Thousand Grain Weight (TGW\text{TGW}) without physical weighing scales [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30). Five regression architectures—Multivariable Linear Regression, Bayesian Ridge Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) Regression, Random Forest Regression, and Gradient Boosting Regression—are trained across multi-variety seed populations (e.g., 500 wheat grains500\text{ wheat grains} across five varieties) and evaluated via Coefficient of Determination (R2R^2), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE\text{RMSE}), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE\text{MAPE}) [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).

Incorporating the ventral sulcus parameters (D,Ssulcus,D/hD, S_{\text{sulcus}}, D/h) alongside 3D mesh volume V0V_0 dramatically improves single-kernel weight prediction over 2D analyzers because of the underlying cereal morphology [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30). In cereal physiology, two wheat kernels with identical 2D dorsal projections (l,wl, w) and identical outer convex hull volumes can possess vastly different masses if one genotype exhibits a deep, wide ventral crease or post-anthesis heat-stress shriveling along the flanks [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30). By subtracting the non-endosperm crease void SsulcusS_{\text{sulcus}} and capturing true concave surface area SaS_a, the 32-parameter feature vector isolates the true starch-bearing endosperm volume, eliminating the +1.5 g+1.5\text{ g} overestimation bias observed in 2D silhouette systems like MARVIN and the −2.7 g-2.7\text{ g} underestimation bias of uncalibrated 2D neural predictors [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1024/3/3/30).

### Conclusions and Design Recommendations

High-precision 3D grain phenotyping cannot be achieved by post-processing blurred or poorly registered imagery; rather, the mechatronic stage controller must be co-designed directly around the optical pixel-smear inequality vproj≤0.5Rs/t0v_{\text{proj}} \le 0.5 R_s / t_0 [18](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/spatial-resolution-machine-vision). Implementing Sliding Mode Control with Explicit Vibration Model Compensation (SMC-VMC) cancels elastic structural modes directly in the equivalent control law, reducing actuator chattering by 72%72\% and steady-state positioning error to 0.034 mm0.034\text{ mm}, thereby ensuring that residual stage velocity during exposure remains strictly within sub-pixel bounds [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-631x/9/1/19).

Simultaneously, because 2D silhouette analyzers exhibit severe genotype-dependent gravimetric biases (+1.5 g+1.5\text{ g} to −2.7 g-2.7\text{ g} in TGW) and remain blind to internal concavities, high-throughput breeding programs should pair structured-light scanning—operated at an empirically validated 37∘37^\circ scanning angle and 30∘30^\circ rotation step over a matte black stage—with differentiable 3D Gaussian Splatting [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). Utilizing Adaptive Density Control to clone and split Gaussian primitives in concert with Second-Order IBVS active view selection resolves complex seed coat geometries and deep ventral creases with statistically higher accuracy than conventional SfM-MVS (p<0.05p < 0.05) [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0472/15/22/2329). Finally, standardizing point cloud orientation via PCA eigenvector alignment (TA=MT⊤(T0−A)\mathbf{T}_A = \mathbf{M}_T^\top(\mathbf{T}_0 - \mathbf{A})) and isolating the ventral sulcus via transverse convex-hull slice subtraction (Ssulcus=Sconvex,yz−SyzS_{\text{sulcus}} = S_{\text{convex},yz} - S_{yz}) yields a 32-parameter phenotypic vector that serves as an exact, non-destructive replacement for X-ray CT [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/). Coupling this validated geometric vector with Bayesian Ridge or Gradient Boosting regressors elevates the grain phenotyping workstation to a Level 3 Functional Twin capable of driving automated genomic selection, milling yield optimization, and precision seed sorting [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9044079/).
