# Shoujo Manga, Queer Japanese Young Adults, and Gendered Pronouns/Sentence-Final Particles: Literature Review, Gap Analysis, and Study Design

No published empirical study has yet tested whether shoujo manga consumption shapes the first-person pronoun or sentence-final particle (SFP) use of queer Japanese young adults — the hypothesis is genuinely open, but the best corpus evidence also suggests its core premise needs reframing, because shoujo manga is the *less* stereotyped genre linguistically, while hyperfeminine "role language" concentrates in shounen manga.

## TL;DR

- **Direct evidence is essentially absent.** No peer-reviewed study or quantified survey links manga/anime/BL/yuri consumption to pronoun or SFP use among queer, X-gender, trans, or fujoshi Japanese speakers; the existing ethnographies (Abe on lesbian bars, Miyazaki on junior-high girls, Xue on an FtM speaker) explain nonconforming choices through peers, gender ideology, and identity work, and claims of media influence come from scholarly commentary, critics, and celebrity anecdotes.
- **The "distinct shoujo pattern" is real but not what is usually assumed.** Giancarla Unser-Schutz's manga corpus finds female characters in shoujo manga using mostly neutral (42.42%) and mildly masculine (28.11%) SFPs — close to real young women's speech — while female characters in shounen manga use strongly feminine SFPs most (45.64%); pronouns in shoujo are conservative (heroines: atashi 73.98%, watashi 24.83%, zero boku/ore). The salient bokukko/otokoyaku heroine is a character *type*, not the statistical norm of the genre.
- **The study is feasible and valuable if redesigned around directionality and character-level exposure.** Measure exposure to specific gender-nonconforming characters (not just "shoujo dose"), elicit self-reference across multiple registers, collect metapragmatic narratives about *where* forms came from, and use comparison groups and timing (age of first exposure vs. age of pronoun adoption) to separate media influence from selective consumption.

## Key Findings

1. **Baseline society has already moved.** Real young women's speech has been documented as far less "feminine" than ideology prescribes since Okamoto (1995);\[1\] junior-high girls legitimize boku/ore among peers (Miyazaki 2002, 2004, 2016, 2023);\[2\] large-scale Twitter data show wa declining and boku rising (Carpi & Iacus 2020).\[3\] Any queer-specific pattern must be measured against this moving baseline, not against textbook joseigo.
2. **"Women's language" is substantially a media-circulated ideology.** Inoue (2006) and Nakamura (2014) show onna kotoba was historically constructed and is maintained largely through fiction, translation, and media\[4\] — so media matters for *knowledge of* gendered forms even where it does not drive everyday *use*.\[5\]\[6\]
3. **Sociolinguistics is divided on media effects.** The classic view (Trudgill, Chambers) holds that media do not transmit structural change; Stuart-Smith et al. (2013) provide the strongest counter-evidence (engagement with *EastEnders* correlated with Glaswegian TH-fronting/L-vocalization), and Sayers (2014) offers a framework.\[7\]\[8\] Pronouns and SFPs are *consciously selectable, socially indexical* items — exactly the class of features where media modeling is most plausible.
4. **Queer Japanese linguistics documents rich pronoun agency but not media sourcing.** Abe's lesbian-bar data show deliberate, context-sensitive choice among ore, boku, jibun, washi, and watashi;\[9\] Maree shows how onē-kotoba is *produced* by media;\[10\] X-gender research (Dale; Takeuchi) does not yet address language systematically.\[11\]
5. **The gap is real and well-defined.** The user's study would be the first to connect specific media exposure to queer speakers' self-reference — a contribution regardless of whether the effect is found.

## Details

### 1. Gendered grammar in Japanese: the baseline

**The prescriptive system.** Standard descriptions classify first-person pronouns along gender and formality: watakushi (formal, all), watashi (formal for men, plain for women), atashi (female, informal), boku (male, plain), ore (male, informal/rough), washi (older male/dialectal), plus jibun and uchi as newer or dialect-derived options. SFPs are classed on a five-way scale — strongly feminine (rising wa, wa yo, no yo, kashira), moderately feminine, neutral (ne, yo after many predicates), moderately masculine (da yo, da na), strongly masculine (zo, ze).\[12\] This scale derives from Okamoto (1995) and Okamoto & Sato (1992) and underlies most quantitative work, including Unser-Schutz's manga corpus.\[13\] Carpi & Iacus (2020) usefully tabulate how different authors classify each SFP and pronoun, and stress that such classifications are "ideological and prescriptive in nature."\[3\]

**Ideology versus practice.** The central finding of three decades of research is the gap between norm and use:

- **Okamoto (1995), "'Tasteless' Japanese: Less 'Feminine' Speech among Young Japanese Women"** (in Hall & Bucholtz, eds., *Gender Articulated*, Routledge, pp. 297–325) compared students with middle-aged women and found the younger women used far fewer feminine forms and many moderately masculine ones (da yo, plain verb forms); some told her that feminine particles like wa and kashira make speech sound indirect or affected.\[12\]\[14\]\[15\]
- **Okamoto & Shibamoto Smith (eds.), *Japanese Language, Gender, and Ideology: Cultural Models and Real People*** (OUP, 2004) is the essential anthology: it contains Inoue's genealogy of women's language, Washi on wartime language policy, Lunsing & Maree on queer reference, Abe on lesbian bars, Miyazaki on junior-high pronouns, and Sturtz Sreetharan on men's speech.\[14\]\[16\]\[17\] Their later monograph *The Social Life of the Japanese Language* (Cambridge, 2016) argues that "normative meanings are contested and that the indexical fields of gendered speech forms are potentially broader and variable."\[3\]
- **Sturtz Sreetharan** (2004, *Language in Society* 33(1); 2009, *Pragmatics* 19(2), "Ore and omae") shows men's pronoun use is contextually governed and regionally variable (Kansai vs. Kanto),\[18\] and that most empirical pronoun research has studied women — a reminder that "masculine" baselines are also under-described.\[19\]\[20\]
- **Inoue, *Vicarious Language: Gender and Linguistic Modernity in Japan*** (UC Press, 2006) traces how "schoolgirl speech" (teyo dawa) was first heard as vulgar in the Meiji era and was later naturalized as "women's language" through novels and print media — the key historical precedent for media *creating* a gendered register.
- **Nakamura Momoko, *Gender, Language and Ideology: A Genealogy of Japanese Women's Language*** (John Benjamins, 2014) argues that "most Japanese learn women's language as knowledge from the media," especially via translated speech of non-Japanese women\[6\] (her example: Angelina Jolie rendered with wa and yo).\[5\] Her Japanese works — 『女ことばはつくられる』 (2007) and the edited 『ジェンダーで学ぶ言語学』 (2010), with chapters on manga and on young women's "otoko kotoba" — are directly relevant.\[14\]

**Contemporary young-adult usage.**

- **Miyazaki Ayumi**'s longitudinal junior-high ethnography is the single most important precedent. Her 2016 article in 『社会言語科学』 19(1): 135–150 (「日本の中学生のジェンダー一人称を巡るメタ語用的解釈」) identifies three shifts: uchi is preferred to atashi as less feminine; girls' use of ore/boku has become "legitimized" (正当化される); and boys' boku indexes undesirable, weak masculinity.\[21\]\[22\] Her 2023 *International Journal of the Sociology of Language* article ("Masculine pronouns are not only for boys") reports girls framing boku as gender-appropriate for girls and attaching strongly negative meanings to feminine pronouns, with no evidence of wanting a male identity — rather, access to "the social capital of maleness."\[23\] In her 2004 chapter a girl explains she uses ore in fantasies of being a boy.\[24\] Crucially for this study, **the available abstracts attribute these shifts to peer dynamics and gender ideology, not manga** (full texts not verified on this point).
- **Rika Ito** (TRANS 16) reports that in a Sapporo elementary school girls' boku was labeled abnormal, while uchi was popular\[25\] — evidence of strong regional/community variation.
- **Survey data**: Hishikari (2007, 『東京女子大学言語文化研究』 16: 33–49) surveyed Tokyo-area youth pronoun use and found uchi and jibun rising;\[13\] Saegusa (2009, as summarized secondarily) found elementary girls using uchi (49%) to friends.\[26\] A 2022 column by linguist Nakagawa Shuta for the Nihongo Kentei reports current university students describing atashi as too "甘えた" and uchi as a childhood fad being abandoned — suggesting women still lack a stable informal pronoun, a structural gap that makes boku attractive.\[27\]
- **Twitter corpus (Carpi & Iacus 2020, arXiv 2006.15935)**: of 408 million Japanese tweets (2015–2019), 26,737,077 — "about 6% of the total," posted by 1,469,232 unique accounts — ended in one of 29 target SFPs or contained one of 8 target pronouns. The authors report that wa "was most observed in 2015 (100%) with an evident decrease in use, as low as 40%, in 2020," while boku and watakushi "display an increasing pattern"; kashira was *not* used more by women than men, and hiragana ぼく appeared in tweets by female mangaka and actresses. This is the best large-scale evidence of "neutralization," but gender was coded by human raters who went "through the full Twitter timeline" of 2,560 selected accounts (1,779 male, 781 female), with timelines retrievable for 2,355 accounts (2,560,596 tweets) — a binary scheme in which nonbinary users are invisible.

**Implication:** Because majority young women already use few strongly feminine forms and some use boku, the user cannot assume a stable "majority pattern" against which queer speakers deviate. The comparison must be empirical and cohort-matched.

### 2. Role language (yakuwarigo) and the actual linguistic profile of shoujo manga

**Kinsui's framework.** Kinsui Satoshi's 『ヴァーチャル日本語 役割語の謎』 (Iwanami, 2003) defines yakuwarigo as speech styles that evoke a character type (the hakase's ~ja, the ojōsama's ~desu wa).\[28\] His edited 『役割語研究の地平』 (Kurosio, 2007) and 『〈役割語〉小辞典』 (Kenkyūsha, 2014) extend it;\[29\] his chapter 「近代日本マンガの言語」 (2007) treats manga specifically. Kinsui argues role language is indispensable to Japanese fiction but warns that its formulaic use masks real linguistic diversity and can transmit discriminatory stereotypes.\[29\] Nishida (2010, 2012) analyzes the bokukko/ボク少女 as an "attribute expression" (属性表現) rather than full role language\[28\] — a useful distinction for this study, since a pronoun alone can signal a character attribute without importing a whole register.

**Corpus evidence — the most important correction to the user's premise.** Giancarla Unser-Schutz built the only large quantitative manga corpus for gendered speech:

- **Unser-Schutz (2011, JALT2010 Proceedings, "Manga as a linguistic resource for learning")**: eight series (four shoujo: *Kimi ni Todoke*, *Nana*, *Nodame Cantabile*, *Love☆Com*; four shounen: *Death Note*, *Detective Conan*, *Naruto*, *One Piece*), first three volumes each, 579,261 characters in 45,073 entries.\[13\]
  - *Pronouns*: female characters in shoujo manga used atashi 73.98% and watashi 24.83% of 584 tokens, with zero boku or ore; male characters in shoujo used ore 79.04%. Uchi and jibun as pronouns were nearly absent, making manga "somewhat conservative."\[13\]
  - *SFPs*: female characters in shoujo used neutral forms (42.42%) and mildly masculine forms (28.11%) most, "similar to reports of young girls' speech," with strongly feminine forms only 9.18%. Female characters in shounen manga used strongly feminine forms most (45.64%) — "very different from real life."\[13\]
  - First-person pronouns appeared in only 9.77% of lines, accurately reflecting subject drop.\[13\]
- **Unser-Schutz (2015), "Influential or influenced? The relationship between genre, gender and language in manga," *Gender and Language* 9(2): 223–254**: with a ten-series corpus she notes that "manga are often given as one influential factor" for Japanese becoming less gendered, "particularly regarding girls' use of masculine first person pronouns; however, previous research has been contradictory."\[30\] She finds pronoun use generally normative while all genders use varied gendered SFPs, and argues manga language *reacts to* rather than drives social change.\[30\]\[31\]\[32\]
- **Unser-Schutz (2015), "What text can tell us about male and female characters in shōjo- and shōnen-manga," *East Asian Journal of Popular Culture* 1(1): 133–153**: in her own words, "with regards to approximately 80% of all text... shōnen-manga are extremely skewed towards male characters," and "only two female characters in all of the shōnen-manga series account for more than 10% of text, whereas all the shōjo-manga have male characters accounting for over 12%." She predicts stereotypical speech is concentrated where female characters are marginal (shounen).
- **Unser-Schutz (2019), "Redefining Shōjo and Shōnen Manga Through Language Patterns," in *Shōjo Across Media*** (Palgrave, pp. 49–82) extends this.\[33\]

**Interpretation.** The "distinct pattern" in shoujo manga is therefore best described as (a) *mainstream* heroines speaking realistically neutral, casual Japanese with conventional feminine pronouns, plus (b) a *salient minority* of gender-crossing characters. The hyperfeminine wa yo/kashira heroine is more a feature of shounen manga, older shoujo, ojōsama characters, and translated fiction (Nakamura) than of contemporary bestselling shoujo. This matters: if queer readers adopt nonconforming forms from manga, the mechanism is probably **identification with specific characters**, not genre-wide linguistic exposure.

**Other content analyses.**

- **Ueno Junko (2006), "Shojo and Adult Women: A Linguistic Analysis of Gender Identity in Manga," *Women and Language* 29(1): 16–25**: compares shoujo and ladies' manga, finding relatively realistic depictions; frames print media as historically influential on women's speech.\[34\]\[35\]
- **Hiramoto Mie**: "Anime and intertextualities: Hegemonic identities in *Cowboy Bebop*" (*Pragmatics and Society* 1(2): 234–256, 2010) and "Hey, you're a girl?" (*Multilingua* 32(1): 51–78, 2013) show how pronouns and SFPs construct heteronormative "hero" and "babe" archetypes;\[36\]\[37\]\[38\] her "Utterance final position and projection of femininity in Japanese" (*Gender and Language* 4(1), 2010) addresses SFP femininity.
- A 2023 *East Asian Journal of Popular Culture* study of idol-song lyrics uses logistic regression on pronoun–SFP combinations to show female idol groups performing male-coded personae\[39\] — a methodological model for coding cross-gender personae.

**Gender-crossing characters (descriptive; few linguistic studies).** The canonical figures — Sapphire in Tezuka's *Princess Knight* (boku), Oscar in *The Rose of Versailles* (Takarazuka otokoyaku lineage), Haruka Tenō/Sailor Uranus, Utena Tenjō (boku) and Anthy in *Revolutionary Girl Utena*, Haruhi in *Ouran High School Host Club* — are widely discussed in manga studies,\[40\]\[41\] but **I could not verify a published corpus study of their pronoun/SFP distributions**. The 1999 Takarajimasha *moe* encyclopedia already treats the ボク女 as a recognized type, and fan reference works distinguish bokukko (varied personalities) from orekko (consistently tomboyish).\[42\]\[43\] A student thesis (Damalas) on SFP wa in *Sailor Moon* exists but is unpublished.\[34\] This is itself a gap the user could fill by coding these characters.

**BL and yuri.** No linguistic corpus study of BL or yuri pronoun/SFP use was found. BL is linguistically relevant because seme/uke roles are often indexed by ore vs. boku and by SFP choice, offering female (and queer) readers sustained exposure to *male* registers voiced in intimate contexts. Yuri is relevant because it frequently features boku- or ore-using women. Both are under-studied linguistically; the user should treat them as separate exposure variables.

### 3. Queer Japanese linguistics

- **Hideko Abe, *Queer Japanese: Gender and Sexual Identities through Linguistic Practices*** (Palgrave Macmillan, 2010). Chapters cover queer advice columns, lesbian bar talk, cross-dressing speech, theater, and "queen's speech."\[44\]\[45\] The lesbian-bar chapter (earlier version: "Lesbian Bar Talk in Shinjuku, Tokyo," in Okamoto & Shibamoto Smith 2004) documents patrons using ore, boku, jibun, and washi alongside watashi/atashi, with one speaker shifting from jibun in formal settings to ore in emotional ones, and a woman in her mid-30s using boku in workplace arguments with a male boss because atashi "didn't make her strong."\[9\]\[14\]\[46\] Abe concludes that pronouns are carefully and consciously chosen because they are the most obvious linguistic markers of gender.\[9\] Abe's data are from around the turn of the century and, per the sources available, do not foreground manga as a source of these forms.
- **Wim Lunsing & Claire Maree, "Shifting Speakers: Negotiating Reference in Relation to Sexuality and Gender"** (in Okamoto & Shibamoto Smith 2004, pp. 92–109)\[47\] shows strategic shifting and avoidance of self-reference, and that onē-kotoba combines feminine forms (atashi, da wa) with crude "masculine" content; it also notes backlash against onē speech from masculinity-oriented gay men.\[48\]\[49\]
- **Claire Maree**: 『「おネエことば」論』 (Seidosha, 2013) traces onē-kotoba's history within the queer community and its absorption into popular culture;\[50\] ***queerqueen: Linguistic Excess in Japanese Media*** (OUP, 2020) introduces "language labour" — the editing, subtitling (telop), and orthographic practices by which media *produce* a recognizable queer speech style that then appears authentic.\[51\]\[52\] Maree is the clearest model for theorizing the media-to-speaker direction, though her focus is on representation of queer men and trans women, not on audience uptake. Maree (2007) also documents pronoun avoidance, nicknames, and kinship terms as early self-reference strategies.\[53\]
- **X-gender and trans speakers.** S.P.F. Dale's "An Introduction to X-Jendā" (*Intersections* 31) and "X-Jendā" (*TSQ* 1(1–2), 2014) establish X-gender as a Japan-specific nonbinary category and explicitly note that "language and linguistic frameworks also have a considerable role" — but leave language unexplored.\[11\]\[54\] Takeuchi Kyoko's sociology (10 semi-structured interviews, 『社会学評論』 72(4); 14 interviews, Kanto Sociological Society 2020) traces how X-gender spread via self-help groups and online communities (HP/mixi), not language.\[55\]\[56\]\[57\] Kōtō Ward (Tokyo)'s 「性的マイノリティ当事者等に対する意識実態調査 報告書」 (March 2020) found 2.5% of respondents identifying as X-gender — defined as wishing to live as 「中性」「両性」「無性」「不定性」 — versus 1.8% transgender and 93.9% cisgender, a non-trivial population. Xue Xiaofan's 2022 Ochanomizu bulletin case study (DOI 10.24567/0002000526) analyzes one FtM speaker's "confusion in the Japanese linguistic expressions regarding sexuality and gender,"\[58\] and Xue & Aono's conference paper (Japanese Psychological Association proceedings vol. 82, five interviewees: one X-gender, two FTM, one bisexual, one gay) found a co-occurrence chain linking 性自認–女子–名前–恥ずかしい–オレ–ボク;\[59\] neither abstract mentions media.
- **Not verified in this review:** specific publications by James Valentine and Laura Miller on queer or girls' language were not retrieved here (Miller's work on kogal/gyaru language is well known and relevant to youth stylization, but should be checked directly); no Japanese survey of trans people's pronoun shifts during transition was found.

**Key finding for Section 3:** Queer Japanese speakers are documented as *highly agentive* pronoun users, and the field has a strong theory of media producing queer speech styles (Maree), but **no study documents queer speakers citing manga/anime characters as their models.**

### 4. Mass media influence on language

**The skeptical position.** Variationist orthodoxy holds that structural change spreads through face-to-face interaction, and that media at most transmit lexis. Trudgill's 2014 reply in the *Journal of Sociolinguistics* is titled "Diffusion, drift, and the irrelevance of media influence";\[8\] Chambers has argued similarly.

**The counter-evidence.** Stuart-Smith, Pryce, Timmins & Gunter (2013), "Television can also be a factor in language change: Evidence from an urban dialect," *Language* 89(3): 501–536, found that Glaswegian adolescents' TH-fronting and L-vocalization showed "positive correlations with strong psychological engagement with the London-based TV soap drama, EastEnders" (not mere viewing hours), alongside street-style affiliation; the abstract frames engagement with a favorite drama only as "an additional accelerating factor," and Stuart-Smith stated in the 2013 Glasgow press release that "the impact of the media is weaker than that of actual social interaction." Sayers (2014), "The mediated innovation model," *Journal of Sociolinguistics* 18(2): 185–212, provides a framework requiring both mediation and evidence of uptake; responses by Androutsopoulos, Tagliamonte, Stuart-Smith, and Trudgill appear in the same issue.\[60\]\[61\]\[62\] Androutsopoulos's "mediatization" approach shifts the question from "influence" to how speakers *appropriate* media resources — the most suitable frame for pronoun choice.

**Why Japanese pronouns/SFPs are a favorable test case.** Stuart-Smith's effect concerned below-awareness phonetics.\[63\] Pronouns and SFPs are the opposite: highly conscious, socially indexical, and selectable at will (Abe; Miyazaki). Theory therefore predicts *stronger* plausible media modeling for these items than for phonology — but also stronger confounding by identity-driven selection.

**Japanese-specific evidence.**

- Nakamura (2014) and Inoue (2006): media and translation sustain *knowledge of* women's language.\[5\]\[64\] Nakamura has also commented publicly that children borrow self-reference terms from anime and social media to construct "jibunrashisa"\[65\] — expert commentary, not data.
- Unser-Schutz (2020), "Nerdy girls talking gross," *Gender and Language* 14(3): 326–346,\[66\] analyzed Japanese BBS posts and found manga language assigned "a vaguely negative influence on communication skills," with beliefs about manga language paralleling stereotypes of otaku speech — i.e., Japanese folk ideology strongly *believes* manga shapes speech, especially of female otaku.\[67\]\[68\] This is a documented *language ideology*, not evidence of an effect, and it is a confound: participants may over- or under-report media influence to align with or resist this stigma.
- A Japanese undergraduate award-winning thesis (J-STAGE, 『都市経済』 15) concludes that ore "probably became general through the influence of manga," based on a reference work's claim that manga heroes popularized ore from the late 1960s\[69\] — an inference, not respondent data.
- **Anecdotal**: Talent Haruna Fuka reports adopting ぼく after encountering *Revolutionary Girl Utena* because women lacked an informal first-person pronoun that was neither stiff nor coquettish (Japanese Wikipedia, ボク少女, secondary).\[40\] Critic Sayawaka (2026) claims some fujoshi "began using 僕 in daily life too," attributing today's girls' boku to social media rather than anime.\[70\] These are hypotheses worth testing, not findings.
- **L2 learners as an analogue**: Unser-Schutz (2011) notes 70–80% of Japanese learners reportedly cite manga/anime as motivation (Kumano 2010)\[13\] and documents teacher concern about learners importing stereotyped forms. A targeted L2 study of anime-derived ore/boku use was not retrieved in this review and should be searched separately.

**Reverse direction.** The dominant scholarly reading (Unser-Schutz 2015; Kinsui) is that manga *crystallize* existing stereotypes and track social change rather than cause it; one citing study summarizes that "language use in Japanese popular media reacted to, rather than directly affected, the societal construction of masculinities."\[30\] The user's study should be designed to adjudicate between "media as source" and "media as mirror."

### 5. Direct evidence on the user's hypothesis — candid assessment

| Population | Direct empirical evidence linking manga/anime to pronoun/SFP use | Closest adjacent evidence | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Queer Japanese young adults | None found | Abe (2004/2010) lesbian-bar pronoun agency; Lunsing & Maree (2004) | Ethnographic, no media variable |
| Japanese young women generally | None quantified | Okamoto (1995); Miyazaki (2002–2023); Carpi & Iacus (2020); Unser-Schutz corpus | Empirical on use; media role asserted, not tested |
| Fujoshi/fandom communities | None found | Unser-Schutz (2020) on folk ideology of otaku/manga speech; critic commentary | Ideology analysis; journalism |
| X-gender / trans | None found | Xue (2022) FtM case; Xue & Aono (5 interviews); Dale; Takeuchi | Qualitative, no media attribution in abstracts |

**Bottom line:** the hypothesis rests on (a) solid evidence that nonconforming pronoun use exists and is agentive, (b) solid evidence that manga encode gendered speech in genre-specific ways, (c) a theoretical literature that permits media modeling of conscious indexical forms, and (d) only anecdote for the causal link.

### 6. Gap analysis and methodological considerations

**What is untested.**
1. Whether exposure to specific gender-crossing characters predicts adoption of their pronouns/SFPs.
2. Whether queer young adults differ from cohort-matched cisgender heterosexual peers in pronoun/SFP repertoire at all, once the already-neutralized majority baseline is measured.
3. Whether sexual orientation and gender identity have separable effects (Abe's lesbian speakers vs. X-gender/trans speakers likely have different motives).
4. Whether BL/yuri exposure functions differently from mainstream shoujo.
5. Whether online text registers (X/Twitter, Discord, LINE) diverge from spoken use.

**Reframe the hypothesis.** Replace "shoujo consumption → nonconforming forms" with competing, testable models:
- *Modeling*: exposure to particular characters precedes and predicts adoption.
- *Licensing/legitimation*: media do not supply forms (already available via peers) but legitimize them, reducing perceived stigma — consistent with Miyazaki's "legitimized" boku/ore.
- *Selective exposure*: people already inclined toward nonconforming forms seek out media featuring them.
- *Mirror*: no causal link; both reflect broader social change.

**Sampling ethically.**
- Recruit through multiple channels to avoid single-community bias: university LGBTQ circles, NPOs (e.g., community centers in Tokyo, Osaka, Fukuoka), queer events, X/Discord fandom spaces, and snowball referral seeded from several unconnected starting points.
- Japan's low disclosure rates make anonymity paramount: avoid collecting real names, allow pseudonymous online participation, store recordings separately from identity data, obtain IRB approval with explicit outing-risk mitigation, and avoid recording in identifiable venues without consent from all present.
- Ask gender identity (including X-gender categories 中性/両性/無性/不定性, following Takeuchi and municipal survey practice) and sexual orientation as separate items.\[71\]
- Target 18–29, stratify by region (Kanto vs. Kansai at minimum — Kansai uchi and SFPs differ, and Unser-Schutz excluded a Kansai-dialect series for this reason).\[13\]

**Measuring consumption.**
- Do not rely on genre labels alone. Use a title-and-character checklist (e.g., *Princess Knight*, *Rose of Versailles*, *Sailor Moon*, *Utena*, *Ouran*, contemporary bokukko/orekko characters, major BL/yuri titles), recording age at first exposure, intensity of engagement (rereading, fan creation, cosplay, role-play), and favorite characters.
- Separate shoujo, josei, shounen, BL, yuri, anime, light novels, games, VTubers, and idol media — co-consumption is the norm.
- Follow Stuart-Smith in measuring *engagement* (identification, liking, imitation), not just hours.

**Eliciting pronoun and SFP use.**
- First-person pronouns appear in under 10% of lines even in manga dialogue (Unser-Schutz 2011),\[13\] and are highly situational; self-report alone is unreliable and naturalistic recording yields few tokens.
- Combine: (1) self-report matrices by interlocutor and setting (friend, queer community, family, workplace, online), modeled on Kim's 本人の意識 vs. 社会的期待 survey design;\[72\] (2) recorded peer conversations in queer and non-queer settings (Abe's and Miyazaki's ethnographic approaches); (3) elicited narratives designed to raise self-reference (e.g., "tell me about a time you stood up for yourself"); (4) text corpora from consenting participants' social media, analyzed with the Carpi & Iacus token set; (5) perception tasks (matched-guise or rating of character lines) to measure indexical associations.
- Code SFPs on the Okamoto five-way scale for comparability with Unser-Schutz's corpus, but treat categories as ideological, not inherent.
- Collect metapragmatic commentary (Miyazaki 2016's method): ask participants when they began using each pronoun, why, and from whom or what — the only way to obtain the missing attribution data.

**Confounds.** Peer groups and communities of practice; online community norms; region/dialect; age cohort; gender identity vs. orientation; non-manga media (idols, Takarazuka, VTubers, social media influencers); the otaku stigma documented by Unser-Schutz (2020), which can bias self-reports; and the ongoing majority-wide neutralization.

**Comparison groups.**
- Cisgender heterosexual young adults with matched manga consumption (isolates queer identity).
- Queer young adults with low manga consumption (isolates media).
- Optionally, heavy shounen/BL consumers vs. heavy shoujo consumers.

**Analytic frameworks.** Ochs's indirect indexicality (pronouns index stances such as toughness or casualness, which in turn index gender); Silverstein's indexical order and Eckert's indexical field; Bucholtz & Hall's tactics of intersubjectivity (adequation/distinction, authentication/denaturalization) for explaining why a lesbian or X-gender speaker might adopt boku; Eckert & McConnell-Ginet's communities of practice; Nakamura's constructive-ideological approach; Agha's enregisterment and Maree's language labour for the media side.

**Establishing directionality.**
- Timeline elicitation: date of first exposure to a character vs. date of pronoun adoption (life-history calendars improve recall).
- Look for *form-specific* matches: if participants adopt a character's distinctive orthography (ボク vs. 僕), SFP combination, or idiosyncratic catchphrase, that is stronger evidence of modeling than generic boku use.
- Test selective exposure directly: ask whether participants sought out the media *because* of characters' gender nonconformity.
- A small experimental component (brief exposure to a bokukko vs. control narrative, then a writing task) can test short-term priming, though it cannot establish long-term adoption.
- Longitudinal follow-up of a subsample over 12–24 months, if resources allow.

## Recommendations

1. **Reframe the hypothesis** from genre-level exposure to character-level identification, and pre-register the four competing models (modeling, licensing, selective exposure, mirror).
2. **Run a pilot of 10–15 semi-structured interviews** with queer young adults (mixed orientation and gender identity) focused on pronoun life histories and media attributions before designing the survey; this directly fills the evidence gap the subagent and this review confirm.
3. **Build a small character corpus** coding pronouns/SFPs of 10–20 canonical gender-crossing shoujo, BL, and yuri characters using Unser-Schutz's scheme — publishable on its own and necessary to test form-specific matching.
4. **Use mixed elicitation** (self-report matrix + recorded peer talk + elicited narratives + consenting social-media text) and code with the Okamoto five-way SFP scale.
5. **Include a cisgender heterosexual comparison group matched on manga consumption**, and a low-consumption queer group.
6. **Treat online text as a distinct register** rather than a proxy for speech.
7. **Write up null results** as findings: a demonstration that queer speakers' forms derive from peer communities rather than manga would itself correct a widespread folk belief (Unser-Schutz 2020).

## Caveats

- Several requested sources were not directly verified in this review: Teshigawara & Kinsui on role language and gender, Kinsui's specific writing on bokukko, Reynolds Katsue Akiba's and Ide Sachiko's individual studies, Ozaki Yoshimitsu's work, and Valentine's and Miller's relevant publications. They are standard references but should be checked before citation.
- Miyazaki's and Xue's full texts were not accessible; statements that they do not attribute pronoun choice to media rest on abstracts and snippets and could change on full reading.
- Nishida (2010, 2012) on ボク少女 is known only through a secondary citation; Saegusa (2009) figures come from a secondary summary.
- Unser-Schutz's corpus covers bestselling series from roughly the 2000s;\[13\] older shoujo (1970s–90s), which current young adults may have read in childhood, may show different patterns. Her pronoun counts for female characters in shoujo are based on 584 tokens across four series — adequate for genre contrasts, not for character-level claims.
- Carpi & Iacus's gender coding is binary and rater-inferred, and their data come from the Twitter search API, which, as the authors note, "only provides a 10% sample of all tweets but Twitter does not disclose any information about the representativeness of this sample."
- Anecdotal evidence (Haruna Fuka, critic commentary, Wikipedia) is cited only as hypothesis-generating; none should be presented as established.
