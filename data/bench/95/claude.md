# The Diamond Sutra (Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra): Study Notes on the Text, Its History, Its Philosophy, and Its Use in Daily Life

The Diamond Sutra teaches one thing in many ways: act fully — give, work, love, teach, lead — while letting go of fixed ideas of “self,” “other,” “achievement,” and “reward.” Its key instruction, to give rise to a mind that abides nowhere, applies directly to careers, marriages, parenting, and emotional life. It must be read with its own safeguard, though: the sutra explicitly rejects nihilism. It asks for engaged, compassionate action without clinging, not for detachment, indifference, or emotional withdrawal.

## TL;DR

- **What it is:**  A short Mahāyāna “perfection of wisdom” dialogue between the Buddha and the elder Subhūti. It was probably composed in India in the early centuries CE (scholars argue for dates between the 2nd and 5th centuries). It was translated into Chinese by Kumārajīva around 401–402, and an 868 printed copy from Dunhuang is the world’s oldest dated printed book. It became a central text of Chan/Zen through the legend of Huineng.
- **What it teaches:**  Its signature pattern runs “X is not-X; therefore it is called X.” This is best read as a cure for reifying things (treating labels as solid essences), not as a mystical rejection of logic. It applies the pattern to the self, beings, merit, the Buddha, and even the teaching itself. Its practical core is generosity and compassion carried out without the four notions of self, person, being, and life-span, and without “abiding” in outcomes.
- **How to use it:**  In work, business, relationships, parenting, and emotional life, the sutra works as a set of diagnostic questions: “What am I abiding in right now? Which notion of self or other am I defending?” It works best alongside ethics and, where needed, professional mental-health care. It works worst as a slogan for not caring (“spiritual bypassing”).

# PART 1 — ORIENTATION AND HISTORICAL CONTEXT

## 1.1 What the Diamond Sutra Is

The Diamond Sutra belongs to the Prajñāpāramitā (“perfection of wisdom”) genre of Mahāyāna sutras. It is one of the most influential Mahāyāna texts in East Asia and is especially prominent in Chan/Zen, alongside the Heart Sutra. [epfl](https://graphsearch.epfl.ch/en/concept/329803) Its form is a dialogue between the Buddha and Subhūti, a senior monk. [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/) Its major themes are:

- not-self (anātman);
- the emptiness of all phenomena, even though the word *śūnyatā*  itself never appears in the text; [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/)
- the liberation of all beings without attachment; [bookey](https://www.bookey.app/book/the-diamond-that-cuts-through-illusion)
- the value of spreading and teaching the sutra itself. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra)[bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/)

Hsing Yun, founder of Fo Guang Shan, summarized the sutra’s practical core as four points:

1. giving without attachment to self;
2. liberating beings without notions of self and other;
3. living without attachment;
4. cultivating without attainment. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra)

This four-part summary is a useful scaffold for everything in Part 4.

**Length.**  A typical English translation runs to about 6,000 words, short enough to read in under half an hour. [learnreligions](https://www.learnreligions.com/the-diamond-sutra-450100) Its Sanskrit name *Triśatikā*  (“Three Hundred [Lines]”) reflects this brevity. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra)[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra)

## 1.2 The Title

| Element | Meaning | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vajra** | “Diamond” or “thunderbolt”: Indra’s indestructible weapon, and later the emblem of Vajrayāna | Gregory Schopen glosses the full title roughly as “The Perfection of Wisdom Text that Cuts Like a Thunderbolt.” [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra)[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra) |
| **Chedikā** | “Cutter,” “that which cuts” | Tibetan *rdo rje gcod pa*  = “vajra cutter.” [abuddhistlibrary](https://www.abuddhistlibrary.com/Buddhism/F-%20Miscellaneous/Miscellaneous%20Buddhism/The%20Diamond-Cutter%20Sutra%20with%20Commentary/The%20Diamond-Cutter%20Sutra%20with%20Commentary.htm) |
| **Prajñā** | Wisdom or insight: seeing how things actually are | Chinese *bore*  般若 transliterates the word rather than translating it. |
| **Pāramitā** | “Perfection,” or literally “gone to the far shore,” transcendence | Sam van Schaik of the British Library’s International Dunhuang Programme notes that *pāramitā*  “actually signifies transcendence, rather than perfection.” [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/) |
| **Sūtra** | A discourse attributed to the Buddha | Opens with “Thus have I heard” (*evaṃ mayā śrutam* ). [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/) |

**Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese titles:**

- Chinese: *Jīngāng Bōrě Bōluómì Jīng*  金剛般若波羅蜜經, usually shortened to *Jīngāng Jīng*  金剛經.
- Japanese: *Kongō-kyō* .
- Korean: *Geumgang-gyeong* .
- Vietnamese: *Kim cương kinh* .

**Why “diamond”?**  Two images are combined:

- The British Library’s explanation: the vajra is “the hardest and strongest of all substances, which can cut through any other”. Emptiness likewise cuts through all substantialist concepts. [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/)
- A Chinese commentator quoted by William Gemmell (1912) says that as the diamond surpasses all gems “in brilliance and indestructibility,” so this wisdom surpasses other knowledge. [gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/64623/64623-h/64623-h.htm)

**Study note.**  Keep both senses in mind. The text is something that cuts (it severs fixed views), and it is something that does not break (its insight survives being questioned).

## 1.3 Place in the Prajñāpāramitā Literature

| Text | Approximate length | Relationship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*  (8,000 lines) | Long | Usually considered the oldest surviving Prajñāpāramitā sutra (Conze dates it between the 1st century BCE and 1st century CE). [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/) |
| *Pañcaviṃśati-*  (25,000) and *Śatasāhasrikā*  (100,000 lines) | Very long | Expansions of the 8,000. They mostly increase repetition. [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/) |
| *Vajracchedikā*  (Diamond, ~300 lines) | Short | Traditionally placed among the later “condensed” texts, but possibly very early (see below). |
| *Hṛdaya*  (Heart Sutra) | Very short | A distillation that does name emptiness explicitly (“form is emptiness”). Its origin is debated (see Part 3). |

The 84000 translation project’s survey of the Kangyur says the most likely overall model is this:

- The *Eight Thousand*  led to longer versions by expansion, “probably during the first three centuries ᴄᴇ.” [84000](https://84000.co/kb-articles/perfection-of-wisdom-kangyur-section)
- Shorter versions such as the Heart and Diamond then appeared by contraction “from the fourth to the sixth century.” [84000](https://84000.co/kb-articles/perfection-of-wisdom-kangyur-section)
- But 84000 adds that “there are reasons for suspecting that the *Vajracchedikā*  may date to as early as the second century ᴄᴇ.” [84000](https://84000.co/kb-articles/perfection-of-wisdom-kangyur-section)

## 1.4 Dating: The Scholarly Debate

- **Conze’s view.**  Edward Conze placed the Diamond Sutra, together with the Heart Sutra, in roughly 300–500 CE, after the expansion of the long sutras. [blogspot](http://jayarava.blogspot.com/2013/11/the-use-of-negation-in-vajracchedika.html)[mnzencenter](https://www.mnzencenter.org/uploads/2/9/5/8/29581455/diamond.pdf)
- **The early-dating view.**  Some scholars now place the Diamond in the earliest layer. Gregory Schopen (1975) cites a source arguing that the latest possible date of the Diamond Sutra is “200 AD or probably 150 AD,” and suggests it may even predate the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* . [blogspot](http://jayarava.blogspot.com/2013/11/the-use-of-negation-in-vajracchedika.html) The usual reasoning compares content and themes, not translation dates. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prajnaparamita)
- **Jan Nattier’s observation.**  The Diamond uses ontological verbs (“is”/”is not,” *asti* /*nāsti* ). The *Aṣṭa*  prefers experiential terms like “is not found.” Nattier takes this as a sign that the Diamond came from “a very different milieu.” [blogspot](http://jayarava.blogspot.com/2013/11/the-use-of-negation-in-vajracchedika.html)
- **The commentary argument.**  Asaṅga and Vasubandhu (4th–5th centuries) had already written authoritative commentaries on it before the first Chinese translation. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra) Schopen takes this to mean the text “had therefore been in circulation for some time.” [encyclopediaofbuddhism](https://encyclopediaofbuddhism.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra)
- **Bottom line.**  Composition in India between the 2nd and 4th centuries CE is the defensible range. Treat any confident single date with suspicion.

## 1.5 Schopen, Harrison, and the “Cult of the Book”

**Schopen’s 1975 article.**  In “The Phrase ‘sa pṛthivīpradeśaś caityabhūto bhavet’ in the *Vajracchedikā* : Notes on the Cult of the Book in Mahāyāna” (*Indo-Iranian Journal*  17, 1975: 147–181; reprinted in *Figments and Fragments of Mahāyāna Buddhism in India* , 2005), Schopen focused on a phrase in sections 12 and 15. [panditproject](https://www.panditproject.org/entity/53328/print)[google](https://books.google.com/books/about/Figments_and_Fragments_of_Mahayana_Buddh.html?hl=fr&id=9SBD45jetbEC) The phrase says that any spot of earth where the sutra is recited or kept “becomes a shrine” (*caitya* ). He argued that early Mahāyāna groups built a “cult of the book,” treating the written or recited text as a sacred presence comparable to relic-shrines (stūpas).

**The challenge.**  David Drewes (“Revisiting the phrase…,” *Indo-Iranian Journal*  50, 2007: 101–143) disputed the idea of a competition between a Mahāyāna book cult and mainstream stūpa worship. [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227033327_Revisiting_the_phrase_sa_prthivipradesas_caityabhuto_bhavet_and_the_Mahayana_Cult_of_the_Book) The debate is live; treat the “cult of the book” as a strong hypothesis, not settled fact.

**Harrison’s contribution.**  Paul Harrison (Stanford) edited the Schøyen manuscript with Shōgo Watanabe and published a translation (2006) based on two old manuscripts from Greater Gandhāra. [blogspot](http://jayarava.blogspot.com/2013/11/the-use-of-negation-in-vajracchedika.html)[stanford](https://religiousstudies.stanford.edu/people/paul-harrison) He also showed that Kumārajīva’s Chinese “often simplifies and augments the original Sanskrit text” (van Schaik’s summary). [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/) His most important interpretive claim, on the negation formula, is treated in Part 3.

**Why this matters for practice.**  The sutra repeatedly promises vast merit for copying, reciting, and teaching it. Modern readers often skip these passages as embarrassing. Historically, though, they explain why the text was copied by the hundreds and why Wang Jie paid for it to be printed (below).

## 1.6 Textual History

### Sanskrit manuscripts

- **Earliest fragments.**  Sanskrit fragments dating to the late 5th or early 6th century were found by Aurel Stein at Dandan Uiliq near Khotan. [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/)
- **Gilgit manuscript.**  Found in northern Pakistan in 1931. [squarespace](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c03ced75ffd204418037b7a/t/5c5306ce575d1f9230da8a6a/1548945103490/Diamond+Sutra-Paul+Harrison+tr.pdf) Edited and translated by Gregory Schopen, “The Manuscript of the *Vajracchedikā*  Found at Gilgit,” in Gómez and Silk (eds.), *Studies in the Literature of the Great Vehicle*  (1989). It dates to roughly the 6th–7th century and preserves the latter part of the text.
- **Schøyen manuscript (MS 2385).**  A birch-bark manuscript in an ornate Gupta script, probably from the Bamiyan region of Afghanistan, bound together with the Medicine Buddha Sutra. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra) It preserves roughly the first half, §§1–16c. [squarespace](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c03ced75ffd204418037b7a/t/5c5306ce575d1f9230da8a6a/1548945103490/Diamond+Sutra-Paul+Harrison+tr.pdf) Edited by Harrison and Watanabe in *Buddhist Manuscripts in the Schøyen Collection* , vol. III (2006).
- **Harrison’s composite.**  Harrison combined the two manuscripts to represent the text as it circulated in Greater Gandhāra in the 6th–7th centuries. [blogspot](http://jayarava.blogspot.com/2013/11/the-use-of-negation-in-vajracchedika.html) Both manuscripts are shorter than the 19th-century editions and closer to Kumārajīva, which suggests the text kept growing after it was first composed.
- **Earlier editions.**  Max Müller published the first Western Sanskrit edition in 1881, based on manuscripts from Japan, China, and Tibet. Conze’s 1957 edition was criticized by Schopen as too eclectic in its sources.

Counting conventions differ. Some lists add a second Bodhiruci (693) or count Xuanzang twice. Traditional Chinese Buddhism speaks of “six translations.”

**A later addition to Kumārajīva’s text.**  The received text of section 21 contains a short passage of about 62 characters. It is traditionally said to have been inserted later by a monk named Lingyou (靈幽) from another translation and is recorded on steles of 822. The 1084 printed edition of the commentary attributed to Huineng (see 1.9) notes that these characters carry no comment because “the Sixth Patriarch’s commentary came first.” This is the tradition’s own explanation, not independent proof of the commentary’s date.

### Tibetan and other versions

- **Canonical Tibetan.**  Translated in the late 8th or early 9th century by the team of Śīlendrabodhi (from India) and Yeshé Dé (from Tibet). It is catalogued in the Kangyur as Toh 16, and 84000 publishes an English translation. Because Tibetan translators developed a technical vocabulary for this work, the Tibetan is more literal than any of the Chinese versions.
- **Other Tibetan.**  A second Tibetan version, translated from Chinese, survives only at Dunhuang.
- **Other languages.**  The sutra also exists in Sogdian, Khotanese, Mongolian, Old Uyghur, and Tangut. [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/)

### The 868 Dunhuang printed scroll

- **What it is.**  British Library Or.8210/P.2, a woodblock-printed scroll about five metres long, with an illustrated frontispiece showing the Buddha teaching Subhūti.
- **How it reached London.**  It was found in the sealed “Library Cave” (Cave 17) at the Mogao Caves near Dunhuang, which a Daoist monk, Wang Yuanlu, opened in 1900. Aurel Stein bought it in 1907. It was probably printed in Sichuan.
- **The colophon.**  It reads: “Reverently made for universal distribution by Wang Jie on behalf of his two parents on the fifteenth day of the fourth month of the ninth year of the Xiantong reign”, which corresponds to 11 May 868.
- **Its status.**  The British Library calls it the earliest complete survival of a dated printed book. [wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jingangjing.jpg)[lifelonglearningcollaborative](https://www.lifelonglearningcollaborative.org/silkroads/articles/diamond-sutra-translation.pdf) Earlier printed fragments exist, but none is dated. It has also been called the first creative work with an explicit “public domain” dedication.
- **Ritual framing.**  It begins with a mantra to purify the mouth before recitation and an invocation of eight vajra deities, and ends with another mantra. It was a ritual object as well as a text.
- **Merit.**  The British Library notes that by sponsoring the printing, Wang Jie “would have acquired merit for both his parents and himself.”

**Other Dunhuang copies.**  As Sam van Schaik writes in *IDP News*  38, “The Dunhuang collections contain hundreds of copies of the Diamond Sutra, mostly in Chinese, with around fifty manuscripts in Tibetan and a few fragments in Khotanese.” Their colophons are moving:

- A nun in 616 hoped all who heard the sutra would benefit, and that she could leave the desert frontier and return to the capital.
- An official in 700 had vowed to commission two copies a month if he was promoted. He explained that wartime paper shortages had delayed him.

### The 32-section division

The familiar division of Kumārajīva’s text into 32 titled sections (分) is traditionally attributed to Prince Zhaoming (Xiao Tong 蕭統, 501–531). He was the crown prince of the Liang dynasty and compiler of the literary anthology *Wen xuan* . Sanskrit-based translations (Conze, Harrison) use their own numbering, which roughly but not exactly matches. Ritual texts such as the *Xiaoshi Jingang Keyi*  (銷釋金剛科儀), still performed in Hokkien-speaking regions, are built around the 32 sections.

## 1.7 The Diamond Sutra in Chan and Zen

### The Huineng story (traditional and legendary)

According to the *Platform Sutra* :

1. Huineng (traditionally 638–713) was an illiterate firewood seller. He heard a customer reciting the Diamond Sutra and his mind “opened.”
2. The man told him that the Fifth Patriarch Hongren taught that merely holding the Diamond Sutra lets one see one’s nature and become a Buddha.
3. Huineng went to Hongren’s monastery and worked in the rice-pounding room.
4. He won the famous verse contest against Shenxiu.
5. Late at night, Hongren privately expounded the Diamond Sutra to him. Hearing the line "應無所住而生其心" — roughly, “one should give rise to a mind that abides nowhere” — Huineng was fully awakened and received the robe.

**Source criticism (essential).**

- Most modern scholars, following John McRae and others, regard Huineng’s extended biography as legendary. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huineng) Much of it was likely shaped or invented by his self-proclaimed disciple Shenhui as part of a campaign against the “Northern School.”
- The *Platform Sutra*  grew from about 12,000 characters in the Dunhuang manuscripts (copied around 830–860, probably based on an edition of about 780) to over 20,000 in the Song and Yuan editions of Qisong and Zongbao (1291). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platform_Sutra)[researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394071281_Words_Falter_in_Encapsulating_the_Dao_yanyudaoduan_The_Philosophy_of_Language_of_Zen_Buddhism_in_The_Platform_Sutra_of_the_Sixth_Patriarch)
- The famous “abide nowhere” line, explicit in the later editions, is reportedly not in the Dunhuang version. Zen teacher Dosho Port remarks wryly that this is “proof that the teaching of nonabiding does not abide.”

### From Laṅkāvatāra to Diamond

- Early Chan was associated with the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* ; one early lineage text is the *Records of the Masters of the Laṅkāvatāra* , c. 713.
- The “East Mountain” teachers Daoxin and Hongren also emphasized the Prajñāpāramitā sutras.
- The “Southern School” around Huineng and Shenhui shifted the doctrinal basis toward the Diamond Sutra.
- Historian Philip Yampolsky called Shenhui’s claim that the Diamond, not the Laṅkāvatāra, was Bodhidharma’s paramount sutra “pure fabrication.” [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shenhui)
- **Study note.**  The shift is real as a matter of Chan self-understanding. As history of Bodhidharma’s lineage, it is retrospective myth-making.

### Deshan and the rice-cake seller (a kōan, legendary)

- **The setting.**  Deshan Xuanjian (780/782–865) was famous for his Diamond Sutra learning; he was nicknamed “Diamond Zhou.” He headed south to refute the Chan claim of a “special transmission outside the scriptures,” carrying commentaries on the sutra.
- **The question.**  An old woman selling refreshments asked him: since the Diamond Sutra says past mind, present mind, and future mind cannot be grasped, with which mind would he “refresh” himself (點心, a pun on “dim sum,” literally “dot the mind”)?
- **The outcome.**  He could not answer. She sent him to the master Longtan, and after awakening there he burned his commentaries.
- **Where it is told.**  The story appears in the *Blue Cliff Record*  commentary, the *Gateless Gate*  (case 28), and Dōgen’s *Shōbōgenzō*  fascicle *Shin fukatoku*  (“Mind Cannot Be Grasped,” 1241). In that fascicle Dōgen criticizes both Deshan and the old woman and suggests what each should have said.
- **Study note.**  Read the story as a teaching device about the gap between knowing the text and living it. Burning the books is a dramatic gesture, not a recommendation to stop studying, and Dōgen himself kept writing.

### Korea, Japan, Tibet, and popular religion

- **Korea (Seon).**  The Diamond Sutra is a core scripture of the Jogye Order. The Korean monk Gihwa (Hamheo Deuktong, 1376–1433) wrote an influential sub-commentary on the “Five Masters’ Commentaries” (金剛經五家解). Paul Harrison discusses this line of transmission in “From Asaṅga to Gihwa: Reflections on the Commentarial Tradition of the *Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā* ” (2023).
- **Japan (Zen).**  The Heart Sutra is chanted far more often in daily Zen liturgy. The Diamond Sutra lives on mainly in kōans (e.g., *Blue Cliff Record*  case 97 on section 16) and in Dōgen.
- **Tibet.**  The sutra is a canonical Kangyur text with Indian commentaries by Kamalaśīla (c. 740–795) and Vasubandhu. [theknowledgebase](https://www.theknowledgebase.com/en/master-kamalashila-meditations-diamond-cutter-sutra-10-2016-en/) The only known native Tibetan commentary is by Chone Lama Drakpa Shedrup (1675–1748). [abuddhistlibrary](https://www.abuddhistlibrary.com/Buddhism/F-%20Miscellaneous/Miscellaneous%20Buddhism/The%20Diamond-Cutter%20Sutra%20with%20Commentary/The%20Diamond-Cutter%20Sutra%20with%20Commentary.htm) The Dalai Lama taught it publicly in Bodhgaya on 22–23 January 2018, “primarily for Chinese students,” and taught it again in Riga, Latvia, in June 2018.
- **Chinese popular religion.**  Copying and recitation were (and are) widespread merit-making practices, and miracle tales are recorded in Chinese, Japanese, Tibetan, and Mongolian sources. On the medieval tales, see Chiew Hui Ho, *Diamond Sutra Narratives: Textual Production and Lay Religiosity in Medieval China* . Recitation remains, in van Schaik’s words, “the most significant use of the Diamond Sutra in modern Buddhist cultures.” [bl](https://idp.bl.uk/newsletters/winter-2011-2012/)

**Where interpreters disagree:**

1. **Is the formula logic or paradox?**  Harrison and Richard H. Jones say it is intelligible logic. D. T. Suzuki and much of popular Zen treat it as a deliberate paradox of “spiritual intuition.”
2. **Is ultimate reality emptiness or Buddha-nature?**  Madhyamaka readers (Kamalaśīla, Conze) stress emptiness. The Huineng-attributed commentary and much of Chan read “the mind that abides nowhere” as one’s original, pure nature.
3. **Is the second half repetition or deepening?**  Conze sees section 17 onward as a doubling. The Chinese tradition sees subtler doubts being addressed (see Part 5).
4. **What about the merit passages?**  Scholars (Schopen) treat them as central to early Mahāyāna religious practice. Many modern Western teachers treat them as skillful means or skip them.

## 1.9 Who Wrote “Huineng’s” Commentary?

The *Exegesis on the Diamond Sutra*  (金剛經解義, also known as 六祖口訣, the “Sixth Patriarch’s Oral Instructions”) is widely published under Huineng’s name. Thomas Cleary’s 1998 Shambhala translation presents it as Huineng’s verse-by-verse commentary without an authorship caveat, and English Wikipedia also credits Huineng without qualification. The evidence is weaker than that:

- **The earliest datable witness is 1084.**  That year Luo Shi (羅適) of Mount Tiantai printed the text with a postface. He had collected eight manuscript versions over ten years.
- **Doubts are old.**  Luo himself reports that some readers doubted it because Huineng was illiterate. He suggested that disciples had recorded Huineng’s words.
- **The modern verdict.**  Yang Huinan (National Taiwan University), in “The Interpretation and Transmission of the Diamond Sutra” (*Chung-Hwa Buddhist Journal*  14, 2001), concludes that both works signed by Huineng “should be regarded as forgeries” (應該是偽作). He shows that the separate one-fascicle *Koujue*  was copied word for word from the first half of Luo Shi’s postface.
- **Internal evidence.**  The *Exegesis*  is saturated with buddha-nature thought; for example, it glosses “self” as “nature” (我者，性也). The *Koujue*  uses Confucian and Daoist cosmology (Heaven’s mandate, the five phases). The Japanese historian Nukariya Kaiten judged it suspect because it differs sharply from the *Platform Sutra*  and lacks terse Tang prose.
- **Not everyone dismisses it.**  Sekiguchi Shindai, Charles Wei-hsun Fu, and Takeuchi Kōdō treat the *Exegesis*  as important for understanding the Huineng tradition. By the 12th century, Yefu Daochuan accepted it as the Sixth Patriarch’s work.
- **Study recommendation.**  Cite it as “the commentary attributed to Huineng (probably Song-era or earlier, compiled within the Chan tradition).” It is valuable as a record of how Chan read the sutra, not as Huineng’s own words.

# PART 2 — SECTION-BY-SECTION WALKTHROUGH (32 SECTIONS)

**Format:**  Title (with Zhaoming’s Chinese title) → What happens → Key teaching → Interpretive notes → Reflection question.

## Cluster A: The Setting and the Question (Sections 1–2)

### Section 1 — The Occasion of the Assembly (法會因由分)

- **What happens:**  The Buddha is staying at Jetavana, Anāthapiṇḍada’s park near Śrāvastī, with 1,250 monks. At mealtime he puts on his robe, takes his bowl, and walks into the city to beg for food in sequence. He returns, eats, puts away his robe and bowl, washes his feet, arranges his seat, and sits.
- **Key teaching:**  The sutra on the highest wisdom opens with the most ordinary actions. Nothing supernatural happens.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Chan readers see the whole teaching already present here: awakening is wearing clothes and eating rice, done completely. The Huineng-attributed commentary and many modern teachers treat the alms round as a model of non-abiding activity. **Study note:**  Begging “in sequence,” without skipping poor households for rich ones, is already an ethic of non-discrimination.
- **Reflection:**  Which of my routine actions today could I do as completely as the Buddha folds his robe?

### Section 2 — Subhūti’s Request (善現啟請分)

- **What happens:**  Subhūti rises, bares his right shoulder, kneels, and praises the Buddha’s care for bodhisattvas. He asks how a person who has set out on the bodhisattva path should *stand*  or *abide*  (住), *proceed* , and *subdue*  or *control*  the mind (降伏其心).
- **Key teaching:**  The whole sutra answers a practical question: where does the mind stand, and how is it trained?
- **Interpretive notes:**  In Harrison’s rendering, the Sanskrit asks how one should “take his stand… proceed… control the mind.” Kumārajīva’s two-part version (abide and subdue) became the classic Chinese framing, and Nan Huai-Chin and Hsuan Hua structure their lectures around it.
- **Reflection:**  Where is my mind “standing” right now — on a worry, a plan, a grievance, an identity?

## Cluster B: The Core Answer (Sections 3–8)

### Section 3 — The True Teaching of the Great Vehicle (大乘正宗分)

- **What happens:**  The Buddha says a bodhisattva should resolve to lead all beings to final nirvāṇa. The list covers beings born from eggs, wombs, moisture, or transformation; with form or without; with perception, without perception, or neither. Yet having done so, no being at all has been led to nirvāṇa. Why? Because a bodhisattva who holds the notion of a self, a person, a being, or a life-span is not a bodhisattva.
- **Key teaching:**  The great vow and selflessness are a single stance: infinite compassion without the frame of “me saving them.”
- **Interpretive notes:**  Thich Nhat Hanh uses the image of the right hand caring for the injured left hand without saying “I am helping you.” Vasubandhu and Thich Nhat Hanh are both described as treating sections 1–5 as the complete core of the teaching, with the rest answering doubts.
- **Reflection:**  When I help someone, what story about “helper” and “helped” am I telling?

### Section 4 — Wondrous Practice Without Abiding (妙行無住分)

- **What happens:**  A bodhisattva should give without abiding in anything: not in forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, or mental objects. They should not even abide in the notion of a sign. The merit of such giving is as unmeasurable as space in the ten directions.
- **Key teaching:**  Giving (dāna) without grasping at giver, gift, recipient, or reward. Later tradition calls this the “purity of the three wheels” (三輪體空).
- **Interpretive notes:**  Hsing Yun’s first point (“giving without attachment to self”) comes from here.
- **Reflection:**  What did I give this week that I am still keeping an account of?

### Section 5 — Seeing the Truth as It Is (如理實見分)

- **What happens:**  Can the Tathāgata be seen by his bodily marks? Subhūti answers no: the marks the Buddha spoke of are no marks. The Buddha then states (in Kumārajīva): 凡所有相，皆是虛妄。若見諸相非相，即見如來 — “All that has marks is deceptive. If you see that marks are not marks, you see the Tathāgata.”
- **Key teaching:**  Reality, and the Buddha, are not identical to appearances. Seeing appearances *as*  appearances is itself seeing truly.
- **Interpretive notes:**  This is one of the most quoted lines in East Asian Buddhism. Kumārajīva’s wording is more sweeping than the Sanskrit, which is a good example of Harrison’s point that Kumārajīva “simplifies and augments.”
- **Reflection:**  Whom am I judging entirely by “marks” — appearance, status, résumé?

### Section 6 — True Faith Is Rare (正信希有分)

- **What happens:**  Subhūti asks whether anyone in the far future will truly believe such teachings. The Buddha says yes: those who keep the precepts and cultivate merit, having planted roots of goodness under countless Buddhas, will produce pure faith with even a single thought. They grasp neither dharma nor non-dharma. Then comes the raft: the teaching is like a raft, and even dharmas should be let go, “how much more so non-dharmas.”
- **Key teaching:**  Teachings are tools. Do not cling to them; and do not cling to rejecting them either.
- **Interpretive notes:**  The raft parable echoes the early-Buddhist Alagaddūpama Sutta (Majjhima Nikāya 22). Note that faith here is linked to *ethics*  (precepts) and *merit* . The sutra does not offer emptiness to the morally careless.
- **Reflection:**  Which of my “rafts” (methods, beliefs, routines) got me across a river I have already crossed?

### Section 7 — No Attainment, No Teaching (無得無說分)

- **What happens:**  Has the Tathāgata attained supreme awakening, or taught any dharma? Subhūti says there is no fixed dharma called supreme awakening, and no fixed dharma the Tathāgata teaches. The dharma cannot be grasped or spoken; it is neither dharma nor non-dharma. All sages are distinguished by the unconditioned (無為法).
- **Key teaching:**  “No fixed dharma” (無有定法). Truth is not a possession or a formula.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Chan uses this to justify its flexibility of method. Madhyamaka readers see the two truths: conventionally there is teaching, ultimately nothing is grasped.
- **Reflection:**  Where am I treating a useful method as the “one right way”?

### Section 8 — Born from the Dharma (依法出生分)

- **What happens:**  This is the first merit comparison. Filling a whole “three-thousand-great-thousand” world system with the seven treasures and giving it away brings great merit. But accepting and explaining even four lines of this teaching brings more. All Buddhas and their awakening come forth from this teaching. Yet “the Buddha-dharma is not the Buddha-dharma.”
- **Key teaching:**  Wisdom outweighs material generosity, and even “the teaching” must not be made into a thing.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Schopen links these passages to the cult of the book. **Study note:**  The comparison does not disparage material giving. It ranks transformation of understanding above transfer of goods.
- **Reflection:**  What “four lines” have most changed my life, and have I passed them on?

## Cluster C: Attainment Without Attaining (Sections 9–10)

### Section 9 — One Mark Which Is No Mark (一相無相分)

- **What happens:**  The Buddha asks whether a stream-enterer thinks “I have attained the fruit of stream-entry.” Subhūti says no: they have entered nothing. The same holds for the once-returner, the non-returner, and the arhat. If an arhat thought “I have attained arhatship,” that would be grasping at self and beings. Subhūti says the Buddha calls him foremost among those who dwell without strife (*araṇā* ), yet he does not think “I am a desire-free arhat.” That is exactly why he can be called one who delights in non-contention.
- **Key teaching:**  Real attainment does not carry a badge. The thought “I have arrived” undoes the arrival.
- **Interpretive notes:**  In early Buddhist texts Subhūti is indeed praised as foremost among those who dwell without strife (*araṇa-vihārin* ). He is the perfect interlocutor for a sutra about not contending over views.
- **Reflection:**  Which achievement am I quietly using as an identity?

### Section 10 — Adorning Pure Lands (莊嚴淨土分)

- **What happens:**  When the Buddha was with the earlier Buddha Dīpaṃkara, did he obtain any dharma? No. Does a bodhisattva “adorn Buddha-lands”? Adorning is not adorning; that is why it is called adorning. Therefore a bodhisattva should give rise to a pure mind that abides in no form, sound, and so on: 應無所住而生其心, “should give rise to the mind without abiding anywhere.” Then comes the image of a body as large as Mount Sumeru: is that large? Very large, says Subhūti, because the Buddha calls it no body; thus it is called a large body.
- **Key teaching:**  This is Huineng’s line. The mind should be *produced* , active and responsive, while abiding nowhere.
- **Interpretive notes:**  The grammar matters: it is not “have no mind” but “give rise to mind” (生其心) *without*  a fixed resting place. Chan teachers present this as the balance of engagement and freedom. Dīpaṃkara’s prediction of Śākyamuni’s buddhahood returns in section 17.
- **Reflection:**  Can I give my full attention to this conversation without needing it to go my way?

## Cluster D: Merit, the Book, and the Name (Sections 11–13)

### Section 11 — The Superiority of Unconditioned Merit (無為福勝分)

- **What happens:**  Imagine as many Ganges rivers as there are grains of sand in the Ganges, and then all the sand in all those rivers. Someone fills that many worlds with treasure and gives it away. Teaching four lines of this sutra exceeds that merit.
- **Key teaching:**  The value of insight is beyond any quantity.
- **Interpretive notes:**  The escalating numbers are a rhetorical device. Indian literature uses huge numbers to break the habit of measuring.
- **Reflection:**  What do I measure obsessively that resists measurement?

### Section 12 — Honoring the True Teaching (尊重正教分)

- **What happens:**  Wherever even four lines of this sutra are taught, that place should be honoured by gods, humans, and asuras like a shrine (*caitya* ). Wherever the sutra is, the Buddha or a revered disciple is present.
- **Key teaching:**  The sacredness of the teaching is located in the place where it is spoken.
- **Interpretive notes:**  This is the phrase at the centre of Schopen’s 1975 “cult of the book” thesis (also at 15c).
- **Reflection:**  What makes a place sacred for me — and could a kitchen table become one?

### Section 13 — Receiving and Keeping It According to the Dharma (如法受持分)

- **What happens:**  Subhūti asks for the sutra’s name. It is to be called *The Diamond Perfection of Wisdom* , and yet “the perfection of wisdom is not the perfection of wisdom.” Dust particles are not dust particles; the world is not a world; the 32 marks are not marks. Giving away one’s life as many times as there are Ganges sands is outmatched by teaching four lines.
- **Key teaching:**  This is the formula at its most compact: X is not X, therefore it is called X. Even the sutra’s own title is subjected to it.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Conze translates “world system” as “no-system.” Harrison argues the Sanskrit should be read “systemless” (lacking an essence), not “non-system” (see Part 3).
- **Reflection:**  Pick one label I use daily (“my job,” “my house,” “my anxiety”). How is it real, and how is it “X-less”?

## Cluster E: Subhūti’s Tears, Patience, and the True Speaker (Sections 14–16)

### Section 14 — Leaving Marks, Tranquil Extinction (離相寂滅分)

This is the longest and richest section.

- **Subhūti weeps.**  Hearing this teaching, Subhūti weeps: he has never heard anything so profound. Those who hear it in the future without fear or dread will be extraordinary.
- **The patient sage.**  The Buddha recalls a past life as Kṣāntivādin, the “teacher of patience.” King Kali (Kaliṅga) cut his body to pieces. Because he had no notion of self, person, being, or life-span, no anger arose; had he held such notions, he would have hated. For 500 lifetimes he practised patience this way.
- **Therefore,**  a bodhisattva should leave all notions and give rise to a mind that abides nowhere.
- **The true speaker.**  The Tathāgata is a speaker of truth, of reality, of suchness; he does not deceive and does not contradict himself (真語者、實語者、如語者、不誑語者、不異語者). In Müller’s words: “a Tathâgata says what is real, says what is true, says the things as they are; a Tathâgata does not speak untruth”. Yet the dharma he realized is “neither truth nor falsehood.”
- **The darkness simile.**  A bodhisattva who gives while immersed in objects is “as a man who has entered the darkness [and] would not see anything” (Müller). One who gives without being immersed is like someone with eyes in sunlight.
- **Key teaching:**  Patience (kṣānti) becomes possible when there is no fixed self to defend. Truthfulness and non-grasping go together.
- **Interpretive notes:**  The Kṣāntivādin story also appears in the Jātaka collection. Thich Nhat Hanh reads it as showing that understanding, not suppression, dissolves anger. **Study note:**  This is not advice to endure abuse passively (see Part 4 cautions).
- **Reflection:**  In my last angry episode, which self-image felt “cut”?

### Section 15 — The Merit of Keeping This Sutra (持經功德分)

- **What happens:**  Someone might give up their body as many times as Ganges sands, three times a day, for countless aeons. Someone else simply hears this sutra with faith and does not resist it; their merit is greater, and greater still if they copy, recite, and explain it. The sutra is for those set out on the “supreme vehicle.” Those attached to lesser views cannot hear it. Wherever it is, the place is to be honoured like a stūpa.
- **Key teaching:**  Openness (“not resisting”) is itself transformative.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Schopen’s phrase recurs here (15c).
- **Reflection:**  Which teaching do I resist because it threatens my self-image?

### Section 16 — Purifying Karmic Obstacles (能淨業障分)

- **What happens:**  If people who uphold this sutra are despised or slighted, it is because of past bad karma that should have led to a bad rebirth. Being slighted now exhausts that karma, and they will attain awakening. The Buddha says he served countless Buddhas before Dīpaṃkara, yet that merit is not a fraction of the merit of upholding this sutra in a later age. If he described it fully, listeners might go mad with disbelief. The meaning of the sutra, and its fruit, are inconceivable.
- **Key teaching:**  Being slighted can be worked with rather than resented, and the sutra declares its own meaning beyond conception.
- **Interpretive notes:**  This is the source of *Blue Cliff Record*  case 97. **Study note:**  The “karma” framing is traditional. A modern reader can take it as an invitation not to add a second injury (resentment) to the first (being disrespected), without accepting a theory of victim-blame.
- **Reflection:**  Can I meet a slight today without adding a second arrow?

## Cluster F: The Second Turning (Sections 17–19)

### Section 17 — Ultimately No Self (究竟無我分)

- **What happens:**  Subhūti repeats his opening question. The Buddha repeats the answer: resolve to free all beings, yet no being is freed. “There is no such thing (dharma) as one who has entered on the path of the Bodhisattvas” (Müller). Dīpaṃkara predicted Śākyamuni’s buddhahood precisely *because*  no dharma was attained; if there had been, no prediction would have been given. “Tathāgata” means the suchness of all dharmas. All dharmas are Buddha-dharmas, and “all dharmas” are not all dharmas. A bodhisattva who thinks “I will adorn Buddha-lands” is not a bodhisattva. One who penetrates the selflessness of dharmas is called a true bodhisattva.
- **Key teaching:**  The deepest layer: even the bodhisattva identity is released.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Conze observed that here the sutra “veers back to its beginning,” repeating sections 2–3 and 10. Chinese commentators read the second half as moving from coarse to subtle attachment: from clinging to objects and practices, to clinging to the very idea of oneself as a practitioner or bodhisattva. Harrison bases his reading of the whole formula on the phrase here, “dharmas are selfless” (*nirātmāno dharmāḥ* ).
- **Reflection:**  Is “being a good person / spiritual person / helper” an identity I defend?

### Section 18 — One Body, Seen Alike (一體同觀分)

- **What happens:**
    - *The five eyes.*  Does the Tathāgata have the five eyes: the physical eye, the divine eye, the wisdom eye, the dharma eye, and the Buddha eye? Yes.
    - *Countless minds.*  Picture as many world systems as there are grains in as many Ganges rivers as there are grains of the Ganges. The Buddha knows the various mind-streams of all the beings in them, because those “minds” are not minds.
    - *The three times.*  “A past thought is not perceived, a future thought is not perceived, and the present thought is not perceived” (Müller). Kumārajīva has: 過去心不可得，現在心不可得，未來心不可得.
- **Key teaching:**  Mind cannot be pinned down in any of the three times. This is the line the rice-cake seller used on Deshan.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Dōgen’s *Shin fukatoku*  is an extended meditation on this passage. Gudō Nishijima contrasted it with Descartes’ “I think, therefore I am”: for Buddhism the mind cannot be grasped by the intellect.
- **Reflection:**  Right now, try to find “my present mind.” What do you actually find?

### Section 19 — The Dharma Realm Transformed Throughout (法界通化分)

- **What happens:**  Another treasure-giving comparison. If merit had real existence, the Tathāgata would not call it great; because it has none, he calls it great.
- **Key teaching:**  Merit is real precisely as non-substantial. Clinging to “my merit” shrinks it.
- **Reflection:**  Can I do good today without banking it?

## Cluster G: Body, Teaching, and Dharma Revisited (Sections 20–25)

### Section 20 — Leaving Form and Leaving Marks (離色離相分)

- **What happens:**  Can the Buddha be seen by a perfected physical body, or by complete marks? No; these are not perfect bodies or marks, and so they are called such.
- **Key teaching:**  This restates section 5 at a subtler level.
- **Reflection:**  Whose “perfect form” (a guru, a CEO, a partner) am I mistaking for the real thing?

### Section 21 — No Teaching Taught (非說所說分)

- **What happens:**  Do not think the Tathāgata thinks “I have a dharma to teach.” Whoever says so slanders the Buddha. Teaching the dharma means there is no dharma to be taught. Subhūti, here called “wisdom-life” (慧命), asks whether future beings will believe. They are neither beings nor non-beings.
- **Key teaching:**  Teaching without being a “teacher.”
- **Interpretive notes:**  The “wisdom-life Subhūti” passage is traditionally said to have been inserted into Kumārajīva’s text later (see 1.6).
- **Reflection:**  When I advise or correct someone, am I attached to being the one who knows?

### Section 22 — No Dharma to Be Attained (無法可得分)

- **What happens:**  Was there anything attained in supreme awakening? Not even the slightest dharma; that is why it is called supreme awakening.
- **Key teaching:**  Awakening is not an acquisition.
- **Reflection:**  What am I trying to “get” from my practice?

### Section 23 — Purify the Mind, Practise the Good (淨心行善分)

- **What happens:**  This dharma is equal, with nothing high or low; that is why it is called supreme awakening. It is realized by practising all wholesome dharmas without notions of self, person, being, or life-span. And “wholesome dharmas” are not wholesome dharmas, which is why they are so called.
- **Key teaching:**  **Ethics is not abolished; it is the path.**  This is one of the strongest textual answers to the misreading “nothing matters.”
- **Interpretive notes:**  “Equality” (平等) became a major theme in Chinese social readings of the sutra.
- **Reflection:**  Which good habit am I doing self-consciously, and can I do it simply?

### Section 24 — Merit and Wisdom Beyond Compare (福智無比分)

- **What happens:**  Heaps of the seven treasures as large as all the Sumeru mountains in a world system, given away, cannot match four lines of this sutra taught.
- **Reflection:**  What would it mean to value understanding over accumulation this month?

### Section 25 — Transforming Without Anyone Transformed (化無所化分)

- **What happens:**  Do not think the Tathāgata thinks “I liberate beings.” There are no beings that the Tathāgata liberates; if there were, he would be holding notions of self and so on. The “self” he speaks of is no self, but ordinary people take it as a self. And “ordinary people” are not ordinary people.
- **Key teaching:**  Even the category “ordinary/foolish people” is released, so there is no spiritual condescension.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Thich Nhat Hanh observes that in the Diamond Sutra the Buddha does not call anyone a bad person: there is “no discrimination, no blaming.”
- **Reflection:**  Whom do I privately regard as “unenlightened,” “toxic,” or “less than”?

## Cluster H: Marks, Annihilation, and Merit (Sections 26–28)

### Section 26 — The Dharma-Body Is Not Marks (法身非相分)

- **What happens:**  Can the Tathāgata be recognized by the 32 marks? In Kumārajīva’s version Subhūti first says yes. The Buddha points out that a universal monarch (*cakravartin* ) also has the 32 marks, so he would be a Tathāgata too. Subhūti corrects himself. Then comes the verse (Kumārajīva): 若以色見我，以音聲求我，是人行邪道，不能見如來 — “Whoever sees me by form, or seeks me by sound, walks a wrong path and cannot see the Tathāgata.”
- **Key teaching:**  Holiness is not detectable by external signs.
- **Interpretive notes:**  The Sanskrit adds a second verse: the Buddhas are to be seen through the dharma, since their bodies are the dharma (*dharmakāya* ), and *dharmatā*  cannot be cognized as an object. Harrison notes it is absent from Kumārajīva’s translation. Harrison also argues the verse means *dharmatā*  is not an object of cognition, not that the dharma is impossible to understand, correcting Müller and Schopen.
- **Reflection:**  What “signs” of success or spirituality impress me most, and why?

### Section 27 — No Cutting Off, No Annihilation (無斷無滅分)

- **What happens:**  Do not think the Tathāgata attained awakening without possessing the marks, either. And do not think that those set out on the path proclaim the destruction or annihilation of dharmas. Müller: “Nor should anybody… say… that the destruction or annihilation of anything is proclaimed by those who have entered on the path of the Bodhisattvas.”
- **Key teaching:**  **Emptiness is not nihilism.**  This is the sutra’s explicit safeguard.
- **Reflection:**  When “letting go” sounds like “nothing matters” in my head, what am I avoiding?

### Section 28 — Neither Receiving Nor Coveting (不受不貪分)

- **What happens:**  Compare a bodhisattva who gives away worlds of treasure with one who knows that dharmas are selfless and attains patient acceptance (*kṣānti* ). The latter’s merit is greater, because bodhisattvas do not “receive” merit. Why not? Because they do not covet or grasp it.
- **Key teaching:**  Good done without appropriation.
- **Reflection:**  Can I accept recognition without needing it, and decline it without performing modesty?

## Cluster I: Coming, Going, and the Final Vision (Sections 29–32)

### Section 29 — Dignified Demeanor, Tranquil and Still (威儀寂淨分)

- **What happens:**  If someone says the Tathāgata comes or goes, sits or lies down, they have not understood. “Tathāgata” means one who comes from nowhere and goes nowhere; that is why he is called Tathāgata.
- **Key teaching:**  This is a play on the title *tathā-gata*  / *tathā-āgata*  (“thus-gone” / “thus-come”). Suchness does not travel.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Thich Nhat Hanh builds his teaching on “no coming, no going” and “no birth, no death” around this section, especially in teaching on grief.
- **Reflection:**  Where is someone I have lost “gone”? Sit with that question without rushing to an answer.

### Section 30 — The Composite Whole (一合理相分)

- **What happens:**  Suppose a world system were ground into dust. Would there be many particles? Yes, but particles are not particles. The world is not a world. If the world truly existed it would be a “composite whole” or “one mass” (一合相, *piṇḍa-grāha* ). Yet the composite whole is spoken of as no composite whole; it is inexpressible, and only ordinary people crave it.
- **Key teaching:**  Both atomism (reality as ultimate particles) and holism (reality as one solid mass) are conceptual graspings.
- **Reflection:**  Do I think of “the company,” “the family,” or “society” as a solid lump? What is it made of?

### Section 31 — Not Giving Rise to Views (知見不生分)

- **What happens:**  If someone says the Buddha taught a “view of self,” a view of person, and so on, have they understood? No. The view of self he spoke of is not a view of self; thus it is called a view of self. One set on awakening should know, see, and resolve on all dharmas in this way, without producing the notion of a dharma.
- **Key teaching:**  Even “no-self” must not become a position to cling to.
- **Reflection:**  Have I turned “non-attachment” into a new identity?

### Section 32 — Manifestations Are Not Real (應化非真分)

- **What happens:**  One last comparison: immeasurable worlds of treasure versus upholding four lines. How should one explain it to others? Without grasping at marks, unmoved in suchness (不取於相，如如不動). Then the closing verse.
    - **Kumārajīva (six similes):**  一切有為法，如夢幻泡影，如露亦如電，應作如是觀 — “All conditioned dharmas are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow; like dew, and also like lightning; one should contemplate them thus.”
    - **Sanskrit (nine similes),**  in Müller: “As in the sky: stars, darkness, a lamp, a phantom, dew, a bubble, a dream, a flash of lightning, and a cloud—thus should we look upon the world.” The Sanskrit terms are star (*tārakā* ), a fault of vision (*timira* ), lamp, illusion (*māyā* ), dewdrop, bubble, dream, lightning, and cloud. Harrison renders the second as “a clouding of the sight.”
    - **The ending.**  Subhūti, the monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen, gods, humans, asuras, and gandharvas rejoice.
- **Key teaching:**  Seeing conditioned things as fleeting and luminous, while still teaching and acting.
- **Interpretive notes:**  Kumārajīva’s “shadow” (影) does not exactly match any of the nine Sanskrit images. Red Pine’s widely quoted English verse rendering mixes images for poetic effect, so do not use it as a literal guide to the Sanskrit.
- **Reflection:**  Choose one simile today and hold one experience in its light.

# PART 3 — CORE TEACHINGS AND PHILOSOPHICAL ANALYSIS

## 3.1 The Formula: “X Is Not X, Therefore It Is Called X”

**Examples:**  the perfection of wisdom (13), dust and worlds (13, 30), the 32 marks (13), beings (21), “all dharmas” (17), merit (19), good dharmas (23), the self-view (31).

| Reading | Main proponents | What the formula means | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Logic of not / paradox** | Hajime Nakamura (“logic of not”); D. T. Suzuki (*sokuhi*  即非, “logic of spiritual intuition”) | A deliberate contradiction that jolts the mind out of either/or thinking | Captures the text’s effect on readers | Can turn into mystification. Jayarava argues Suzuki was “misled by the Chinese translations.” |
| **Perspectival shift** | Shigenori Nagatomo, “The Logic of the Diamond Sutra: A is not A, therefore it is A,” *Asian Philosophy*  10(3), 2000: 213–244 | Contradictory from a “dualistic, either-or egological standpoint”; intelligible once one shifts to a “non-dualistic, non-egological stance” | Philosophically careful; links logic to transformation of self | Still treats the surface form as paradox |
| **Dialectic** | Edward Conze | Thesis, negation, and a higher affirmation | Classic and influential | Based on Conze’s eclectic edition |
| **Bahuvrīhi / “X-less”** | Paul Harrison (2006), endorsed with modifications by Jayarava and Richard H. Jones | The negated term is an *adjective* : “the world system is *systemless* ,” i.e. lacking an essence. The world is called “world” conventionally while being essence-less, because “dharmas are selfless.” | Fits the Sanskrit grammar and the Tibetan translations; removes the apparent contradiction | Less dramatic; some see it as flattening the text’s rhetorical force |
| **Deconstruct and reconstruct** | David Kalupahana | Affirm (naïve), critique (no substance), reconstruct (conventional and dependently arisen). He calls the sutra “one colossal attempt to avoid the extremist use of language… while at the same time retaining their pragmatic value.” | Shows why concepts keep their use | Less attention to meditation practice |
| **Two truths (Madhyamaka)** | Kamalaśīla; Conze broadly; Tibetan tradition | “Not X” = ultimately empty; “called X” = conventionally valid | Systematic; connects to Nāgārjuna | The sutra itself never uses two-truths vocabulary |
| **Three natures (Yogācāra)** | Asaṅga and Vasubandhu tradition | “X” as imagined (*parikalpita* ), “not X” as seen through dependence (*paratantra* ), and the purified seeing (*pariniṣpanna* ) | Gives a map of cognition and its transformation | **Study note:**  this mapping is a common modern schematization of the Yogācāra approach. Check Asaṅga’s actual verses before attributing a specific triad to him. |
| **Interbeing** | Thich Nhat Hanh | A flower is made of non-flower elements (sun, rain, soil); seeing that is seeing the flower | Accessible and experiential | Some scholars (one recent study) read his approach as rejecting the Western law of identity, which is contested |
| **Chan direct pointing** | Huineng tradition, Linji, Dōgen | The formula is a finger pointing at the mind that abides nowhere; understanding it means living it | Ties text to practice | Easily becomes anti-intellectual (see Deshan) |

**Bottom line for study.**  Harrison’s reading is the most defensible on philological grounds. It means “X lacks its own essence, which is why it can function as X.” The paradox and Chan readings describe the *experience*  of reading the sutra well. Keep both: the grammar tells you what it means, and the practice tells you what it does.

**Harrison’s caution.**  Harrison warns against reading “some kind of mystical subversion of language” into the formula. Jones calls the idea that the text is illogical or not meant to be understood “frankly baffling and insulting to the ingenuity of the authors.”

## 3.2 The Missing Word: Emptiness

The Diamond Sutra never uses *śūnyatā*  (“emptiness”) or *svabhāva*  (“own-being”). Van Schaik notes that it eschews “argument and analysis and not even using the term ‘emptiness’.”

**Why it matters:**

1. It supports the early-dating hypothesis: the text may predate the technical vocabulary.
2. It shows the teaching working by *performance*  rather than doctrine. The text makes you do the negating instead of telling you a theory.
3. It blocks a common trap: turning “emptiness” into a new thing to believe in. The sutra’s own logic, applied to emptiness, would yield “emptiness is not emptiness, therefore it is called emptiness.”

## 3.3 The Four Notions (四相 *sì xiàng* ; Skt. *saṃjñā* )

| Notion | Sanskrit | Chinese | Traditional sense | Thich Nhat Hanh’s gloss | Practical shorthand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self | *ātman* | 我相 | A permanent, unchanging “I” | A self made entirely of non-self elements; removing this notion is “the basic action for peace” | “This is who I am, fixed.” |
| Person | *pudgala* | 人相 | A personal entity that carries karma and transmigrates | The notion of “human” separate from other species | “People like me vs. others.” |
| Being | *sattva* | 眾生相 | A sentient being as a separate unit | Separate living beings vs. the non-living world | “Beings are separate units.” |
| Life-span / soul | *jīva* | 壽者相 | A life-principle or soul enduring from birth to death | The idea that we exist only from birth to death; “we are born and die during every second” | “My life is a fixed span I must hold onto.” |

**Terminology.**  Kumārajīva’s *xiàng*  相 usually means “mark” or “sign” (*lakṣaṇa* ). The Sanskrit here has *saṃjñā*  (“notion,” “perception,” “idea”). This is why English translations alternate between “marks,” “notions,” and “perceptions.”

**Why a bodhisattva with these notions “is not a bodhisattva”.**  The bodhisattva’s whole project is to free all beings. If that project runs on the idea of a solid self rescuing solid others, it reproduces the very structure (self vs. other, mine vs. theirs) that generates suffering. Thich Nhat Hanh calls the four notions “the crown of discrimination and fear and hate.”

**Red Pine’s approach.**  Red Pine tends to read the four terms historically, as targets in Indian philosophical debates: Brahmanical *ātman* , the Buddhist “personalist” *pudgala* , the Jain *jīva* . **Study note:**  This historical framing is useful context, but verify specific claims against his book.

## 3.4 Non-Abiding (*apratiṣṭhita* , 無住 *wú zhù* )

**What it is.**  “Non-abiding” means not taking a fixed stand in any object: sense-objects (form, sound, and so on), ideas, or even dharmas.

**What it is not:**

| Non-abiding | Look-alikes it is *not* |
| --- | --- |
| Full engagement without a fixed resting place | **Indifference**  (“I don’t care”) |
| Responsiveness: the mind is *produced*  (生其心) | **Suppression**  (“I won’t feel this”) |
| Caring without possessing | **Detachment as withdrawal**  (“I’m above this”) |
| Acting without being defined by the act | **Dissociation**  (numbness, unreality) |

The *Platform Sutra*  tradition later made “non-abiding” (無住) one of three pillars, alongside “no-thought” (無念) and “no-form” (無相). Qisong’s preface states “non-abiding is the fundamental.”

## 3.5 Giving Without Abiding: The “Economics” of Merit

- **Three-wheel purity (三輪體空).**  No reified giver, gift, or recipient. This is a later scholastic formulation that summarizes section 4.
- **The merit paradox.**  The sutra both (a) massively promotes merit and (b) says merit has no substance and that bodhisattvas don’t “receive” it (19, 28). The resolution: merit grows when it is not grasped. Grasping turns giving into a transaction.
- **Why four lines outweigh worlds of treasure:**
    - Material gifts relieve conditions temporarily.
    - Insight changes the giver’s and receiver’s relation to all conditions.
    - The merit claims also had an institutional function: promoting the copying and teaching of this text (Schopen).
- **Study note on the “economics.”**  The sutra keeps the language of accounting (“more merit”) only to break the habit of accounting. It uses the ledger to dissolve the ledger.

## 3.6 Emptiness Is Not Nihilism

The sutra explicitly blocks the slide into nihilism in several places:

- **Section 6:**  don’t grasp dharma, and don’t grasp non-dharma.
- **Section 23:**  practise *all wholesome dharmas* .
- **Section 27:**  bodhisattvas do not proclaim the annihilation of dharmas.
- **Section 31:**  don’t even cling to “no-self” as a view.

This is the classic middle way between eternalism (things have fixed essences) and annihilationism (nothing exists, nothing matters).

## 3.7 Wisdom and Compassion as One Movement

The paradox in section 3 — liberate all beings, yet no being is liberated — is not a contradiction to be solved but a description of mature compassion:

- **Wisdom without compassion**  becomes cold: “beings are empty, so why bother?”
- **Compassion without wisdom**  becomes burnout, saviour complexes, and resentment: “after all I’ve done for them.”
- **Both together**  mean tireless care with no fixed self to exhaust.

## 3.8 The Tathāgata: Not Seen by Marks, Neither Coming Nor Going

Sections 5, 13, 20, 26, and 29 dismantle the idea of a Buddha who can be identified by physical signs, tracked in space, or reduced to a body or voice. This is a teaching about every person: no one can be fully known by their “marks.” It also guards against guru-worship and charismatic authority. The verse in section 26 is often read this way in Zen.

## 3.9 Skillful Means, the Raft, and the Sutra’s Self-Reference

- **A text that relativizes itself.**  The sutra tells you to let go of the dharma (6). It says there is no dharma to teach (7, 21). It calls its own meaning “inconceivable” (16). Yet it claims supreme value for itself (8, 11–16, 24, 32).
- **Reading 1 (skillful means).**  The book is a raft: take it seriously, then let it go.
- **Reading 2 (cult of the book, Schopen).**  It is a sacred object whose presence sanctifies.
- **Study note.**  These readings need not compete. A practitioner can chant and honour the text as a support while understanding that its point is to free you even from it.

## 3.10 Time and Mind: The Three Unobtainable Minds

**The teaching (section 18).**  Past mind is gone. Future mind has not arisen. Present mind cannot be held: when you try to grasp it, it has already changed.

**Relation to mindfulness.**  Modern mindfulness often says “be in the present moment.” The Diamond Sutra goes further: even the present cannot be grasped. “Being present” therefore cannot mean *clinging*  to the present. It means meeting experience without trying to own it.

**Limits.**  This does not abolish planning, memory, or responsibility. The Buddha in section 1 still goes for alms at mealtime. The point concerns *grasping* , not functioning.

## 3.11 Comparative Perspectives (Flagged by Closeness of Fit)

| Comparison | Fit | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heart Sutra** | Close (same genre) | The Heart names emptiness explicitly (“form is emptiness, emptiness is form”) and uses a negation list. The Diamond enacts emptiness through dialogue and never names it. **Origin debate:**  Jan Nattier (1992) argued the Heart Sutra was composed in China and back-translated into Sanskrit. The hypothesis is widely discussed but not universally accepted. |
| **Nāgārjuna’s Madhyamaka** | Close | Same anti-essentialism; the two truths and dependent origination give a formal framework the Diamond lacks. Nāgārjuna’s remark that emptiness wrongly grasped is like a snake seized by the wrong end echoes the Diamond’s anti-nihilism. |
| **Daoist resonances in Chinese commentary** | Historical, real | The *Laozi* ’s “the Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao” and the *Zhuangzi* ’s “free wandering” shaped how Chinese readers like Sengzhao heard “no fixed dharma” and “abiding nowhere.” The *Koujue*  even imports Heaven’s mandate and the five phases. |
| **Apophatic theology**  (Pseudo-Dionysius, Eckhart) | Analogical | Both use negation to free the mind from conceptual idols. Apophatic theology negates in order to approach a transcendent God; the Diamond posits no such ground. Loose. |
| **Wittgenstein’s ladder**  (*Tractatus*  6.54) | Analogical, fairly apt | Throw away the ladder after climbing it, like the raft. But Wittgenstein’s ladder is propositions about logical form; the raft is a whole path of practice. |
| **Kant’s thing-in-itself** | **Loose; potentially misleading** | Kant posits a noumenal reality behind appearances. Jayarava stresses that early Buddhism “accepted no noumena behind phenomena,” and the Diamond does not say there is a true thing behind the marks. Use with care. |
| **Stoic non-attachment**  (Epictetus’ dichotomy of control) | Analogical, practically useful | Both separate effort from attachment to outcomes. Stoics keep a rational self with fixed virtue; the Diamond dissolves the self-notion itself. |
| **ACT cognitive defusion**  (Steven Hayes and colleagues) | Analogical, practically strong | Defusion means noticing “I’m having the thought that I’m a failure” instead of fusing with it. That resembles not abiding in a self-notion. ACT’s “self-as-context” resembles seeing that the self is not a fixed content. ACT is a clinical method with its own evidence base, not a Buddhist doctrine. |
| **Self-schema and identity research** | Analogical | Psychology shows that rigid self-schemas (“I am incompetent”) drive distress, and that flexible self-concepts aid resilience. This supports the *practical*  value of loosening the four notions; it does not “prove” the metaphysics. |

## 3.12 Common Misreadings and Pitfalls

1. **“Nothing matters.”**  This is directly refuted by sections 23 and 27.
2. **Spiritual bypassing.**  Psychologist John Welwood coined the term (“Principles of Inner Work: Psychological and Spiritual,” *Journal of Transpersonal Psychology*  16(1), 1984: 63–73) after observing his own Buddhist community. He described “a widespread tendency to use spiritual ideas and practices to sidestep or avoid facing unresolved emotional issues, psychological wounds, and unfinished developmental tasks.” He also warned of “premature transcendence.” The Diamond Sutra’s language of “no self, no beings, no notions” is especially easy to bypass with.
3. **Using emptiness to avoid responsibility.**  “There’s no ‘I’ who did it” is not an apology. The sutra’s true speaker (14) does not deceive.
4. **Intellectualizing.**  The Deshan story exists to puncture this.
5. **Magic charm vs. transformative text.**  Historically the sutra has been both a merit-generating object and a teaching. Teachers handle this differently:
    - Hsuan Hua and traditional Chinese teachers encourage recitation and accept its merit.
    - Thich Nhat Hanh shifts the emphasis to understanding and practice.
    - Scholars contextualize the merit claims.
    - **Study recommendation:**  recite if it supports you, and let the recitation be a training in attention, not a transaction.
6. **Non-attachment as emotional unavailability.**  This is especially damaging in relationships (see Part 4).

# PART 4 — APPLIED PERSPECTIVES

**Method note.**  Some applications draw directly on named teachers: Thich Nhat Hanh on the four notions and interbeing, Hsing Yun’s four points, the Chan reading of the alms round, Welwood on bypassing, and Roach on business. Where no source applies the sutra to a domain, I reason from the text and mark it **[reasoned from text]** .

## 4.1 Daily Life

### Core principles

- **The alms-round model (section 1).**  Ordinary actions done completely, in sequence, without skipping or rushing to the “important” part.
- **Non-abiding in sense-objects (sections 4, 10, 14).**  Enjoy food, music, and beauty without making them the place you live.
- **No fixed dharma (section 7).**  Routines serve you; you don’t serve them.

### Concrete applications

| Area | Diamond Sutra lens | Practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine chores | Section 1 | Pick one daily task (dishes, commute). Do it as the Buddha “washed his feet and sat down”: start, middle, finish, with no multitasking. |
| Consumption | Sections 4 and 30 (craving for the “composite whole”) | Before a non-essential purchase, ask: “What am I trying to abide in — status, comfort, an identity?” Wait 24 hours. **[reasoned from text]** |
| Social media and identity | Sections 5 and 26 (not seen by marks) | Notice when you curate “marks” (photos, bios) to be seen a certain way. Post once this week without checking reactions. **[reasoned from text]** |
| Decision-making | Section 7 (no fixed dharma); section 6 (the raft) | For a decision, list the “rafts” (rules of thumb) you rely on. Ask which still fit this river. |
| Habits | Section 18 (three unobtainable minds) | When a habit fires, ask: “Which mind is doing this — past conditioning, future craving, or the present?” |
| Impermanence | Section 32 (similes) | Each evening, recall one moment from the day and see it as “a dewdrop” or “a bubble.” Note what you feel: relief, grief, tenderness? |
| “Beginner’s mind” | Sections 9 and 22 (no attainment) | Approach a familiar task as if for the first time. (The phrase “beginner’s mind” comes from Shunryu Suzuki’s Zen teaching, not from the sutra, but the spirit matches “no dharma attained.”) |

### Daily micro-practice: “Where does the mind abide?”

Three times a day (at meals, echoing the alms round), stop for one breath and ask:

> *Where is my mind standing right now?*

Name it: a worry, a person, a plan, a self-image. Do not push it away. Simply notice that it is a *place*  the mind has settled, and that it could move.

## 4.2 Workplace and Career

### Core principles

- **The four notions as professional self-image [reasoned from text]:**
    - *self*  = “I am my title”;
    - *person*  = “my type (engineers, executives) vs. theirs”;
    - *being*  = colleagues as separate units competing for scarce resources;
    - *life-span*  = “my career is a fixed arc I must protect.”
- **Giving without abiding (section 4).**  Service, mentorship, and credit-sharing.
- **Araṇā, non-contention (section 9).**  Subhūti excels without needing to “win.”
- **The mind that abides nowhere (section 10).**  Full effort, without making outcomes your home.

### Applications

**1. Identity not fused with title.**  Job loss, re-organization, and retirement hit hardest when the title has become the self.

- *Exercise:*  write “I am a [title].” Then write three statements that are true of you if the title disappears tomorrow.
- *Diamond formula:*  “My role is not my role; therefore it is called my role.” It is real and functional, but it lacks an essence you must defend.

**2. Ambition without clinging.**  The sutra does not tell the bodhisattva to vow *less* : the vow is to free *all*  beings. It tells the bodhisattva to vow without the four notions. **[reasoned from text]**  Set big goals, pursue them hard, and treat outcomes as weather, not verdicts on your worth.

**3. Praise and criticism.**  Section 16 (being slighted) and section 28 (not receiving merit).

- *Praise:*  receive it with “thank you,” then notice the urge to replay it. That replay is “receiving merit.”
- *Criticism:*  separate content (useful information) from the “second arrow” (self-attack). Ask: “Which self-image feels attacked?”

**4. Performance reviews.**

- *Before:*  write down what you hope to hear. Recognize those hopes as “marks” you hope to be seen by (section 5).
- *During:*  listen for accuracy, not validation.
- *After:*  identify one actionable change. Let the rest be “a dream, an illusion, a bubble.”

**5. Failure and setbacks.**  “No dharma attained” (section 22) also means no failure is final. A failed project is “not a failure, therefore it is called a failure”: a real event with no fixed essence that defines you. **[reasoned from text]**

**6. Leadership without ego.**

- Section 21: teach without being “the one who has a dharma to teach.”
- Section 25: no condescension toward “ordinary people.”
- Section 29: no coming or going, so steadiness that doesn’t depend on personal presence.
- *Practice:*  in your next meeting, speak last. Credit others’ ideas by name.

**7. Mentorship as dāna.**  Give time and knowledge without expecting loyalty or repayment. Hsing Yun’s first point applies directly.

**8. Burnout.**  Burnout often comes from compassion *with*  the four notions: “*I*  must save *them* ; *my*  worth depends on it.” **[reasoned from text]**  Section 3’s paradox points to sustainable care: tireless effort without a self to exhaust. Section 3 is not a substitute for rest, boundaries, or workload change, though; see the cautions in 4.6.

**9. Competition and araṇā.**  Subhūti’s excellence in “non-contention” does not mean he is passive. He is the sutra’s sharpest questioner. Compete on the merits and stop competing for identity.

**10. Difficult colleagues and bosses.**

- Section 25: they are “not ordinary people” — not fixed as “toxic.”
- Section 14 (Kṣāntivādin): patience comes from not defending a fixed self-image.
- **Caution:**  patience is not tolerance of harassment, discrimination, or abuse. Use HR, documentation, and legal protections. Kṣāntivādin is an image of inner freedom, not a workplace policy.

### Workplace reflection exercise: the “four notions audit”

1. *Self:*  “What part of my identity is riding on this project?”
2. *Person:*  “Which group am I defending (my team, my profession)?”
3. *Being:*  “Whom am I treating as a separate obstacle rather than a fellow participant?”
4. *Life-span:*  “What timeline am I clinging to — ‘by 35 I should…’?”

## 4.3 Business Practices

### Core principles [mostly reasoned from text]

- **Right livelihood and the true speaker (section 14).**  Honesty in claims, contracts, and marketing.
- **Three-wheel purity (section 4).**  Fairness in exchanges without manipulation.
- **No fixed dharma (section 7) and the raft (section 6).**  Adaptability; letting go of past business models.
- **The composite whole (section 30).**  A company is not a solid lump. It is people, relationships, and conditions.
- **Brand.**  “A brand is not a brand, therefore it is called a brand”: real and useful, but without an essence apart from the conduct that sustains it.

### Applications

| Business issue | Sutra lens | Concrete practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethics and marketing | The true speaker (14) | A “true-speaker test” for every claim: is it real, true, and as things are? Would we say it to a friend? |
| Fair pricing and partnerships | Three-wheel purity (4) | Ask whether both sides would accept the deal with full information. |
| Strategy | No fixed dharma (7); the raft (6) | Annual “raft review”: which strategies served past conditions but no longer fit? Kill one. |
| Innovation | No attainment (22) | Treat current success as non-final; run small experiments that could cannibalize your own product. |
| Long-term vs. short-term | Merit not grasped (19, 28) | Measure stakeholder trust and employee development, not only quarterly results. |
| Negotiation | Non-contention (9); four notions | Separate interests from egos. Ask what the other party needs, not just what they demand. |
| Corporate identity | Composite whole (30); not seen by marks (5, 26) | Don’t defend the brand at the cost of truth. Recall and repair rather than spin. |
| Generosity | Dāna (4); four lines vs. treasure (8, 11) | Share knowledge (open training, mentoring, open-source contributions) as well as money. |

### A business case to read critically: Geshe Michael Roach

- **The book.**  *The Diamond Cutter: The Buddha on Managing Your Business and Your Life*  (Doubleday, 2000; later editions with Christie McNally). It is the most prominent explicit application of the Diamond Sutra to business.
- **The company.**  Roach helped found the jewelry firm Andin International in 1981 and worked there for many years; Diamond World names Ofer and Aya Azrielant as Andin’s founders. In Roach’s own words in *The Diamond Cutter* , “The business was started with a $50,000 loan and only three or four employees, including myself.” Roach’s Diamond Silk Entrepreneur University says the firm “went from zero to US$250 million in annual sales” and was sold to Warren Buffett in 2009, becoming part of his Richline Jewelry Group. The sales figure is self-reported; the trade publication JCK independently confirms that Andin joined Berkshire Hathaway’s Richline Group.
- **His interpretation.**  Roach reads the sutra (via Tibetan sources, including Chone Lama Drakpa Shedrup’s commentary) as teaching that “empty” situations are shaped by mental “imprints” from one’s own past conduct, so ethical behaviour causes business success.
- **Cautions:**
    - (a) Roach claims the sutra was originally aimed at businesspeople. Nothing in the text supports this; its audience is monks and bodhisattvas at Jetavana.
    - (b) His karmic-causation reading is a distinctive, prosperity-flavoured interpretation that many teachers would dispute.
    - (c) Roach’s later career involved serious controversies around his Diamond Mountain retreat centre. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Roach)
    - **Use:**  take what is sound (honesty, generosity, seeing situations as open rather than fixed) and treat the “it will make you rich” frame skeptically. The sutra itself says bodhisattvas do *not*  covet merit (28).

### Business reflection

> If our company’s “marks” — logo, revenue, ranking — vanished, what would remain that deserves to exist?

## 4.4 Marriage and Intimate Partnership

**[Mostly reasoned from text, with Thich Nhat Hanh’s interbeing as a direct source]**

### Core principles

- **“My partner is not my partner, therefore I call them my partner.”**  The relationship is real, committed, and precious. But your partner is not reducible to your notion of them, and the relationship has no fixed essence that excuses you from tending it.
- **Impermanence and renewal (sections 18, 32).**  The person you married is not the person in front of you. Past mind, present mind, and future mind cannot be grasped.
- **Letting go of the ledger (sections 4, 19, 28).**  Giving without keeping score.
- **Patience (section 14).**  Conflict met without defending a fixed self-image.

### Applications

1. **Seeing the partner freshly.**
    - *Practice:*  once a week, ask your partner a question you assume you know the answer to (“What are you looking forward to?”). Listen as if you don’t know.
    - Section 5: don’t see them only by their “marks” (habits, roles).
2. **The ledger.**  Much marital resentment lives in an unspoken account book: “I did the dishes three times, you did them once.” The sutra uses the language of merit only to dissolve the ledger.
    - *Practice:*  for one week, do one helpful thing daily without mentioning it or mentally recording it.
    - **Important caution:**  this does not mean tolerating chronic unfairness. If labour is structurally unequal, *talk about it honestly*  (the true speaker) rather than “non-attaching” to it silently.
3. **Conflict and Kṣāntivādin.**  In a fight, ask: “What self-image of mine is being cut right now — ‘I’m the reasonable one’? ‘I’m unappreciated’?” Naming it often drops the heat. Patience here means not retaliating from wounded self-image. It does not mean submitting to contempt.
4. **Non-abiding love vs. clinging.**
    - Possessiveness is “abiding” in a notion of ownership.
    - Non-abiding love wants the partner’s flourishing even when that flourishing does not centre you.
5. **The great danger: non-attachment as emotional unavailability.**  Welwood’s “spiritual bypassing” is especially relevant. Saying “I’m not attached” to avoid vulnerability, conflict, or commitment is not the Diamond Sutra; it is abiding in a self-image of being above it all. The sutra’s bodhisattva is radically *engaged* .
6. **Forgiveness.**  Section 25: the Buddha does not label anyone permanently “bad.” Forgiveness means releasing the frozen notion of the person, not denying the harm. Trust can be rebuilt gradually, and boundaries remain.
7. **Growing together.**  “No coming, no going” (section 29): relationships change form. Treat anniversaries as moments to ask, “Who are we now?”

### Couple’s exercise: the four notions in our relationship

Each partner answers separately, then shares:

- **Self:**  “The role I insist on playing is…”
- **Person:**  “The ‘type’ I’ve put you in is…”
- **Being:**  “Where I treat us as two competing units is…”
- **Life-span:**  “The timeline I’m holding the relationship to is…”

## 4.5 Parenting

**[Reasoned from text; Thich Nhat Hanh’s interbeing and the raft are directly relevant]**

### Core principles

- **No fixed labels.**  A child is “not ‘the shy one’, therefore called shy.” Labels describe moments, not essences.
- **Giving without expecting gratitude (section 4).**
- **The raft (section 6).**  Parenting itself is a raft. Your role must change as the child crosses into adulthood.
- **Patience under provocation (section 14).**
- **“My child” (the four notions).**  Love fiercely without making the child an extension of your self-image.

### Applications

1. **Labels.**  Children internalize labels as identities (“the smart one,” “the difficult one”).
    - *Practice:*  replace trait labels with descriptions of actions: “you worked hard on that” rather than “you’re so smart.”
    - **Study note:**  this also fits widely discussed educational research on praising effort, but don’t overclaim the research; the point here is the sutra’s anti-essentialism.
2. **Generosity without expected return.**  Parental giving is the clearest everyday example of “giving without abiding.” Notice when “after everything I’ve done for you” arises. That is a ledger.
3. **Letting go as children grow.**  “Dharma should be abandoned, how much more non-dharma.” Rules and supports suited to a seven-year-old are rafts to put down at fifteen. Each developmental stage asks you to let go of a method that worked.
4. **Patience under provocation.**  When a toddler hits or a teenager sneers, Kṣāntivādin’s insight applies: much of the heat comes from a wounded parental self-image (“I’m being disrespected”). *Practice:*  one breath, then name the self-image (“I’m a failure as a parent”), then respond to the child’s actual need.
5. **Grades, achievements, and outcomes.**  Model effort without clinging: “Did you learn something? What would you try differently?” Avoid making your child’s results your own merit (section 28).
6. **Supporting a child’s suffering.**  When your child is in pain, the notion of “*my*  child” can turn your anxiety into the centre of the situation. Non-abiding presence means staying with *their*  experience: “That sounds really hard. I’m here.” Fixing comes second.
7. **Teaching generosity.**  Let children give anonymously sometimes (an unsigned note, a donation) and talk about how it felt. That is the three wheels, in kid-size form.

### Parent reflection

> Which of my child’s “marks” (grades, behaviour, appearance) do I treat as the whole child?

## 4.6 Emotional Well-Being

### Core principles

- **The self as construct.**  Relief from a rigid self-image (the four notions; section 31).
- **The three unobtainable minds (section 18).**  Rumination lives in past mind and anxiety in future mind; neither can be grasped.
- **The similes (section 32).**  Impermanence as both grief and freedom.
- **Kṣāntivādin (section 14).**  Anger understood rather than suppressed.
- **Praise and blame (sections 16, 28).**
- **The non-nihilism warning (section 27).**

### Applications

| State | Sutra resource | Practice | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Anxiety** | Section 18 | Notice “future mind” stories. Label them: “a future thought, not perceivable.” Return to sensation (feet, breath). | Chronic anxiety disorders benefit from evidence-based therapy. |
| **Rumination and regret** | Section 18 | “Past mind cannot be grasped”: the memory is a present event. Ask what action, if any, is needed now. | Trauma memories need trauma-informed care, not forced “letting go.” |
| **Grief** | Sections 29 and 32 | Contemplate the similes slowly: “like dew.” Let tenderness, not just loss, arise. Thich Nhat Hanh’s “no coming, no going” can console. | Grief is not a mistake to correct. The similes describe impermanence; they do not instruct you to stop mourning. |
| **Anger** | Section 14 | Ask: “Which self-image is being cut?” | Anger can signal real injustice. Act on it skillfully. |
| **Shame and perfectionism** | Sections 9, 22, 28 | “No attainment” and “no merit received” loosen the need to *be*  perfect. Practise “good enough, offered freely.” | Severe shame may need therapeutic support. |
| **Praise addiction / blame sensitivity** | Sections 16 and 28 | Keep a short “praise and blame” log for a week. Notice how both are weather. | — |
| **Depression** | Section 27 (anti-nihilism) | “Nothing matters” is a symptom, not insight. The sutra itself rejects annihilation and urges wholesome action (23). | **Depression is a medical condition. Seek professional help.**  Do not use emptiness teachings to interpret depressive flatness as awakening. |
| **Equanimity vs. suppression** | Section 10 (“give rise to the mind”) | Check: am I *feeling and not grasping* , or *not feeling* ? The first is equanimity; the second is suppression. | Persistent numbness or depersonalization needs evaluation. |

### Meditative practices derived from the sutra

1. **Four-notions reflection (10–15 minutes).**
    - Sit, settle the breath.
    - Bring up a current difficulty.
    - Ask in turn: *What “self” is involved? What “person/type”? What separate “being”? What “life-span” or timeline?*
    - After each, rest: notice the notion as a notion.
2. **Giving meditation (dāna).**
    - Visualize giving something specific to someone.
    - Then look for the giver, the gift, and the recipient as fixed entities. Notice that each is made of conditions.
    - Rest in the giving without the three “wheels.”
    - Then actually give something today.
3. **Contemplating the nine similes.**
    - One simile per day for nine days: star, fault of vision, lamp, illusion, dew, bubble, dream, lightning, cloud.
    - For each, recall an experience that fits.
    - Journal what changes when you see it that way.
4. **Recitation or chanting.**
    - Recite section 32’s verse (Kumārajīva’s Chinese, or an English rendering) or the whole sutra (about 30–40 minutes).
    - Treat it as concentration practice: attention to each word.
    - Traditional practitioners regard it as merit-making; secular practitioners can treat it as training attention.
5. **“Where does the mind abide?” inquiry.**
    - In any moment of strong emotion, ask: *Where is the mind standing?*
    - Then ask: *Can I find the mind that is standing there?*  (This is the Deshan question, internalized.)

### When professional help is needed

Seek qualified help (a physician, psychologist, or licensed therapist) for any of the following:

- persistent low mood, hopelessness, or thoughts of self-harm;
- trauma symptoms such as flashbacks or dissociation;
- panic or anxiety that disrupts daily life;
- substance dependence;
- any situation involving abuse.

Contemplative practice can complement treatment but should not replace it. Intensive meditation on “no-self” can sometimes destabilize people with certain conditions, so discuss it with a clinician and an experienced teacher.

## 4.7 Interpersonal Dynamics and Community

### Core principles

- **The four notions as the root of “us vs. them.”**  Thich Nhat Hanh calls them “the crown of discrimination and fear and hate.”
- **Non-contention (araṇā; section 9).**
- **No fixed views (sections 7, 31).**
- **True speech (section 14).**
- **No one labelled permanently bad (section 25).**

### Applications

1. **Us vs. them.**  Map a group you feel opposed to. Apply the formula: “They are not ‘them’; therefore they are called ‘them’.” The category is useful but not an essence. Seek out one individual story that complicates it.
2. **Listening without fixed views.**  In disagreement, first summarize the other’s view until they say “yes, that’s it.” Only then respond. This is non-contention in practice: you don’t need to win to be secure.
3. **True speech (section 14).**  Before speaking, check whether it is *real*  (factual), *true*  (honest in intent), and *as things are*  (not distorted to win). And is it non-deceiving and consistent with what I said before?
4. **Conflict, gossip, and blame.**
    - Gossip is usually abiding in a “mark” of someone (section 5): one trait made into the whole person.
    - Blame is often about protecting “self.”
    - *Practice:*  for one week, say nothing about anyone not present that you wouldn’t say in front of them.
5. **Compassion for difficult people.**  Section 25: the Buddha does not regard anyone as an “ordinary person” in the dismissive sense. Wish the difficult person well, *and*  keep appropriate boundaries.
6. **Giving time, attention, and credit.**  Give credit publicly and specifically. Give attention fully (phone away). Give time without signalling sacrifice.
7. **The bodhisattva ideal in friendship.**  Show up reliably, help without making the friend a project, and celebrate their wins without comparison.
8. **Social media disputes.**
    - Before replying, ask which of the four notions is driving the reply.
    - Ask whether the reply will reduce suffering or just defend a self-image.
    - Often, araṇā means not replying.

### Community reflection

> Whom did I “see by marks” this week — and what did I miss?

| H | 26–28 | Marks, non-annihilation, and merit | “Seek me in form…”; not annihilation; not receiving merit |
| I | 29–32 | Final vision | No coming or going; composite whole; no views; **dream, bubble, dew, lightning** |

**Why does the second half repeat the first?**

- **Conze (philological view).**  At section 17 the sutra “veers back to its beginning”; sections 17a–g correspond closely to sections 3, 7, 10, and 14. This suggests a composite or orally expanded text. Conze’s abbreviated version even omitted much of the second half as repetitive.
- **The Chinese “doubts” view.**  Following Vasubandhu and later Hanshan Deqing’s *Resolving Doubts* , Chinese commentators read the sutra as anticipating and answering successive doubts, moving from coarser to subtler attachments. A common reading: the first half frees one from clinging to *objects and practices* ; the second from clinging to the *self as practitioner or bodhisattva* . Some teachers trace the core teaching to the first five sections and treat the rest as answers to doubts.
- **Oral-pedagogical view.**  Repetition is normal in Buddhist oral literature. Re-hearing a teaching at a deeper level is the method. As one Zen centre’s study notes put it, thinking of the second half as “add-on” “may reflect our own lack of understanding.”
- **Study recommendation.**  Read the first half twice before reading the second half. Then notice what has changed in *you*  when you meet the same questions again.

## 5.8 Annotated Further Reading

### Primary translations

- **F. Max Müller (trans.), “The Vagrakkhedikâ or Diamond-Cutter,” in *Buddhist Mahâyâna Texts* , Sacred Books of the East 49 (Oxford, 1894).**  Public domain; on sacred-texts.com. Victorian diction, based on Müller’s 1881 edition from later manuscripts. Good for quotation; dated in places.
- **William Gemmell (trans.), *The Diamond Sutra (Chin-kang-ching), or Prajna-Paramita*  (1912).**  From Kumārajīva’s Chinese, with notes comparing Müller. Public domain, on Project Gutenberg.
- **Edward Conze, *Buddhist Wisdom Books: The Diamond Sutra and the Heart Sutra*  (Allen & Unwin, 1958)** , and his Sanskrit edition (Rome, 1957; 2nd ed. 1974). A landmark, with line-by-line commentary. Use with awareness of Schopen’s and Harrison’s critiques.
- **Gregory Schopen, “The Manuscript of the *Vajracchedikā*  Found at Gilgit,” in L. O. Gómez and J. A. Silk (eds.), *Studies in the Literature of the Great Vehicle*  (1989).**  Scholarly translation of the Gilgit text.
- **Paul Harrison, “*Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā* : A New English Translation of the Sanskrit Text Based on Two Manuscripts from Greater Gandhāra,” in *Buddhist Manuscripts in the Schøyen Collection*  III (Hermes, 2006).**  **The most reliable translation of the early Sanskrit.**  The text and translation are online via the University of Oslo’s Bibliotheca Polyglotta.
- **Red Pine, *The Diamond Sutra: The Perfection of Wisdom*  (Counterpoint, 2001).**  Readable translation plus rich selections from Indian and Chinese commentators. Excellent for study groups.
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, *The Diamond That Cuts Through Illusion*  (Parallax, 1992).**  Translation plus warm practical commentary. Best for application, not history.
- **Mu Soeng, *The Diamond Sutra: Transforming the Way We Perceive the World*  (Wisdom, 2000).**  Translation plus a historical survey and psychological and scientific framing. Its science comparisons are analogical.
- **A. F. Price and Wong Mou-lam, *The Diamond Sutra and the Sutra of Hui-neng*  (Shambhala Classics, 2005).**  Classic paired edition.
- **84000, *The Sūtra on the Perfection of Wisdom “The Diamond Cutter”*  (Toh 16).**  Free online translation from the Tibetan.
- **Alex Johnson, *Diamond Sutra*  (2019).**  A composite drawn from 15 translations. Smooth to read; not a critical text.

### Classical commentaries available in English

- **Hanshan Deqing, *The Diamond Cutter of Doubts* ,**  trans. Charles Luk in *Ch’an and Zen Teaching* , First Series.
- **“Huineng,” commentary in Thomas Cleary, *The Sutra of Hui-neng, Grand Master of Zen: With Hui-neng’s Commentary on the Diamond Sutra*  (Shambhala, 1998).**  Treat the attribution as traditional, not historical (see 1.9).

## Caveats

- **Dates are approximate and contested.**  Composition (2nd–5th century), Kumārajīva’s translation (401, 402, or 403), and Deshan’s dates (some sources give 780/782–865, others 819–914) all vary by source.
- **Legends are legends.**  Huineng’s awakening, the verse contest, Deshan’s book-burning, and the Huineng commentary are best treated as sacred narrative and tradition, not verified history.
- **The Yogācāra three-natures mapping (3.1)**  is a common schematization. Check it against Asaṅga’s verses before attributing it to him.
- **The Western comparisons are analogies,**  most loosely with Kant.
- **The applied sections marked [reasoned from text]**  are interpretive applications, not claims about what any classical commentator said about modern workplaces or families.
- **Sheng Yen and Hsing Yun titles:**  confirm current English editions before purchase.
- **Translation differences matter.**  Kumārajīva’s Chinese (the version chanted in East Asia and quoted by Huineng) differs from the Sanskrit in wording, length (e.g., six vs. nine similes), and some content (e.g., the missing second verse in section 26). When quoting, say which version you are using.
- **Mental health.**  Contemplative practice complements but does not replace professional care.
