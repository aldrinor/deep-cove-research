# Who Is Liable When Driver and Machine Share the Wheel? Liability Allocation for ADAS Crashes (SAE Levels 2–3), October 2026

Under current U.S. law, the human driver of a Level 2 system remains the legal "driver" and absorbs most of the fault for a crash, but the manufacturer is now demonstrably exposed to shared liability, including punitive damages, when its design makes foreseeable misuse easy and its marketing invites over-reliance; *Benavides v. Tesla* (driver 67%, Tesla 33%, $242.57 million judgment, now on appeal)\[1\] is the template.\[1\] The law shifts much more sharply at Level 3, where the system is the driver within its operational design domain. The doctrinal gap that most needs closing is the supervised Level 2 zone, where engineering, human-factors evidence and marketing all pull against the legal fiction that a "supervising" human is fully in control.

**Scope and defaults.** No jurisdiction was specified, so this report anchors the analysis in U.S. federal and state law and draws comparatively on the EU, UK, Germany and UN ECE/WP.29, with brief notes on Japan and China. It focuses on SAE Levels 2 and 3, using Level 4/5 driverless operation as a contrast. Status is current as of mid-October 2026; open matters are flagged.

## TL;DR

- **Level 2: shared fault, with the driver bearing the majority but the OEM no longer immune.** The driver is the legal operator and is criminally and civilly responsible. But juries and regulators now treat weak driver monitoring, permissive operational design domain (ODD) limits and "self-driving" marketing as design and warning defects. The Benavides verdict, the California DMV's December 2025 finding that "Autopilot"/"FSD" marketing was misleading, and NHTSA's 2023 Autopilot recall all point the same way.
- **Level 3: responsibility flips to the system, but only inside the ODD and outside the takeover window.** Germany (StVG §§1a–1c), the UK (AEVA 2018 / AV Act 2024), and UN R157 put the system or its insurer in the driver's seat when it is engaged. The open question is the handover: how many seconds a distracted user needs, and who bears the risk in that interval. Human-factors evidence says the window is seconds to tens of seconds, not the instantaneous takeover the law sometimes assumes.
- **What to do:** adopt a federal Level 2 driver-monitoring and ODD-enforcement standard; require a standardized, accessible ADAS data recorder with an adverse presumption when the OEM controls missing data; enact a statutory "operator" definition keyed to SAE level and engagement state, with a manufacturer-responsibility presumption at Level 3+ and a takeover grace period; restrict "self-driving" marketing for Level 2 (UK and China models); and adopt insurer-pays-first compensation with subrogation (UK AEVA model).

## Key Findings

1. **The Level 2/Level 3 "cliff" is the single most important liability variable.** At Level 2, SAE J3016 assigns object and event detection and response (OEDR) supervision to the human at all times;\[2\] at Level 3, the automated driving system (ADS) performs the entire dynamic driving task (DDT) within its ODD, and the human is a "fallback-ready user." Law in Germany, the UK and several U.S. states now tracks this distinction; general tort law largely does not, and leaves Level 2 crashes to ordinary negligence plus product liability.
2. **Juries will split fault rather than choose between driver and machine.** *Benavides* (S.D. Fla., verdict August 1, 2025) found Tesla liable on design defect and failure to warn, allocated 33% to Tesla and 67% to the driver, and awarded $200 million in punitive damages not reduced by the fault split.\[1\]\[3\]\[4\] Judge Beth Bloom denied Tesla's post-trial motions on February 19, 2026; the appeal (11th Cir. No. 26-10858) is in briefing, with Tesla's reply due November 6, 2026.\[1\]
3. **Earlier defense wins show the "driver was warned" defense still works where the driver is the plaintiff and misuse is stark.** *Hsu v. Tesla* (April 21, 2023) and *Molander v. Tesla* (October 31, 2023, 9–3) were defense verdicts.\[5\]\[6\]\[7\] *Benavides* differed because the victims were third parties, the evidence focused on ODD permissiveness and Elon Musk's public statements, and there were data-handling disputes.\[8\]
4. **Regulators have converged on driver engagement and naming as defect theories.** NHTSA's EA22-002 led to recall 23V-838 (2,031,220 vehicles, December 2023) for insufficient controls against misuse.\[9\] Recall query RQ24-009 then questioned the remedy, and FSD investigations followed: PE25-012 on traffic-law violations (~2.88 million vehicles) and EA26-002 on degraded visibility, which NHTSA opened on March 18, 2026 covering 3,203,754 vehicles (2016–2026 Model S/X, 2017–2026 Model 3, 2020–2026 Model Y, 2023–2026 Cybertruck). The California DMV found the "Autopilot" and "Full Self-Driving Capability" names misleading.\[10\]\[11\] The UK's ban on marketing driver assistance as self-driving takes full effect January 7, 2027, and China's MIIT banned "autonomous driving"-style terms for L2 in April 2025.\[12\]\[13\]
5. **Federal law has no Level 2 performance standard, and NTSB recommendations remain largely unimplemented.** NHTSA still has no method to verify that Level 2 systems are limited to their ODD (H-17-38, "Open—Unacceptable Response").\[14\] In March 2026 the NTSB superseded its 2020 driver-monitoring recommendations with H-26-04, calling for driver monitoring on all new Level 2 vehicles, after two fatal Ford BlueCruise crashes.\[15\] Federal policy under the 2025 AV Framework has meanwhile moved toward deregulation and lighter crash reporting.\[16\]\[17\]\[18\]
6. **Information asymmetry is the hidden driver of outcomes.** OEMs hold the telemetry, and the 2025 amendment to NHTSA's Standing General Order (SGO) reduced Level 2 reporting.\[17\]\[19\] The EU's new Product Liability Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2853, transposition deadline December 9, 2026) is the first major regime to answer this with court-ordered disclosure and presumptions of defect and causation.\[20\]\[21\]

## Details

### 1. Technical principles that determine legal responsibility

**SAE J3016 vocabulary as legal infrastructure.** The DDT comprises sustained lateral and longitudinal control plus OEDR.\[2\] At Levels 1–2, the system executes part of the control task while the human performs OEDR and supervises. At Level 3, the ADS performs the full DDT within its ODD (the roads, speeds, weather and times it was designed for). It must issue a timely takeover request when it reaches ODD limits or detects a system failure, and the fallback-ready user must respond. At Level 4, the system performs its own DDT fallback, achieving a minimal risk condition, typically by stopping safely, without any human. Legally, **the identity of the entity performing OEDR is the closest technical analog to "who was driving."** At Level 2 it is always the human; at Level 3, it is the system until a takeover request has been issued and a reasonable interval has elapsed.

**Perception architecture and failure modes.** Most OEMs fuse cameras with radar, and in many Level 3 systems also with lidar, so each sensor covers the others' blind spots. Tesla moved to camera-only "Tesla Vision" in 2021.\[22\] NHTSA's EA26-002 (upgraded March 18, 2026 from PE24-031) focuses on whether FSD's "degradation detection system" fails to detect glare, fog and airborne dust and fails to warn drivers in time.\[23\]\[24\] NHTSA's own Information Request EA26002-01 (July 2, 2026, addressed to Tesla's Eddie Gates, response due August 12, 2026) confirms that Tesla had submitted, in response to an earlier PE24-031 request, an internal document titled "Radar Saves Us," which NHTSA said "appears particularly relevant"; this is an investigative request, not a defect finding, but it shows how sensor-architecture choices become discoverable risk-utility evidence. Recurring failure modes define what is foreseeable: stationary objects (the Mountain View crash attenuator, parked emergency vehicles, the parked SUV in *Benavides*), crossing tractor-trailers (Williston 2016, Delray Beach 2019), phantom braking, lane-marking ambiguity at gore areas, out-of-distribution edge cases, and traffic-control errors (PE25-012 documents FSD proceeding through red lights and entering opposing lanes).\[2\]\[25\]\[26\]

**Driver monitoring.** Torque-based hand detection infers engagement from small steering inputs. It is easily defeated and measures hands, not eyes or minds; the NTSB found in its Mountain View report that measuring steering-wheel torque is an ineffective proxy for engagement.\[2\] Infrared camera-based gaze and head-pose monitoring, used in GM Super Cruise and Ford BlueCruise, is better but not sufficient: the NTSB's March 2026 BlueCruise investigation of two fatal 2024 crashes (San Antonio and Philadelphia) shows that camera-monitored hands-free systems still produce fatalities when drivers are inattentive and the system cannot detect stationary vehicles.\[27\] IIHS's 2024 safeguard ratings credited Super Cruise as the only system to meet every emergency-procedure requirement, including locking the driver out for the rest of the drive after an unresponsive stop.\[28\] Tesla's 23V-838 remedy added a one-week Autopilot suspension after repeated forced disengagements.\[29\]

**Human factors: why "supervision" is an unstable legal premise.**
- **Bainbridge's "ironies of automation" (1983):** automating the easy parts leaves humans the hardest task, monitoring for rare failures, which humans do poorly.
- **Vigilance decrement and automation complacency:** the NTSB has repeatedly named driver over-reliance and inattention as probable-cause factors in the Williston, Mountain View and Delray Beach crashes and in the Uber Tempe crash.\[2\]\[15\]
- **Takeover times:** Eriksson and Stanton (2017, *Human Factors*) reviewed 25 studies and found mean takeover times of about 2.96 s, with reported times from about 1.14 s up to 15 s.\[30\] In their own non-critical-transition experiment, drivers took considerably longer and showed much larger variance when engaged in secondary tasks, with individual times ranging up to roughly 25 seconds.\[31\] A later meta-analysis of 129 studies (Zhang et al., 2019) confirmed that takeover time scales with the time budget offered and with secondary-task engagement.\[30\]\[32\] Distributions are right-skewed, so a time budget set at the mean leaves a substantial minority of drivers short.\[33\] Any rule treating the driver as liable the instant a takeover request issues is therefore empirically unjustifiable.
- **Misuse under partial automation:** IIHS's first partial-automation safeguard ratings (March 2024) found only 1 of 14 systems "acceptable" (Lexus Teammate), 2 "marginal," and 11 "poor," including both Tesla systems and BlueCruise.\[28\] IIHS President David Harkey said on March 12, 2024 that some drivers may find partial automation makes long drives easier "but there is little evidence it makes driving safer," and a July 2024 IIHS-HLDI follow-up characterized partial automation as "a convenience feature like power windows or heated seats rather than a safety technology." An IIHS/MIT AgeLab four-week field trial (Reagan et al., Volvo S90 Pilot Assist, 10 drivers) found that after a month of use drivers were more than three times as likely to show signs of disengagement, and 17 times as likely to take both hands off the wheel, as at the start.

**Data and evidence infrastructure.** EDRs under 49 CFR Part 563 capture a short pre-crash window of basic dynamics, not ADAS engagement state, takeover requests or driver-monitoring status. That richer information sits in proprietary OEM logs and telematics. In closing EA22-002, NHTSA recorded that gaps in Tesla's telematic data create uncertainty about actual Autopilot crash rates and that Tesla is not aware of every crash, even severe ones.\[19\] The SGO (2021-01) requires named OEMs and operators to report ADS and Level 2 crashes.\[34\] The Third Amended SGO (issued April 24, 2025, effective June 16, 2025) consolidated reporting into a five-day window, eliminated duplicate reports, and narrowed Level 2 reporting to the most serious crashes (fatality, hospital-treated injury, vulnerable road user, airbag deployment).\[17\]\[35\]\[36\] Internationally, UN R157 requires a data storage system for automated driving (DSSAD) for Level 3 ALKS, and the R171 amendments add in-service monitoring and occurrence reporting for Level 2 DCAS.\[37\]

**Naming and HMI as legal facts.** A name sets user expectations, and expectations define foreseeable use. In *Benavides*, the court admitted Musk's public statements and Tesla's 2016 "Paint It Black" video, which stated the person in the driver's seat was "only there for legal reasons."\[8\]\[38\]\[39\] The California DMV's administrative law judge found Tesla's naming "follows a long but unlawful tradition of intentionally using ambiguity to mislead consumers,"\[40\] and called "Full Self-Driving" "actually, unambiguously false."\[41\]

### 2. Existing legal frameworks

#### United States: federal

**Authority and the missing standard.** NHTSA regulates through the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act (49 U.S.C. Chapter 301) via two levers: Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS) and defect/recall authority. No FMVSS governs Level 2 performance, driver monitoring, or ODD enforcement, so NHTSA regulates ADAS almost entirely ex post, through the Office of Defects Investigation.\[2\]

**The Tesla enforcement arc.**
- **PE21-020 → EA22-002:** this investigation reviewed 956 crashes. It found Autopilot's controls "may be insufficient to prevent crashes due to lack of driver engagement" and identified a pattern of frontal crashes "avoidable by attentive drivers."\[9\]\[19\]\[42\] It closed in April 2024.\[43\]
- **Recall 23V-838 (December 12, 2023; 2,031,220 vehicles):** an over-the-air remedy that strengthened alerts, increased monitoring after engagement, simplified activation, and introduced a one-week suspension after repeated forced disengagements.\[9\]\[29\] Tesla filed the recall while stating it did not concur with NHTSA's analysis.\[44\]
- **RQ24-009 (opened April 2024):** this query assesses the remedy's adequacy, citing post-remedy crashes, preliminary VRTC test results, opt-in elements, and the fact that Autopilot can still be engaged off-highway.\[29\]\[44\]\[45\] No public closure was located as of October 2026; treat it as open.
- **PE25-012 (opened October 7, 2025):** this investigation covers approximately 2,882,566 FSD-equipped vehicles over red-light running and wrong-direction lane use; NHTSA's information request issued December 3, 2025.\[46\]\[47\]
- **EA26-002 (March 18, 2026):** this investigation covers FSD in degraded visibility across 3,203,754 vehicles (2016–2026 Model S/X, 2017–2026 Model 3, 2020–2026 Model Y, 2023–2026 Cybertruck), with nine crashes including one fatality; NHTSA defines the alleged defect as "failure of the FSD's degradation detection system... to detect a degraded state and to warn the driver appropriately."

Opening an investigation is not a defect finding.\[1\] But these investigations are powerful evidence in civil litigation, and the 23V-838 recall effectively concedes that the pre-recall engagement controls created an unreasonable safety risk.

**Deregulatory counter-current.** The April 2025 AV Framework paired the lighter SGO with expanded exemption pathways for AVs, and a July 31, 2026 Federal Register notice (91 FR 48485) issued interim guidance on commercial deployment exemptions.\[16\]\[36\] The federal trajectory is toward enabling Level 4 deployment, not toward a Level 2 safety standard.

**Preemption.** *Geier v. American Honda* (2000) held that a state tort claim conflicted with FMVSS 208 because the standard deliberately preserved a mix of options. *Williamson v. Mazda* (2011) narrowed *Geier*: manufacturer choice alone does not imply preemption absent a significant federal regulatory objective. Because no FMVSS addresses Level 2 driver monitoring or ODD limits, conflict preemption of ADAS design-defect claims is weak today; recalls and investigations are not "standards" with preemptive force, and the Safety Act's savings clause (49 U.S.C. §30103(e)) preserves common-law liability. A future federal ADAS standard could change this, which is why preemption language must be drafted deliberately.

#### United States: state tort law

**Negligence.** The driver of a Level 2 vehicle owes ordinary care, and using Autopilot does not dilute that duty. The manufacturer may also be negligent in design, warnings, testing or post-sale conduct. Comparative-fault regimes then determine how much each party pays:
- Texas applies modified comparative responsibility with a 51% bar (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code ch. 33). A claimant more than 50% responsible recovers nothing, and joint and several liability attaches only to a defendant more than 50% responsible.
- California (*Li v. Yellow Cab*, 1975) applies pure comparative fault. Florida historically did too, but its 2023 tort reform (HB 837) moved general negligence to a modified 51% bar; practitioners should check how the new rule applies to a given product claim.
- *Benavides* applied Florida's apportionment. The plaintiffs were third parties, so no claimant fault was at issue; the allocation ran between Tesla and the non-party/settling driver George McGee.\[1\]\[8\]

**Strict product liability.**
- *Design defect:* California uses both the consumer-expectations test and the risk-utility test with burden shifting (*Barker v. Lull*, 1978; *Soule v. GM*, 1994). Many states follow Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability §2(b), which requires a reasonable alternative design. For ADAS, plausible alternative designs are concrete and already in the market: camera-based driver monitoring, geofencing to mapped controlled-access highways (Super Cruise, BlueCruise), and stronger escalation and lockout. That makes §2(b) claims unusually tractable.
- *Foreseeable misuse:* Restatement (Third) §2 cmt. m and §17 treat reasonably foreseeable misuse as relevant to defect, not as an automatic defense. That is the doctrinal bridge from "the driver was inattentive" to "the design made inattention easy." NHTSA's EA22-002 closing language, "foreseeable driver misuse of the system played an apparent role," is effectively an agency finding that misuse was foreseeable.\[19\]
- *Failure to warn and marketing defect:* warnings buried in manuals and click-through screens may be inadequate if contradicted by naming and executive statements. *Benavides* went to the jury on precisely this theory.\[8\]\[39\]
- *Manufacturing defect* is rarely the right frame for software; *Benavides* dismissed it on summary judgment, as it did negligent misrepresentation.\[4\]\[39\]
- *Post-sale duties and OTA:* Restatement (Third) §10 recognizes a post-sale duty to warn, and §11 recognizes a duty to recall only when a statute or regulation requires one or the seller voluntarily undertakes one. OTA capability arguably expands what is "reasonable" post-sale: if a fix can be pushed overnight, delay is harder to defend. Each software version is effectively a new design decision, which complicates "time of sale" defect analysis.
- *Economic-loss rule:* this bars pure economic loss in tort, so consumer claims over overpaying for FSD proceed in contract and consumer-protection law.

**Punitive damages.** Florida requires conduct equivalent to criminal manslaughter for punitive damages in products cases.\[48\] In *Tesla v. Banner* (Fla. 4th DCA, February 26, 2025), the court reversed an order allowing the estate of Jeremy Banner (Delray Beach 2019, crossing semi-trailer) to plead punitive damages. It held Tesla "cannot be liable for failing to provide technology that it did not advertise and that did not exist," and that the estate had not shown Tesla knew its Level 2 features were likely to cause death.\[48\]\[49\] Months later, a federal jury in *Benavides*, applying the same state's law, awarded $200 million in punitive damages.\[1\]\[3\] This tension is a central issue in Tesla's Eleventh Circuit appeal, along with the argument that Florida's statutory cap (generally three times compensatory damages) was exceeded.\[1\]\[4\]\[50\]

#### United States: state AV statutes

- **Texas.** Transportation Code ch. 545, Subchapter J (SB 2205, 2017) treats the owner of an automated motor vehicle as the operator when the ADS is engaged and preempts local AV regulation. SB 2807 (2025; effective September 1, 2025; enforcement from May 28, 2026) requires TxDMV authorization for commercial operation of automated vehicles without a human driver. Applicants must certify traffic-law compliance, a recording device, the ability to achieve a minimal risk condition, insurance, and a first-responder interaction plan; TxDMV may suspend or restrict authorizations.\[51\]\[52\]\[53\]\[54\] Both statutes target driverless operation and do not reallocate Level 2 liability.\[55\]
- **California.** DMV regulations (13 CCR Div. 1, Ch. 1, Art. 3.7 and 3.8) govern AV testing and deployment permits and do not cover Level 2. SB 1398 (2022), codified at Vehicle Code §24011.5, prohibits naming or marketing a partial automation feature in a way that implies autonomy. The DMV's advertising authority under Vehicle Code §11713 underpinned its Tesla action.
- **Florida.** Fla. Stat. §316.85 deems the ADS the operator when engaged, for purposes of traffic law, regardless of whether a person is physically present. This frames Level 3+ engagement as system operation, though the statute does not by itself create manufacturer tort liability.
- **Arizona.** Executive orders (2015, 2018) and later statute (A.R.S. §28-9701 et seq., 2021) authorize driverless operation, and the person who submits the law-enforcement interaction plan bears responsibility for traffic violations.
- **Nevada, Michigan, Tennessee, Georgia, Utah** and others define "operator" or deem the ADS the driver when engaged; several also shield original manufacturers from liability for defects introduced by third-party conversions. **No U.S. state has enacted a liability statute specific to Level 2 driver-assistance crashes**; Level 2 is left to general tort law, and California's naming statute is the main state-level Level 2 intervention. Most AV statutes require the same insurance minimums as human-driven vehicles, with some higher figures for driverless operation.

#### Contract, consumer protection and arbitration

Tesla's on-screen acknowledgments ("keep your hands on the wheel") and owner's-manual warnings are powerful against driver-plaintiffs (*Hsu*).\[5\]\[56\] Against third-party victims they are legally irrelevant, though they remain relevant to the driver's comparative fault. In the consumer class action *In re Tesla Advanced Driver Assistance Systems Litigation* (N.D. Cal. No. 3:22-cv-05240, Judge Rita Lin), the court compelled four of five original plaintiffs to arbitration in September 2023. In August 2025 it certified classes limited to California purchasers and lessees who paid separately for FSD. The Ninth Circuit heard argument on the certification appeal (No. 25-7927) on August 31, 2026, and no decision had issued as of late September 2026; the district case is stayed.\[1\] (The task's premise of a "2024 denial of class certification" could not be verified.) A further proposed class action, *Waller v. Tesla*, was filed in June 2026.\[1\]

#### Insurance

Auto liability insurance pays first for the driver's negligence; the insurer may then pursue product-liability subrogation against the OEM, which depends on access to logs. Tesla Insurance prices partly on telematics "Safety Score" data, giving the OEM-insurer a uniquely informed position on the very data contested in litigation, a structural conflict regulators have not addressed. Scholarship proposes alternatives: RAND's *Autonomous Vehicle Technology: A Guide for Policymakers* (Anderson et al., 2014/2016) discusses no-fault and first-party options, and Abraham and Rabin propose "Manufacturer Enterprise Responsibility" (MER), a no-fault, administratively scheduled regime charging manufacturers for injuries involving highly automated vehicles (*Virginia Law Review*, 2019). IIHS's position is empirical: as its president put it in March 2024, there is "little evidence it makes driving safer," so safeguards should be mandatory.

#### Criminal law

- **Kevin George Aziz Riad (Gardena, December 29, 2019):** with Autopilot engaged, his Model S exited a freeway at 74 mph, ran a red light and killed two people in a Honda Civic.\[57\]\[58\] Charged with vehicular manslaughter with gross negligence, the first U.S. felony prosecution of a driver using a widely available Level 2 system, he pleaded no contest in June 2023 (not 2022, as sometimes stated) and was sentenced to two years' probation plus house arrest and community service.\[57\]\[59\]\[60\]\[61\]
- **Rafaela Vasquez (Uber ATG, Tempe, March 18, 2018):** she was the safety driver of a test vehicle that killed Elaine Herzberg.\[62\]\[63\] On March 4, 2019, the Yavapai County Attorney (handling the matter because of a Maricopa conflict) found "no basis for criminal liability for the Uber corporation."\[64\] Vasquez was charged with negligent homicide, pleaded guilty to endangerment on July 28, 2023, and received three years' supervised probation.\[59\]\[63\]\[65\]\[66\]
- **Manufacturers:** no OEM has been criminally charged over a Level 2 crash. Tesla disclosed in its FY2022 Form 10-K that it had received requests from the DOJ for documents related to Autopilot and FSD, adding that "to our knowledge no government agency in any ongoing investigation has concluded that any wrongdoing occurred"; no charges have followed to date. The only AV-company criminal resolution is Cruise's November 2024 deferred prosecution agreement ($500,000) for a false report to NHTSA, a candor offense, not a homicide theory.\[67\]\[68\]

The pattern is stark: **criminal law reaches the supervising human, while the corporation faces civil, regulatory and reporting-integrity liability.**

#### European Union

- **Revised Product Liability Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2853).** In force December 8, 2024; transposition deadline December 9, 2026; applies to products placed on the market after that date, with the 1985 Directive continuing for earlier products.\[69\]\[70\]
  - It defines "product" to include software and AI systems.\[20\]
  - It treats a manufacturer as retaining control, and therefore liability, over defects caused by software updates, or by failure to supply safety-necessary updates within its control (Art. 11(2)(c)).\[70\]
  - It empowers courts to order disclosure of evidence (Art. 9).\[20\]
  - It presumes defectiveness where the defendant fails to disclose, the product violates mandatory safety requirements, or there is an obvious malfunction, and presumes defect and/or causation where technical complexity makes proof excessively difficult (Art. 10).\[21\]\[71\]
  - For ADAS crashes in the EU, this is the single most consequential change, because it reverses the information asymmetry that dominates U.S. litigation.
- **AI Liability Directive (COM(2022) 496)** was withdrawn in the Commission's 2025 work programme (announced February 2025), leaving fault-based AI liability to national law plus the PLD.
- **AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689).** Vehicle AI safety components fall within Annex I sector legislation (type-approval); AI Act requirements are integrated through future type-approval delegated acts, not applied directly.
- **Motor Insurance Directive** (2009/103/EC, as amended by 2021/2118): compulsory insurance covers the vehicle regardless of who or what was driving, so victims are compensated by the insurer first.
- **Type-approval.** Regulation (EU) 2019/2144 (General Safety Regulation) mandates ADAS features and driver drowsiness and distraction warnings. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/1426 covers ADS for fully automated vehicles in small series. UN regulations apply via the 1958 Agreement:
  - **R79** (steering, ACSF categories);
  - **R157** (ALKS, Level 3; 2022 amendments raised the ceiling to 130 km/h and allowed lane changes; requires DSSAD);
  - **R171** (DCAS, Level 2; adopted March 2024, in force September 2024).\[72\] Its 01 series (adopted March 2025, in force September 2025) permits system-initiated manoeuvres on highways and withholding of hands-on requests where driver monitoring shows visual engagement.\[73\]\[74\]\[75\] Its 02 series (adopted June 2026, expected in force around January 2027) extends system-initiated manoeuvres to non-highway environments and adds safety-management, in-service monitoring and occurrence-reporting duties.\[37\]\[75\] Contracting parties warned during GRVA deliberations that these extensions risk making DCAS "too close to ADS,"\[37\] which restates the Level 2 liability problem in regulatory form;
  - **R155/R156** (cybersecurity and software-update management systems).

#### Germany

- **StVG §§1a–1c (2017)** authorize "highly and fully automated" driving functions (Level 3). The driver may turn away from traffic but must remain "wahrnehmungsbereit" (ready to perceive), and must retake control without undue delay when the system requests it or when the driver recognizes, or must recognize, that conditions for intended use no longer apply.
- **StVG §§1d–1l (Autonomous Driving Act, 2021)** authorize Level 4 in defined operating areas, supervised remotely by a "Technische Aufsicht" (technical supervisor).
- **Liability is anchored in strict keeper liability (Halterhaftung, StVG §7)** backed by compulsory insurance, with caps raised for automated functions (§12, doubled for vehicles using automated functions). Victims recover from the keeper's insurer without proving fault, and the insurer pursues the manufacturer under product liability law.
- **§63a requires data storage** of position and time whenever control changes between driver and system, and on takeover requests or malfunctions, with access rights for authorities and for parties in accident claims.
- **Mercedes-Benz Drive Pilot** received the world's first UN R157 Level 3 type approval from the KBA in December 2021 (up to 60 km/h); the KBA approved 95 km/h on motorways in December 2024.\[76\]\[77\] Mercedes' widely reported "acceptance of liability" is narrower than headlines suggest: the company said it would accept responsibility for crashes caused by a fault in its technology, but not where the driver "failed to comply with their duty of care."\[78\]\[79\] Legally this is largely a restatement of product liability exposure, not an assumption of all tort liability.\[78\] Unlike NHTSA's self-certification plus ex post enforcement, the KBA approves systems ex ante.

#### United Kingdom

- **Automated and Electric Vehicles Act 2018.** Under s.2, where an accident is caused by an automated vehicle "driving itself," the insurer is directly liable to the victim, including the insured. Under s.3, contributory negligence reduces recovery, and the insurer is not liable if the injured person allowed the vehicle to drive itself when it was inappropriate. Under s.4, insurers may exclude or limit liability for unauthorized software alterations or failure to install safety-critical updates. Under s.5, the insurer can recover from any other party liable, such as the manufacturer.
- **The Law Commissions' joint report (January 2022)** proposed the "user-in-charge" (immune from dynamic-driving offences while the ADS is engaged but responsible for non-dynamic duties such as insurance and loads), "no-user-in-charge" (NUIC) operation, and the "authorised self-driving entity" (ASDE), which is responsible for the system's driving.
- **The Automated Vehicles Act 2024** (Royal Assent May 20, 2024)\[80\] enacted these concepts. It also created a statutory distinction between self-driving and driver assistance, and Part 4 offences for marketing unauthorized vehicles in terms likely to confuse users into believing they can drive themselves.\[79\]\[81\] Part 4 comes fully into force on January 7, 2027 (SI 2026/731).\[12\] The government targets full implementation in the second half of 2027.\[81\]\[82\]
- **Ford BlueCruise** became the first hands-off Level 2 system approved in Great Britain (April 2023), on designated motorway "Blue Zones." It remains driver assistance: the driver is fully responsible.\[83\]

#### Japan and China (brief)

- **Japan** amended the Road Traffic Act and Road Transport Vehicle Act (effective 2020) to permit Level 3. Honda's Legend received the world's first Level 3 type approval (Traffic Jam Pilot, November 2020; sales March 2021), and 2023 amendments permit licensed Level 4 operation in designated areas.
- **China.** The Shenzhen intelligent connected vehicle regulation (effective August 2022) allocates liability to the driver where a driver is present (including Level 3) and to the owner or manager for driverless vehicles, with recourse against manufacturers. After the fatal Xiaomi SU7 crash in March 2025, MIIT convened automakers on April 16, 2025, barred terms such as "autonomous driving," "self-driving" and "intelligent driving" for Level 2 marketing, restricted public beta programs, and required OTA updates affecting assisted driving to be filed, with emergency updates subject to recall procedures.\[13\]\[84\]\[85\]\[86\] MIIT opened a draft mandatory national standard, "Safety Requirements for Combined Driving Assistance Systems," for comment on September 17, 2025.\[87\]

**NTSB recommendations.** HAR-17/02 (Williston), HAR-20/01 (Mountain View, adopted February 25, 2020) and HAR-20/02 (Delray Beach) found that torque-based monitoring is ineffective and that, unless Level 2 is restricted to its ODD, out-of-ODD use is foreseeable and crashes will continue.\[2\]\[106\] Recommendations went to NHTSA (H-17-38: develop a method to verify ODD limitation; H-20-3/4: driver-monitoring standards and mandate) and to manufacturers (H-17-41: build ODD safeguards).\[100\] NHTSA said it had no current plans and saw no practical way to verify ODD limitation; H-17-38 remains "Open—Unacceptable Response."\[14\]\[107\] On March 31, 2026, following its BlueCruise investigation, the NTSB superseded H-20-3/4 with H-26-04 (require driver monitoring on all new Level 2-capable vehicles), and superseded its crash-data recommendations with H-26-03, classified "Open—Unacceptable Response" on issuance.\[15\]\[27\]

**Discovery, preemption, arbitration, and non-U.S. decisions.** In *Benavides*, Tesla's post-trial motion attacked the admission of vehicle data-related evidence,\[38\] and plaintiffs' allegations that Tesla mishandled or withheld crash-snapshot data were a theme at trial;\[41\] these remain allegations, not adjudicated spoliation sanctions as far as the public record shows. No reported decision has held Level 2 design claims preempted by NHTSA action. Arbitration has been enforced against consumer-purchaser plaintiffs but cannot bind third-party crash victims. No German Bundesgerichtshof, UK AEVA s.2, Chinese, Australian or Canadian appellate decision squarely allocating Level 2/3 crash liability was identified; German practice resolves such cases through §7 keeper liability and insurer recourse.

### 4. Systematic framework: the boundaries of responsibility

**Dimension-by-dimension analysis.**

1. **Automation level and the "driver" definition.** Level 2 places the DDT legally on the human, so the OEM's exposure comes through product defect, not through being the "driver." At Level 3, statutes (StVG, AEVA/AVA, Fla. §316.85 for traffic law) make the system the operator when engaged within the ODD. Liability then shifts to the keeper's insurer and the manufacturer, and the human's exposure shrinks to failing to respond to a takeover request or misusing the system outside its ODD.
2. **ODD compliance.** The most powerful lever in Level 2 litigation is whether the system *permitted* engagement outside its ODD. A system that can be activated on surface streets with cross-traffic (as in *Benavides* and Gardena), when the OEM's own manual says it is designed for controlled-access highways, invites a reasonable-alternative-design argument (geofencing). Conversely, a geofenced system shifts more of the residual risk back to the driver.
3. **System-initiated vs. driver-initiated transitions.** If the system issues a takeover request, liability during the transition should depend on the adequacy of the time budget and warning; empirical takeover times of several seconds up to tens of seconds argue for a defined grace period. If the driver disengages voluntarily, as by steering override (NHTSA found most such crashes occurred within 5 seconds of a torque override), the driver bears primary responsibility, subject to the design question of whether the system "silently" dropped lane centering, as IIHS criticized.\[19\]\[42\]
4. **Driver monitoring and engagement enforcement.** Under Restatement (Third) §2, foreseeable misuse is a design input. Torque-only monitoring, long hands-off intervals and no lockout are design choices that juries and NHTSA have treated as defects.
5. **Marketing and representations.** Executive statements and product names shape "ordinary consumer expectations" and the adequacy of warnings. The California DMV finding and *Benavides*' admission of Musk statements show courts treating marketing as part of the product.
6. **Software version and the temporal dimension.** Liability attaches to the version running at crash time. Under the EU PLD the manufacturer remains liable for update-related defects and for failing to update; under AEVA s.4 the owner who fails to install safety-critical updates may lose insurance cover. U.S. law has no clear rule on owner refusal; comparative fault is the default tool.
7. **Data access and evidentiary burdens.** Whoever holds the logs controls the narrative. The EU's presumptions and German §63a access rights are the models; U.S. plaintiffs depend on discovery and spoliation doctrine.
8. **Multi-party allocation.** Driver, owner or keeper, OEM, suppliers and software vendors, mapping providers, road authorities (Caltrans' unrepaired attenuator in Mountain View), fleet operators, aftermarket modifiers, and the victim's own comparative fault.\[2\]
9. **Criminal versus civil.** Mens rea for vehicular manslaughter (gross negligence) is met where a driver ignores the road for an extended period; good-faith reliance on a system marketed as autonomous may mitigate but has not excused. Corporate criminal law has reached only reporting candor (Cruise).

**Responsibility matrix.**

| Scenario | Level 2 hands-on (e.g., Autopilot, FSD Supervised) | Level 2 hands-off, camera-DMS, geofenced (Super Cruise, BlueCruise) | Level 3 engaged in ODD (Drive Pilot, Honda TJP, BMW Personal Pilot) | Level 4 driverless (Waymo, Zoox; Cruise contrast) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary perception failure in ODD; driver attentive | Driver primary; OEM design/warning claim plausible if alternative design existed | Driver primary; OEM exposure higher if DMS showed engagement yet no time to react | **OEM/keeper-insurer primary**; user not at fault | **Operator/OEM** |
| Driver inattentive, system did not detect or enforce | Shared: driver majority, OEM share for weak DMS (Benavides 67/33) | Driver majority; OEM share reduced if DMS escalated properly | User not required to watch road; OEM/insurer primary | N/A |
| System engaged outside ODD because the system permitted it | **Shared, OEM share rises** (geofencing as reasonable alternative design; NTSB H-17-41) | Rare (geofenced); if it occurs, OEM-heavy | System should refuse; if permitted, OEM primary; if user forced misuse, user | Operator/OEM |
| Takeover request issued; crash within a short window | N/A (no TOR concept; driver always responsible) | Driver, unless warning inadequate | **Grace period: OEM/insurer**; after a reasonable window, user if unresponsive | MRC is system's duty |
| Driver-initiated override/disengagement precedes crash | Driver primary (design issue if lane centering drops silently) | Driver primary | User (now driving) | N/A |
| Owner declined safety update / modified system | Driver/owner share increases | Same | AEVA s.4-type exclusion; insurer recourse | Operator |
| Marketing implied autonomy | OEM failure-to-warn/marketing-defect share increases; consumer-protection exposure | Moderate | Accurate marketing expected | Accurate |
| Criminal exposure | Driver (Riad) | Driver | User largely immune for dynamic driving (UK AVA; StVG duties narrowed) | Entity: regulatory/reporting crimes (Cruise DPA) |

**Doctrinal gaps.**
- *Negligence assumes a human actor.* There is no "reasonable algorithm" standard; plaintiffs must attack design via product liability rather than ask whether the system "drove negligently." Scholars have proposed judging the ADS's driving against a human-driver negligence benchmark: Geistfeld ("A Roadmap for Autonomous Vehicles," *California Law Review*, 2017), Gurney ("Sue My Car Not Me," 2013) and Bryant Walker Smith ("Automated Driving and Product Liability," *Michigan State Law Review*, 2017).
- *Product liability assumes a static product.* OTA updates make the product a stream of versions.
- *The "reasonable driver" under supervised automation* is held to a vigilance standard that the human-factors literature says humans cannot sustain.

Taking a position: for Level 2, the best fit is **strict-liability-style product claims plus mandatory design standards, with comparative fault preserved**. For Level 3+, it is **insurer-pays-first with manufacturer subrogation and a statutory presumption of system responsibility when engaged within the ODD**, rather than Abraham and Rabin's full no-fault MER.

## Recommendations

**Federal regulator (NHTSA / Congress)**
1. **Level 2 driver-engagement FMVSS.** *Problem:* torque-only monitoring and weak escalation make misuse foreseeable, and there is no federal floor. *Mechanism:* require camera-based gaze/head monitoring, defined alert timing, escalation to a controlled stop, and lockouts after repeated non-compliance, benchmarked to UN R171 and IIHS safeguard criteria. *Trade-offs:* privacy (cabin cameras), cost, and possible preemption of state design claims. *Precedent:* NTSB H-26-04; R171; Tesla recall 23V-838. Include an express **savings clause** so compliance is a floor, not a tort shield.
2. **ODD enforcement for Level 2.** *Mechanism:* require that systems marketed for controlled-access highways cannot engage elsewhere, verified by map or geofence, and that OEMs document the ODD to NHTSA. *Trade-off:* reduces convenience. *Precedent:* NTSB H-17-38/H-17-41; Super Cruise and BlueCruise geofencing.
3. **ADAS/ADS data recorder standard (extend 49 CFR Part 563).** *Mechanism:* mandatory, tamper-evident recording of engagement state, takeover requests, DMS status, alerts, software version and sensor-health flags, for at least 30 seconds pre-crash, with standardized retention and neutral, court-supervised access for crash parties, insurers and NHTSA. *Precedent:* StVG §63a, R157 DSSAD, NTSB H-26-03.
4. **Restore and strengthen SGO reporting for Level 2**, including software version and DMS status, and publish de-identified rates with exposure denominators so that "safer than human" claims can be tested.

**State legislatures**
5. **Statutory "operator" definition keyed to SAE level and engagement state.** At Level 3+, while engaged within the ODD, the ADS is the operator. Create a **rebuttable presumption that the manufacturer is responsible** for crashes during engagement, and a **takeover grace period** (for example, at least 10 seconds, adjustable by regulation as evidence accumulates) during which the user is not liable. At Level 2 the human remains the operator, but **manufacturer comparative fault is expressly recognized** where the system permitted out-of-ODD use or lacked compliant monitoring. *Precedent:* UK AVA user-in-charge immunity; StVG §1b.
6. **Conditional safe harbor.** OEMs that comply with the federal monitoring/ODD standard and data-recorder rules receive a cap on punitive damages, not on compensatory liability; non-compliance triggers a presumption of design defect.
7. **Marketing restrictions.** Extend California Vehicle Code §24011.5 nationally or by uniform act: no "autopilot," "self-driving," "autonomous" or "full self-driving" for Level 2, and executive statements count as advertising. *Precedent:* UK AVA Part 4 (from January 7, 2027); China MIIT (April 2025).
8. **Owner update duties.** Require OEMs to notify owners of safety-relevant OTA updates. Allow insurers to reduce cover for owners who unreasonably refuse safety-critical updates (AEVA s.4 model), while keeping the manufacturer liable where the update was not offered or was defective (EU PLD Art. 11).

**Courts**
9. **Evidentiary burden-shifting.** Where the OEM controls crash data and fails to produce it, apply an adverse-inference or defect presumption modeled on EU PLD Arts. 9–10, and treat telemetry as presumptively discoverable under protective orders.
10. **Apply Restatement (Third) foreseeable-misuse doctrine explicitly** to engagement design, and treat NHTSA recalls and investigative findings as admissible (not conclusive) evidence of foreseeability.

**Manufacturers**
11. Ship geofenced, camera-monitored Level 2 by default; design cooperative steering so driver input does not silently cancel lane centering; publish ODD, disengagement and crash-rate data; align naming with function. Where accepting Level 3 responsibility, state it in contract terms, not press releases, defining exactly when responsibility transfers and returns.

**Insurers**
12. Adopt **insurer-pays-first** for crashes with automation engaged, with subrogation against OEMs supported by guaranteed data access (UK AEVA model). For OEM-affiliated insurers (e.g., Tesla Insurance), impose data-neutrality rules so that telematics are available to adverse claimants on equal terms.

**Standards bodies (SAE, ISO, UNECE)**
13. Develop measurable driver-monitoring performance standards (NTSB H-20-7 was closed unanswered in 2026).\[27\] Define takeover time budgets using right-tail (for example, 95th-percentile) human data rather than means. Harmonize U.S. requirements with UN R157 and R171, and watch R171's 02-series expansion: the more a Level 2 system does, the more the law should treat it like Level 3.

## Caveats

- **Pending matters can change the picture quickly.** The Benavides appeal (reply due November 6, 2026), the Ninth Circuit class-certification ruling, Tesla's challenge to the DMV order, RQ24-009, PE25-012 and EA26-002 are all open. Investigations are not defect findings, and a jury verdict is not binding precedent.
- **Some reported facts rest on secondary sources.** These include the count of post-Benavides settlements and the Maldonado settlement, and statements about the Banner settlement; the "Radar Saves Us" document request is now confirmed by NHTSA's own July 2, 2026 Information Request EA26002-01. NTSB's March 2026 BlueCruise summary was marked preliminary.\[27\] Statute section numbers and insurance minimums for several states were not individually re-verified for 2026 amendments.
- **The human-factors numbers are setting-dependent.** Takeover times vary with time budget, secondary tasks, and simulator versus road conditions; the proposed 10-second grace period is a policy judgment informed by that literature, not a figure the literature itself establishes.
