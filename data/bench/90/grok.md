Shared human–machine driving does not fit a rule that either “the driver is always responsible until the car is fully driverless” or “the manufacturer is responsible whenever software touched the wheel.” Liability should follow **who actually had the dynamic driving task, and whether the human could still perform the share the system left them**.

## 1. What the technology actually allocates

SAE J3016 (2021) is a role taxonomy, not a safety grade and not a liability code. It splits the dynamic driving task (DDT) into sustained lateral and longitudinal control, object and event detection and response (OEDR), and DDT fallback, inside an operational design domain (ODD).

| Level | Motion control | OEDR | Fallback | Human’s real job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | Human, or one axis only | Human | Human | Driver |
| 2 Partial automation | System, both axes | Human must supply it | Human | Supervising driver |
| 3 Conditional automation | System, entire DDT in ODD | System | Human, after a request to intervene; system must keep driving for at least several seconds | Fallback-ready user, who may look away |
| 4–5 High / full | System | System | System, to a minimal-risk condition | Passenger (remote supervisor only if the design says so) |

The legally unstable band is Level 2 and Level 3. At Level 2 the system steers and speeds for a sustained period but is **not** the OEDR agent. At Level 3 it is the OEDR agent, until it issues a transition demand or hits a failure the fallback user must catch. SAE itself warns that a higher number is not a higher safety level: a poorly monitored Level 2 feature can be more dangerous than a simpler assist, because supervision is a task humans do badly.

Three human-factors facts set the liability boundary:

- **Ironies of automation.** People are poor continuous monitors. Vigilance drops quickly; automation complacency and out-of-the-loop unfamiliarity are predictable, not exotic misuse.
- **Mode confusion.** If the interface and the marketing imply the car “sees” the road, drivers stop supplying OEDR. A torque sensor that checks hands on the wheel does not check eyes or mind.
- **Takeover is not instantaneous.** A request to intervene needs seconds of continued system control plus time to rebuild situation awareness. A hand-back inside the time-to-collision of a hazard the human was induced not to watch is a system failure, not driver negligence.

UNECE R157 (Automated Lane Keeping Systems) already writes some of this into type approval for a narrow Level 3 case: highway lane-keeping, system in primary control, driver monitoring, a transition demand, a minimal-risk manoeuvre, and a data storage system for automated driving. In June 2026, WP.29 adopted a first global regulation for fully driverless automated driving systems, built on a safety case, a safety management system, in-service monitoring, and a standard of at least a competent human driver. Those instruments govern market access. They do not, by themselves, allocate civil damages.

## 2. Why ordinary traffic law misfires

Classic negligence asks whether a human driver kept a proper lookout and reasonable control. Product liability asks whether the vehicle was defective in design, manufacture, or warning. Shared driving breaks both questions in the same accident:

- Control is **time-sliced and partial**. Steering authority can sit with the software while the legal duty to see a crossing pedestrian still sits, on paper, with a person whose gaze the software invited elsewhere.
- The ODD is often a **manual paragraph**, not a geofence. Foreseeable use outside that paragraph is then labelled “driver misuse.”
- Evidence is **asymmetric**. Automation state, sensor confidence, driver-monitoring output, and software version sit with the manufacturer. Victims and drivers get them late, if at all.
- Marketing and the owner’s manual **contradict each other**. The manual says “you must supervise.” The product name and public claims say the car drives.

Treating SAE level badges as the liability rule makes this worse. A feature sold and used as if it performs the whole DDT can still be labelled Level 2 so that the human remains the residual risk-bearer.

## 3. How current law draws the line

**United States.** There is no federal liability statute for ADAS or automated driving. Allocation is state tort law: driver negligence, comparative fault, and product liability (design defect under risk-utility, warning defect, misrepresentation, sometimes a direct negligence theory aimed at the driving behaviour itself). NHTSA’s tools are recalls and defect investigations, not a shift of civil responsibility.

The leading illustration is *Benavides v. Tesla* (S.D. Fla.). In April 2019, near Key Largo, George Brian McGee was using Autopilot, dropped his phone, and did not see that the road was ending at a T-intersection. The car did not stop in time. Naibel Benavides Leon was killed; Dillon Angulo was severely injured. On 1 August 2025 a federal jury assigned **about two-thirds of the fault to the driver and one-third to Tesla**, with roughly $200 million in punitive damages and Tesla’s share of compensatory damages bringing the company’s total near $243 million. The plaintiffs’ theory was not that Autopilot had promised to be a legal driver. It was that Tesla defectively designed a system that could be engaged on a road outside its own described domain, did not adequately police driver engagement, and was marketed in a way that inflated trust. Tesla has said it will appeal; post-trial motions and Florida damages doctrine may change what is actually paid. The verdict still matters: a Level 2 label and an owner’s-manual warning did not, as a matter of fact, exhaust the manufacturer’s share.

Criminal law has cut the other way. In the 2018 Tempe crash, an Uber test vehicle killed Elaine Herzberg. The NTSB found the safety driver’s phone distraction to be the probable cause and also found that Uber’s programme — including disabling the Volvo’s automatic emergency braking and failing to monitor the operator — contributed. Arizona prosecutors charged the operator, Rafaela Vasquez, and **declined to charge Uber**. In 2023 she pleaded guilty to endangerment and received probation. A California driver who left a Tesla Autopilot feature engaged through a fatal red-light crash has likewise faced vehicular manslaughter exposure. Criminal liability is still concentrating on the human even where the safety case was organisationally thin.

Earlier civil experiments point the same direction without a stable ratio. *Nilsson v. General Motors* (N.D. Cal., Cruise) pleaded the automated vehicle’s driving as ordinary negligence, not only as a product defect, and resolved short of a liability rule. Tesla has won or settled other Autopilot suits; *Benavides* is the first major jury allocation against it, not a national standard. NHTSA’s Autopilot investigation, which produced a large 2023 recall, concluded that Autopilot’s controls did not sufficiently ensure attention and appropriate use, and that the feature invited more confidence than those controls could support. A later Full Self-Driving probe has focused on degraded visibility (sun glare, dust, fog): cases in which the system did not detect the degradation, or alerted too late for a driver to respond, and lost or never acquired the lead vehicle.

**United Kingdom.** The Automated Vehicles Act 2024 is the clearest statutory split, and it applies only to features the state has **authorised as self-driving** — able to drive safely and legally without being monitored. While an authorised user-in-charge feature is engaged, the person in the seat is not guilty of an offence arising from the manner of driving. They remain the “driver” for other purposes (insurance, condition of the vehicle, not interfering with the system) and must be able to resume after a transition demand. An Authorised Self-Driving Entity, typically the manufacturer or developer, is responsible for the feature continuing to meet the self-driving test. No-user-in-charge operation adds a licensed operator. The Automated and Electric Vehicles Act 2018 already points victims to the insurer first when a listed vehicle is driving itself, with the insurer recovering from the responsible party. Commencement of the 2024 Act is phased. Ordinary ADAS that is **not** authorised stays on the old driver-negligence footing. The Act refuses to treat a hands-off-feeling Level 2 product as self-driving just because the steering wheel is quiet.

**Germany.** The Road Traffic Act keeps two tracks. The holder’s strict liability (*Gefährdungshaftung*) remains the victim’s primary civil path; for highly and fully automated functions the statutory caps were doubled (commonly cited as €10 million for personal injury and €2 million for property damage). For Level 3, the human may turn attention away from the driving task but must stay able to retake control when the system demands it. The 2021 autonomous-driving amendments allow Level 4 only inside an approved operating area, with a technical supervisor (*Technische Aufsicht*) who is **not** the driver: the holder must maintain the system and staff that supervisor; the supervisor must release or refuse alternative manoeuvres, deactivate on demand, and respond when the vehicle reaches a minimal-risk state. Manufacturer duties sit beside that structure; software providers are still reached mainly through product liability and tort, not through a named “computer driver.”

**China (Shenzhen).** The Shenzhen Special Economic Zone intelligent-connected-vehicle regulations, as amended with effect from 1 July 2026, are explicit and more driver-centric than the UK model. If the vehicle is human-driven, **or** if a conditional (Level 3) system is active, and the vehicle’s side is at fault, **the driver compensates**. If a high or full automation system is active, the **owner and manager** compensate. Either payor may then claim against the producer or seller for a product defect under the Product Quality Law and the Civil Code’s strict product-liability rules. Conditional automation therefore does not, in Shenzhen, move manner-of-driving liability off the human merely because the system is performing the DDT.

**European Union product law.** The revised Product Liability Directive treats software and AI-enabled features as products, eases proof where technical complexity is asymmetric, and expands disclosure. An ADAS stack that continues to learn or ship over-the-air updates remains inside the producer’s control. That does not identify the driver–system split in a given crash; it makes the manufacturer’s side of a defect claim easier to prove once the split is litigated.

**Canada**, for context, has endorsed the UNECE safety-case direction but has no UK-style authorisation-and-shift statute. Provincial highway and motor-vehicle negligence, plus ordinary product liability, still do the work. A Level 2 crash in British Columbia would today be argued as driver negligence and, where the pleadings support it, design, warning, and misrepresentation claims against the manufacturer.

## 4. Where the responsibility boundary actually is

A workable boundary has four operational modes, not six SAE labels. Testing, conventional, supervisory, and autonomous (the scheme developed by Widen and Koopman) match how control is exercised.

**Conventional and Level 1.** The human owes ordinary care. A failed feature that was specified to mitigate a crash (automatic emergency braking, forward collision warning) can make the manufacturer liable for the **increment** of harm that feature was supposed to prevent. It does not erase the driver’s duty.

**Supervisory mode (honest Level 2).** The human owes OEDR and must remain able to override, **but only if the design makes that duty performable**. That means: eyes-on driver monitoring that will disengage the feature, an ODD the software actually enforces, and an interface that states supervision rather than autonomy. Where those conditions hold and the driver is looking at a phone, the driver is primarily responsible, including criminally if the departure is gross. Where they do not hold — engagement off-domain, hands-only nag, autonomy branding, a perception miss with no timely alert — the manufacturer shares or bears primary responsibility. *Benavides* is this case: distraction was real, and so was the design that made distraction foreseeable and the domain optional. One-third / two-thirds is a jury finding on those facts, not a tariff.

**Conditional mode (Level 3).** While the system is performing the entire DDT inside its ODD and has not issued a timely transition demand, manner-of-driving responsibility belongs with the system’s sponsor. The human’s duty is receptivity, not continuous scanning. After a demand, there should be a **protected resumption interval**: no contributory negligence for the human during the time a competent person needs to take over, unless that person has already made fallback impossible (intoxication, leaving the seat, defeating the monitor). Events the system hands over too late stay with the system. Shenzhen’s choice to leave conditional-automation fault on the driver is the weaker rule. It preserves a familiar defendant and under-deters ODD and transition design. The UK user-in-charge immunity, limited to authorised features and subject to a real transition duty, matches the task allocation.

**Autonomous mode (Level 4–5 inside the ODD).** The occupant is a passenger. Compensation runs through compulsory insurance and, where the jurisdiction has it, holder or operator strict liability, with recourse to the manufacturer for defects and to the remote supervisor only for breach of defined duties (deactivate, approve an alternative manoeuvre, respond to a minimal-risk stop). Moment-to-moment driving negligence is not a passenger concept. Germany’s technical supervisor and the UK no-user-in-charge operator are the right kind of defendant: role-specific, not an all-purpose “driver” standing in for the software.

**Mixed control in fact.** If a feature labelled Level 2 is executing, and being trusted as, the whole DDT, the fact-finder should be allowed to treat actual control as higher than the badge. The designation is evidence. It is not conclusive.

Third parties should not fund the uncertainty. The German holder action and the UK insurer-first model get this right: pay the victim on a strict or near-strict basis, then allocate among driver, holder, operator, and manufacturer. The American pattern — full fault trial, comparative percentages, punitive exposure, multi-year appeal — tells the truth about shared blame slowly, and only for plaintiffs who can finance the record.

## 5. Regulatory recommendations

1. **Legislate control states, not SAE slogans.** Define conventional, supervisory, conditional, and autonomous modes by who must perform OEDR and fallback at the moment of the crash. Liability follows the mode actually engaged and whether its preconditions were met.
2. **Force a binary choice on sustained lateral-plus-longitudinal control.** Either the feature is constrained driver assistance — camera driver monitoring, software ODD lockout, no autonomy branding, driver primary — or it is authorised self-driving under a safety case, and manner-of-driving liability moves to a named entity (the UK ASDE). The present middle, in which a consumer system is legally Level 2 and practically trusted as Level 3, is the defect.
3. **Make ODD enforcement a design obligation.** A reasonable alternative design is to refuse engagement, or to degrade safely, outside the domain. Leaving domain compliance to the driver, after inviting hands-off use, should weigh heavily toward defect. That is the theory that succeeded in *Benavides*.
4. **Set a human-factors floor.** For any supervisory feature: eyes-on monitoring, escalation, and deactivation. Hands-on torque is not enough; NHTSA’s Autopilot findings already say so. For any conditional feature: a quantified transition window during which the system continues the DDT or executes a minimal-risk manoeuvre, consistent with R157.
5. **Mandate a crash-locked driving-automation recorder.** Mode, ODD status, driver-monitor state, transition-demand time, sensor-confidence flags, and software version should be available to police, insurers, and claimants under a disclosure rule modelled on the revised EU Product Liability Directive. Allocation without that record is guesswork.
6. **Treat public capability claims as part of the warning.** A manual that says “supervise” does not cure advertising that says the car drives. Names such as “Autopilot” or “Full Self-Driving” should be unavailable for features that are not authorised self-driving.
7. **Compensate victims first, then allocate.** Compulsory motor insurance should respond to ADAS and ADS crashes without a preliminary verdict on the human–machine split, with subrogation against the manufacturer or authorised entity. Strict holder or operator liability, with published caps, is appropriate for authorised high automation. Caps should **not** cover deception, deliberate non-enforcement of the ODD, or disabled safety monitors. Punitive damages remain justified for that narrower set, which is what the Miami jury was reacting to.
8. **Impose a post-sale software duty.** A known perception failure (stopped emergency vehicles, sun-glare loss of the lead vehicle, late alerts) is a continuing defect until remedied by recall or update. The version on the vehicle at impact is the product.
9. **Recalibrate criminal law to the assigned role.** Gross inattention by a person who accepted a real supervisory or fallback duty can be criminal, as in Tempe. An attentive supervisor hit by a sub-second perception miss generally should not be. Organisations that disable a safety system or run a test programme they know cannot support the residual human role should be chargeable. The Tempe split — operator convicted, company not — is the outcome to stop repeating.
10. **Do not shift liability until the safety case exists.** The 2026 UNECE automated-driving regulation’s competent-driver equivalence, safety management system, and in-service monitoring are the right **precondition** for treating the system as the driver. Below that bar, do not pretend the human has been relieved. Above it, do not pretend the human was still driving.

The boundary, stated short, is this: **responsibility tracks controllable authority**. A driver who looks away from a system that truthfully requires eyes-on supervision owns that choice. A manufacturer that designs, names, and leaves unrestricted a system people cannot supervise in the way the law assumes owns the crash that design makes likely. A conditional or high-automation system that has taken the DDT owns the driving until it gives the human a fair, timely, and recorded chance to take it back.
