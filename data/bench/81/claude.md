# Usable Pasts: How Contemporary Politics Is Reinterpreting History (2015–2026)

The central finding of this analysis is that the past is no longer argued over mainly by historians: across very different regimes, states, parties and social movements now use law, curricula, museums, monuments and anniversaries to manage historical meaning. Between 2024 and 2026 the dominant trend shifted from *recognition* of previously silenced victims toward *state-directed restoration* of heroic national narratives, and this shift now reaches established democracies as well as authoritarian states.

## TL;DR

- **Ideologisation and instrumentalisation are converging globally.** Russia criminalises "rehabilitation of Nazism" and, since 2025–2026, denial of a "genocide of the Soviet people"; China's Patriotic Education Law (in force 1 January 2024) writes the fight against "historical nihilism" into law; India's NCERT textbooks (2025–26) have been rewritten; and in the United States Executive Orders 14253 (March 2025) and 14416 (July 2026) put the federal government directly into Smithsonian and National Park Service interpretation.
- **Reclamation of silenced narratives is real but contested and reversible.** Truth commissions, restitution (Germany's 2022 pledge to return 1,130 Benin Bronzes; the Netherlands' return of 119 in June 2025), apologies and renamings have widened whose past counts. Yet Germany's 2021 Namibia declaration remains unratified, Argentina's Milei government has cut memory policy, and the US reversed all nine Confederate base renamings in 2025.
- **Commemoration does not just reflect historiography — it drives it.** Monuments, holidays and anniversaries (Victory Day 2025, Spain's 50 years since Franco's death in 2025, the US Semiquincentennial in 2026) set research agendas, provoke counter-memories and invite legal regulation. The responsible response is neither neutral "history for its own sake" nor activist memory. It is transparent, evidence-bound pluralism that resists both state orthodoxy and new orthodoxies of reclamation.

## Introduction

"The past is a foreign country," wrote L. P. Hartley, and David Lowenthal turned the line into a programme. In *The Past Is a Foreign Country* (1985) and *The Heritage Crusade and the Spoils of History* (1996/1998), he argued that modern societies constantly remake the past to meet present needs. In his account heritage is not bad history. It is a different activity altogether: it celebrates, takes possession of and exaggerates the past. That distinction has become politically explosive. In the 2020s, debates once confined to seminar rooms (whether slavery was central to the American founding, whether Poles were complicit in the Holocaust, whether Ukraine is a "real" nation, whether the Mughals were foreign oppressors) are settled or suppressed by statute, executive order and textbook boards.

This essay looks at four linked processes: the *ideologisation* of history, meaning the imposition of doctrinal frames; the *instrumentalisation* of the past for legitimacy, mobilisation and geopolitics; the *reclamation* of silenced narratives; and the role of *commemorative practice* in shaping what historians write and publics believe. It draws on cases from Europe, Asia, Africa and the Americas, and it argues that these processes are not opposites. They share techniques and constantly feed into one another.

## 1. Conceptual Framing: History, Memory, Heritage, Commemoration

**Collective memory.** Maurice Halbwachs (*Les cadres sociaux de la mémoire*, 1925; *La mémoire collective*, 1950) established that remembering is socially framed: groups supply the "frameworks" through which individuals recall, and memory is rebuilt in the present rather than retrieved intact. Jeffrey Olick ("Collective Memory: The Two Cultures," 1999) later distinguished "collected" memory (aggregated individual recollection) from "collective" memory (public symbols, rituals and institutions). Most political conflict concerns the second.

**Memory versus history.** Pierre Nora's *Les Lieux de mémoire* (1984–1992) and his essay "Between Memory and History" (1989) cast history as critical, analytical and secular, and memory as lived, affective and absolute. In his view, *lieux de mémoire* arise because living memory (*milieux de mémoire*) has disappeared. Paul Ricoeur (*Memory, History, Forgetting*, 2000) softened this binary: history depends on testimony and therefore on memory, and both carry an ethical "duty" of just memory that guards against both too much and too little remembering. Ricoeur's warning against "manipulated memory" and "commanded forgetting" (amnesties) remains among the most useful diagnostic tools for memory laws.

**Cultural and communicative memory.** Jan Assmann (*Das kulturelle Gedächtnis*, 1992) and Aleida Assmann (*Erinnerungsräume*, 1999) distinguished *communicative* memory, which is informal and spans roughly three generations, from *cultural* memory, which is institutionalised in texts, rites and monuments. The distinction explains why the 2020s are so turbulent: the last eyewitnesses of the Second World War, of decolonisation and of the Franco dictatorship are dying. As communicative memory passes into cultural memory, the question of who controls the archive, the curriculum and the monument becomes decisive.

**Invented traditions and imagined communities.** Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger's *The Invention of Tradition* (1983) showed that many "ancient" rituals are modern products of nation-building. Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983) explained how print capitalism made it possible to imagine a nation as a "deep, horizontal comradeship" extending back in time. Duncan Bell's notion of the "mythscape" (2003) adds that national myths are a terrain of constant contest rather than a settled story.

**Why the past is contested.** Reinhart Koselleck (*Futures Past*, 1979) argued that modernity widened the gap between the "space of experience" and the "horizon of expectation". When the future looks uncertain, the past becomes a resource for orientation. Jörn Rüsen's theory of historical consciousness makes the same point from didactics: people need narratives that tie past, present and future together to act at all. Hayden White (*Metahistory*, 1973) added the uncomfortable corollary that historical writing itself uses narrative emplotment (romance, tragedy, comedy, satire). If that is so, the line between "history" and "ideological narrative" is a matter of degree and discipline, not of kind.

Taken together, these theories suggest a working definition. *History* is the evidence-bound, revisable reconstruction of the past. *Memory* is the socially framed, identity-bearing use of it. *Heritage* is its curated possession. *Commemoration* is its ritual performance. Politics enters wherever the last three claim the authority of the first.

## 2. The Ideologisation of History

Ideologisation means imposing a doctrinal frame that defines which interpretations are legitimate, enforced through law, education and censorship. Nikolay Koposov's *Memory Laws, Memory Wars: The Politics of the Past in Europe and Russia* (2017) traces a key shift. Western European memory laws began as bans on Holocaust denial, which protected victims. Many Eastern European and Russian laws, by contrast, protect the *nation's* image. Koposov calls the second type a weaponisation of memory against minorities and neighbours.

**Russia: from memory law to wartime doctrine.** Article 354.1 of the Russian Criminal Code, introduced by Federal Law No. 128-FZ of 5 May 2014 (weeks after the annexation of Crimea), criminalises "rehabilitation of Nazism", including spreading "knowingly false information" about the USSR's activities during the Second World War. One of its first targets, blogger Vladimir Luzgin, was fined 200,000 rubles for reposting a text saying that the USSR and Germany jointly invaded Poland in 1939. Critics such as Jacob Mchangama noted that the statement was essentially factually correct.\[1\] In April 2022, weeks into the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, a new administrative offence (Article 13.48) banned equating the aims and actions of the USSR and Nazi Germany, and denying the "decisive role of the Soviet people" and the "humanitarian mission of the USSR" in liberating Europe.\[2\] The human-rights group OVD-Info counted 212 prosecutions under the rehabilitation-of-Nazism provisions initiated since 2022.\[3\] Federal Law No. 74-FZ of 21 April 2025 then codified a "genocide of the Soviet people" during the Great Patriotic War,\[4\] and the Berlin-based Centre for East European and International Studies (ZOiS) reports that denial of this genocide became a criminal offence in 2026. ZOiS argues that the move borrows the victim vocabulary of Holocaust remembrance while erasing Soviet perpetrator roles, notably the 1939 invasion of Poland and Katyn.\[5\] The liquidation of Memorial, Russia's leading historical-memory NGO, by the Supreme Court in December 2021 completed the turn: the state now holds a near monopoly on twentieth-century memory.

**China: "historical nihilism" as legal category.** Under Xi Jinping, "historical nihilism" (*lishi xuwuzhuyi*) labels any account that departs from the Communist Party's line on its own history. The 2018 Law on the Protection of Heroes and Martyrs made smearing the Party's canon of heroes punishable.\[6\] The Patriotic Education Law, passed on 24 October 2023 and in force since 1 January 2024, requires that patriotic education "permeate the entire course of school education". It turns museums and memorial sites into patriotic education venues and obliges internet providers to produce patriotic content.\[7\] Its Article 37 forbids "distorting, smearing, desecrating, or negating" heroes and martyrs and denying "wars of aggression" or massacres, aimed mainly at Japanese wartime denialism. Legal scholars at NYU's U.S.-Asia Law Institute call the statute "a manifesto masquerading as legislation".\[8\] The underlying narrative of the "century of humiliation" (1839–1949) and national "rejuvenation" is a classic case of what Eviatar Zerubavel (*Time Maps*, 2003) calls a plot structure of decline and redemption.

**India: textbooks as battleground.** India shows ideologisation through curriculum rather than criminal law. NCERT's new social science textbooks for 2025–26 (*Exploring Society: India and Beyond*) moved the Delhi Sultanate and Mughals from Class 7 to Class 8. They add a note on the "darker periods" of history, describe Babur, Akbar and Aurangzeb with emphasis on brutality and religious intolerance, drop sections on Mughal decline, the Marathas, Tipu Sultan and the Anglo-Mysore Wars, and label Mir Jafar a "traitor".\[9\]\[10\]\[11\] Michel Danino, who heads NCERT's social science curricular group, defends the changes on the grounds that Indian history "cannot be sanitised".\[11\] Romila Thapar responds that removing entire dynasties is "nonsense" because history is a continuous process.\[12\] The episode illustrates a recurring pattern: a "corrective" to an earlier orthodoxy (here, a Nehruvian-secular textbook tradition) is presented as de-ideologisation while installing a new majoritarian frame.

**Poland and Hungary: protecting the "good name".** In January 2018 Poland's Law and Justice majority amended the Act on the Institute of National Remembrance (IPN) to penalise attributing to the "Polish Nation or Polish State" responsibility for Nazi crimes "contrary to the facts", with up to three years' imprisonment.\[13\] After protests from Israel, the US and historians, the criminal penalties were dropped in June 2018 and the provision became a civil one.\[14\] The chilling effect continued through civil litigation: in February 2021 a Warsaw court ordered Holocaust historians Barbara Engelking and Jan Grabowski to apologise over one paragraph of their 1,600-page *Night Without End* (*Dalej jest noc*).\[15\] The suit was backed by the Polish League Against Defamation, and the ruling was later overturned on appeal.\[16\] In Hungary, the House of Terror museum (opened 2002) and the 2014 Liberty Square memorial cast Hungary chiefly as a victim of foreign occupations. Jelena Subotić (*Yellow Star, Red Star*, 2019) analyses such cases as the "appropriation" of Holocaust memory by post-communist states, which use its vocabulary to tell stories of national suffering under communism while marginalising local complicity.

**Ukraine: memory as national defence.** Ukraine's 2015 "decommunisation" laws, which banned Soviet and Nazi symbols and honoured independence fighters including controversial OUN–UPA figures, drew an April 2015 open letter, initiated by David R. Marples and signed by about 70 scholars including Omer Bartov, John-Paul Himka and Per Anders Rudling, which urged President Poroshenko not to sign them and declared that "we regard these laws with the deepest foreboding." After 2022, the 2023 law "On the Condemnation and Prohibition of Propaganda of Russian Imperial Policy in Ukraine and the Decolonisation of Toponymy", signed on 21 April 2023 and in force from 27 July 2023, moved from decommunisation to decolonisation.\[17\]\[18\] In 2022 alone, 9,859 place names were changed and 145 monuments removed.\[18\] Monuments to Pushkin and Prince Vorontsov in Odesa were taken off the national heritage register in 2023 and dismantled.\[17\]\[19\] Scholarship in *Nationalities Papers* notes the ambivalence: in Odesa, "decolonisation" largely means derussification of a city whose identity was itself shaped by empire.\[20\] Ukraine shows that ideologisation is not only an authoritarian vice. Under existential threat, democracies also legislate memory, and Maria Mälksoo's concept of "mnemonical security" (2015) explains why: states treat contested pasts as security issues.

## 3. The Instrumentalisation of the Past

If ideologisation is about *doctrine*, instrumentalisation is about *use*: legitimating rulers, mobilising voters, justifying wars and conducting diplomacy.

**Justifying war.** The clearest recent case is Russia. Putin's July 2021 essay "On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians" and his 21 February 2022 address, which claimed that modern Ukraine was "created by" Bolshevik Russia, served as historical arguments for denying Ukraine's statehood. The invasion itself was framed as "denazification". At the 80th-anniversary Victory Day parade on 9 May 2025, attended by the 29 foreign leaders Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov had told TASS to expect, including Xi Jinping, Brazil's Lula and the only European leaders present, Serbia's Aleksandar Vučić and Slovakia's Robert Fico, Putin declared that "Russia has been and will remain an indestructible barrier against Nazism, Russophobia and anti-Semitism" and praised troops fighting in Ukraine. Kyiv called it a "parade of cynicism". Timothy Snyder (*The Road to Unfreedom*, 2018) describes this as the "politics of eternity": a cyclical story in which the nation is forever an innocent victim and history is a repeating morality play rather than a field of choices.

**Regime legitimation and anniversaries.** Victory Day became, in the AP's description, a key pillar of Putin's 25-year rule.\[21\] China's 3 September 2025 parade for the 80th anniversary of victory over Japan played the same legitimating role. Hobsbawm's "invented traditions" work exactly this way: periodic, highly choreographed rituals that tie current rulers to founding sacrifices.

**Culture wars and electoral mobilisation in the United States.** The American case shows instrumentalisation inside a democracy. The *New York Times*' 1619 Project (August 2019) reframed the founding around slavery. President Trump's 1776 Commission answered with a report issued on 18 January 2021, two days before President Biden rescinded it. In June 2021 Juneteenth became a federal holiday, the first new one since Martin Luther King Jr. Day. The second Trump administration made history policy a governing priority:

- **Executive Order 14253, "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History" (27 March 2025)**, accused the Smithsonian of falling under "a divisive, race-centered ideology" and ordered the removal of content that "inappropriately disparage[s] Americans past or living". The American Historical Association replied that the order "egregiously misrepresents the work of the Smithsonian Institution".\[22\]\[23\]
- On **12 August 2025** the White House announced a "comprehensive internal review" of eight Smithsonian museums, among them the National Museum of African American History and Culture and the National Museum of the American Indian. The review aimed at "alignment with the President's directive to celebrate American exceptionalism" ahead of the 250th anniversary.\[23\]\[24\]
- In **February–June 2025** Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth restored all nine Army base names that a congressionally mandated Naming Commission had changed in 2023, among them Fort Bragg, Fort Hood and Fort Benning. To get around the 2021 statutory ban on Confederate names, each was rededicated to a different soldier with the same surname, such as Pfc. Roland L. Bragg, a World War II Silver Star recipient, rather than Confederate General Braxton Bragg.\[25\]\[26\]\[27\]
- On **22 January 2026**, National Park Service crews removed 34 panels and switched off the videos at the President's House site in Philadelphia. The exhibit, opened in 2010, told the story of the nine people George Washington enslaved there.\[28\] Philadelphia sued the same day (*City of Philadelphia v. Burgum*).\[29\]\[30\]\[31\]\[32\]\[33\] On 16 February 2026, Judge Cynthia M. Rufe ordered the site restored to its 21 January condition, writing that it represented "the whole, complicated truth".\[31\]\[33\] After a partial stay by Third Circuit Judge Thomas Hardiman on 9 April 2026, which left about 16 panels up and 18 in storage, the Third Circuit unanimously vacated the injunction on 18 June 2026.\[34\] It held that "the city does not own the President's House or anything in it".\[35\]\[36\]\[37\]\[38\]\[39\]\[40\]
- On **4 July 2026**, the 250th anniversary of independence, the White House Domestic Policy Council released a 162-page report, *Saving America's Story: How Ideological Capture at the Smithsonian Institution's National Museum of American History Erases Our Heritage*. It accused museum leadership of treating American history "as a political instrument to divide, dispirit, and discourage our citizens". It targeted exhibits such as "Entertainment Nation" (objecting, for example, to a label calling Alexander Hamilton an "influential and flawed founding father") and "American Democracy: A Great Leap of Faith", and criticised the museum for lacking a dedicated founding-era exhibit in the anniversary year.\[41\]\[42\]\[43\] **Executive Order 14416 (24 July 2026)** followed, directing the National Park Service to install signs on sidewalks leading to the museum warning visitors of its "ideological capture" and pointing them to "accurate information".\[44\]\[45\]

Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch replied in a 7 July 2026 staff memo that "this report is not a fair characterization of the work and totality of the National Museum of American History".\[46\] Museum director Anthea Hartig told a House subcommittee that the museum has "a dozen exhibitions that include the founding era right now: 368 objects, 340 labels".\[47\] The Organization of American Historians stated that "No president has the authority to dictate the content of the Smithsonian's exhibitions", and AHA executive director Sarah Weicksel said there was "no precedent" for a president demanding that a museum "present a completely alternative narrative".\[48\]\[49\] Bunch announced his resignation in September 2026, effective at year's end.\[50\]\[51\] Supporters, including the Heritage Foundation's Matthew Spalding, argue that the federal government is entitled to make sure a taxpayer-funded national museum tells a coherent, celebratory national story.\[52\]

The underlying conflict is old. Kirk Savage (*Standing Soldiers, Kneeling Slaves*, 1997) and David Blight (*Race and Reunion*, 2001) showed how "Lost Cause" memory, spread through monuments built mostly between the 1890s and 1920s, reconciled white North and South at the expense of emancipation. Dell Upton (*What Can and Can't Be Said*, 2015) showed how later civil-rights monuments were constrained by the same commemorative landscape. What is new in 2025–2026 is the scale of executive power applied directly to interpretive content.

**Argentina: reopening a settled consensus.** Since 1983 Argentina has been a global model of "Memory, Truth and Justice": the CONADEP report *Nunca Más* (1984), trials of junta leaders, and the conversion of clandestine detention centres into memory sites. The ex-ESMA site became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.\[53\] The Milei government has challenged this consensus. On 24 March 2024 and again on 24 March 2025, the National Day of Memory for Truth and Justice, it released videos calling for "complete memory". The videos disputed the human-rights movement's figure of 30,000 disappeared, citing 8,753 (close to CONADEP's documented 8,961 cases), and revived what critics call the discredited "two demons" theory.\[54\]\[55\]\[56\] Vice-President Victoria Villarruel founded an organisation for victims of left-wing guerrillas.\[57\] The government has dismissed hundreds of memory-policy workers (the ATE union reported 405 dismissals from the former Human Rights Secretariat by May 2025, with at least 170 more expected, including 87 Haroldo Conti centre staff let go by WhatsApp on 31 December 2024), downgraded the Secretariat to a subsecretariat, withdrawn funding from the ex-ESMA site and closed the Haroldo Conti cultural centre to the public, though Judge Ariel Lijo ordered the government to protect former detention centres. The case shows that a hard-won memory regime can be reversed by electoral politics within a single term.

**Interstate diplomacy.** Japan's Sado gold mines were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site on 27 July 2024 after Tokyo promised to reflect the "whole history", including Korean wartime labour, and to hold annual memorials. South Korea has boycotted the Japanese-hosted ceremony in 2024, 2025 and 2026, because the ceremonies do not acknowledge that the labour was *forced*.\[58\]\[59\]\[60\] Then-Foreign Minister Fumio Kishida had earlier stated that "forced to work" did not mean "forced labour".\[61\] At least 1,519 Koreans are documented as having been mobilised to the site.\[62\] The episode shows how international heritage bodies have become arenas of memory diplomacy, and how small semantic choices carry legal weight, since forced-labour compensation suits have shaped Korea–Japan relations since 2018.

## 4. Reclaiming Silenced Narratives

Against the state-centred practices above stands a powerful countercurrent: the recovery of histories that official narratives excluded.

**Theories of silence.** Michel-Rolph Trouillot's *Silencing the Past* (1995) remains the essential framework. He identifies four moments at which silences enter history: fact creation (making sources), fact assembly (making archives), fact retrieval (making narratives) and retrospective significance (making history in the final instance). His central example, the Haitian Revolution as "unthinkable" to contemporaries, shows that power works before the historian ever writes. Michel Foucault's "subjugated knowledges" (*Society Must Be Defended*, lectures of 1975–76) and his notion of "counter-memory" give the philosophical basis for treating popular, local and disqualified knowledges as resources against official history. The Subaltern Studies collective radicalised this for South Asia: Ranajit Guha's 1982 manifesto attacked both colonialist and bourgeois-nationalist "elitism", and Gayatri Spivak's "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) warned that recovering subaltern voices risks ventriloquising them. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and Dipesh Chakrabarty's *Provincializing Europe* (2000) challenged the assumption that European history is the universal template. Howard Zinn's *A People's History of the United States* (1980) brought "history from below" to a mass audience, at the cost, its critics argue, of replacing one morality tale with another. Saidiya Hartman's "critical fabulation" ("Venus in Two Acts", 2008) offers a method for archives of slavery that record the enslaved only as commodities or corpses. It combines archival research with disciplined speculation, while openly acknowledging the limits of what can be known. Ann Laura Stoler's "colonial aphasia" (2011) and *Imperial Debris* (2013) describe a culture that has not so much forgotten empire as lost the vocabulary to name it. The prompt's term "colonial aporia" most likely refers to this concept.

**Truth commissions and recognition.** South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–1998) and Canada's TRC (final report 2015, 94 Calls to Action) put survivor testimony at the centre of national history. In May 2021 Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc First Nation announced about 200 potential unmarked burials, detected by ground-penetrating radar, at the former Kamloops Indian Residential School.\[63\]\[64\]\[65\] This triggered national mourning and, later, a backlash: an Angus Reid Institute survey of 2,508 adults published on 14 August 2025 found that 63% of Canadians (and 56% of Indigenous respondents) believe further evidence through exhumation is necessary, and that Canadians opposed criminalising denialism by 63% to 24%. The Assembly of First Nations and NDP MP Leah Gazan have called for "residential school denialism" to be criminalised, echoing Special Interlocutor Kimberly Murray's call for "urgent consideration" of "both civil and criminal sanctions"; a Senate amendment to that effect was defeated. The episode raises the central dilemma of reclamation, discussed below: whether new truths should be protected by the same legal tools once used against minorities.

**Restitution and decolonising museums.** The Benin Bronzes, looted in the British punitive expedition of 1897, are the emblematic case. The Smithsonian adopted an ethical returns policy in 2022 and transferred ownership of 29 bronzes to Nigeria's National Commission for Museums and Monuments on 11 October 2022.\[66\] Germany and Nigeria signed a joint declaration on 1 July 2022 covering 1,130 objects. The Netherlands returned 119 on 21 June 2025, the largest single repatriation to date.\[67\]\[68\] Restitution also exposes new questions of ownership. A 2023 decree by President Buhari recognised the Oba of Benin as owner and custodian, which disrupted plans for the Museum of West African Art (MOWAA) in Benin City; tensions erupted at a MOWAA preview in late 2025, and the museum reports holding no Benin Bronzes.\[69\] In the US, the Restitution Study Group, led by descendants of enslaved people, sued unsuccessfully to block the Smithsonian's return, arguing that the bronzes were partly cast from manillas paid for enslaved Africans.\[70\] "Reclaiming" a past thus raises the question: reclaimed *by whom*?

**Apologies and their limits.** Elazar Barkan (*The Guilt of Nations*, 2000) described a new international morality of restitution in which nations negotiate historical injustice. The record since then is mixed:

- **Germany–Namibia.** Germany acknowledged in 2015 that its 1904–1908 campaign against the Ovaherero and Nama amounted to genocide. A 2021 joint declaration pledged €1.1 billion in development aid over 30 years, but the declaration remains unratified. Ovaherero and Nama traditional authorities say they were excluded from the talks, and Amnesty International noted in October 2025 that Germany "continues to deny that it has a legal duty to provide reparations". Namibia held its first national Genocide Remembrance Day on 28 May 2025.\[71\]\[72\]\[73\]\[74\]\[75\]
- **The Netherlands.** Prime Minister Mark Rutte apologised for the Dutch state's role in slavery on 19 December 2022, and King Willem-Alexander apologised on 1 July 2023 (Keti Koti), opening a commemorative "Slavery Memorial Year".
- **Belgium.** King Philippe expressed his "deepest regrets" for colonial violence in Congo in June 2020. In 2022 Belgium returned a tooth of Patrice Lumumba, but a parliamentary commission on the colonial past ended in December 2022 without agreement on an apology.
- **France.** Benjamin Stora's report on memories of colonisation and the Algerian War (January 2021) recommended "small steps" rather than an apology, an approach Algerian critics found insufficient.
- **Spain.** The Historical Memory Law (2007) and the broader Democratic Memory Law (Law 20/2022) moved Spain from the "pact of forgetting" of the transition to state responsibility for exhumations. Franco's remains were moved out of the Valley of the Fallen in October 2019, and the site was renamed the Valley of Cuelgamuros. By November 2025, the government reported, the law had enabled recovery of the remains of 9,000 people and delivery of 1,614 declarations of recognition to victims. A redesign of Cuelgamuros ("The base and the cross") was chosen just before the 50th anniversary of Franco's death on 20 November 2025.\[76\]\[77\]\[78\]\[79\] Conservative Catholic groups and the Partido Popular call this "imposed" memory, and in 2024 PP and Vox jointly registered "concord" bills in Aragón, Castilla y León and the Comunitat Valenciana; Aragón repealed its regional memory law, and the Sánchez government said it would challenge the bills before the Constitutional Court.

**Statues and renaming.** The 2015 #RhodesMustFall movement at the University of Cape Town removed Cecil Rhodes's statue on 9 April 2015; Achille Mbembe framed the episode as part of a wider "decolonisation of knowledge and the archive". In Bristol, protesters toppled the statue of slave trader Edward Colston on 7 June 2020.\[80\] A jury acquitted the "Colston Four" on 5 January 2022.\[81\] After an Attorney General's Reference, the Court of Appeal ruled on 28 September 2022 that the human-rights "proportionality" defence does not cover violent or significant damage.\[82\]\[83\] The ruling left the acquittals intact but closed the defence for future protesters.\[82\] Bristol placed the graffiti-covered statue in a museum, an inventive solution that preserves the act of removal as part of the object's history.\[80\] The UK National Trust's September 2020 report linking 93 properties to colonialism and slavery set off a political backlash, showing how heritage institutions get caught between reclamation and "culture war".

## 5. Commemorative Practice and the Making of Historiography

Commemoration does not merely *express* historical understanding; it *produces* it. Three mechanisms stand out.

**First, monuments fix interpretation in space and time.** Jay Winter's *Sites of Memory, Sites of Mourning* (1995) showed that First World War memorials served mourning more than nationalism. Their meanings shifted over time as the bereaved died. James E. Young (*The Texture of Memory*, 1993) described German "counter-monuments", such as Jochen Gerz and Esther Shalev-Gerz's Monument Against Fascism in Hamburg-Harburg (1986–1993), which slowly sank into the ground, as refusing to do the work of memory on the viewer's behalf. Recent responses to contested monuments borrow from this tradition: Bristol's museum display of Colston, contextualising plaques, and Spain's "fissure" across the Cuelgamuros plaza intended to break Francoist symmetry. Base renaming in the US is the opposite strategy, a legal sleight of hand that keeps the commemorative sign while changing its official referent.

**Second, anniversaries set research and funding agendas.** Centenaries and jubilees (1914–18, 1619–2019, the 2025 eightieth anniversaries, the 2026 US Semiquincentennial) shape grant programmes, exhibitions, publishers' lists and school curricula. Peter Novick (*The Holocaust in American Life*, 1999) showed how the Holocaust moved from the margins to the centre of American public culture from the late 1960s, driven less by new scholarship than by political and communal needs. Scholarship then followed public memory. Alon Confino ("Collective Memory and Cultural History," 1997) warned that memory studies risks turning everything into "memory" without explaining *why* certain representations win out. The 2026 Smithsonian report shows the reverse dynamic: a commemorative occasion (the 250th) is used to judge, and potentially reorganise, a research institution.

**Third, holidays and national days institutionalise a canon.** Juneteenth (2021), Argentina's 24 March, Namibia's new 28 May Genocide Remembrance Day, China's Martyrs' Day (30 September, legislated in 2014) and Russia's Victory Day each encode a narrative.\[84\] Holidays are especially powerful because they are repeated every year. They turn Assmann's cultural memory into habit, and once created they are hard to abolish but easy to reinterpret, as Milei's March 24 videos show.

**The feedback loop.** Astrid Erll's work on "travelling memory" (2011) and Michael Rothberg's *Multidirectional Memory* (2009) together describe a circuit. Activists and artists articulate a counter-memory. Media amplify it. Commemorative institutions adopt it. Historians then research and refine, or contest, the new canon. The Holocaust shaped postcolonial memory, and postcolonial memory has in turn reshaped Holocaust studies. Patrick Hutton (*History as an Art of Memory*, 1993) argued that historiography itself is a kind of disciplined memory work; the loop is therefore unavoidable, and the question is how to keep it critical.

## 6. Historical Memory and Present Agendas: Memory Wars, Competitive Victimhood and Platforms

**Memory wars between states.** Koposov's term fits the Russia–Baltic–Poland–Ukraine axis. The European Parliament's resolution of 19 September 2019 equated Nazi and Soviet crimes and blamed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact for the outbreak of war; Moscow treats the same comparison as a crime. Tony Judt's epilogue to *Postwar* (2005), "From the House of the Dead", warned that the eastern enlargement of the EU would import a second, communist-centred memory that would clash with the West's Holocaust-centred one. Jan-Werner Müller's edited volume *Memory and Power in Post-War Europe* (2002) had already shown how foundational myths in the postwar period served stability through selective forgetting. Japan–China–Korea disputes over Yasukuni Shrine visits, "comfort women" and textbooks, and Turkey's continued rejection of the term "genocide" for the 1915 Armenian deportations and massacres (prosecutions under Article 301 for "insulting Turkishness"; President Biden's formal recognition on 24 April 2021), show that memory wars are global.

**Competitive versus multidirectional memory.** The most important scholarly debate of the last five years concerns whether remembering one atrocity crowds out others. Rothberg argues against "competitive memory", in which memory is a zero-sum game. He holds that memory is "multidirectional": subject to negotiation, cross-referencing and borrowing, as when W. E. B. Du Bois wrote about the Warsaw Ghetto. The German translation of his book in 2021 helped set off the "Historikerstreit 2.0".\[85\]\[86\] The controversy over Achille Mbembe in 2020 came first, and then A. Dirk Moses's essay "The German Catechism" (May 2021).\[87\] Moses argued that German Holocaust memory had hardened into a quasi-religious creed of uniqueness used to discipline minorities and critics of Israel.\[88\] His critics, among them historians who defend the Holocaust's specificity, replied that comparison can relativise, and that Moses caricatured a self-critical memory culture. Rothberg himself notes the irony: in the original 1986 *Historikerstreit*, "comparison was a tool of the right" (Ernst Nolte equating Auschwitz with the Gulag), whereas today it is mostly used by the postcolonial left.\[85\] Germany's simultaneous reckoning with Namibia, the Humboldt Forum's colonial collections and Benin restitution makes the debate concrete rather than abstract. Since 7 October 2023 and the Gaza war, the conflict over Holocaust analogies has grown sharper still. In Israel and Palestine, the Nakba and the Holocaust are often set against each other as rival memories; Israel's 2011 "Nakba Law" allows funding cuts for bodies that mark Independence Day as a day of mourning.

**The legal regulation of memory.** Memory laws now span a spectrum. At one end are victim-protective bans on Holocaust denial, upheld by the European Court of Human Rights in cases such as *Perinçek v. Switzerland* (2015, Grand Chamber). There the Court found that convicting a Turkish politician for denying the Armenian genocide violated free speech, distinguishing Armenian genocide denial from Holocaust denial in the Swiss context. At the other end are nation-protective laws such as Russia's and Poland's 2018 amendment. Rwanda's 2008 law against "genocide ideology", revised in 2018, sits uneasily between the two: it protects survivors of the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi, but scholars and rights groups argue that it also constrains discussion of crimes by the Rwandan Patriotic Front and shields the governing party's narrative. Canada's debate about residential-school denialism shows that progressive memory movements are now tempted by the same legal tools.

**Social media and memory activism.** Platforms have accelerated both reclamation and distortion. Images of Colston in the Bristol harbour, Kamloops children's shoes on the steps of legislatures and Milei's March 24 videos all circulated far faster than any historian could contextualise them. The online harassment that pushed the AHA to lock its Twitter account during the 2022 presentism row, and the night-time visitors with shovels at Kamloops, show the darker side of that speed. Enzo Traverso (*Le passé, modes d'emploi*, 2005) warned that memory's "obsession" can freeze history into moral spectacle; social media pushes this tendency to an extreme.

## 7. Critical Assessment: Presentism, Reclamation and Responsible Historiography

**The presentism debate.** On 17 August 2022, AHA President James H. Sweet published "Is History History? Identity Politics and Teleologies of the Present" in *Perspectives on History*. Echoing Lynn Hunt's 2002 "Against Presentism", he argued that projecting present identity politics onto the past, citing both the 1619 Project and conservative Supreme Court justices' use of history in the 2022 gun and abortion rulings, threatened the discipline.\[89\]\[90\]\[91\] The backlash was immediate. On 19 August Sweet apologised for causing "harm", and the AHA locked its Twitter account amid abuse.\[92\]\[93\] Critics such as Priya Satia and Malcolm Foley responded that history and politics have always been intertwined, that some presentism is unavoidable, and that Sweet's examples conflated popular memory with professional history.\[89\]\[92\]\[94\] Defenders, including some who disliked the apology more than the essay, argued that he had raised a legitimate methodological concern and been shouted down.\[95\] Both sides have a point. Every question historians ask is posed from the present, as Croce and Carl Becker knew. But anachronism, meaning judging the past by categories it lacked, remains a methodological error, not a political virtue.

**Is all history political?** In one sense, yes. The choice of subjects, archives and narrative forms reflects present concerns (White, Trouillot). But "political" in that weak sense differs from "politicised" in the strong sense of conclusions dictated by power. Richard Evans (*In Defence of History*, 1997) defended the discipline against postmodern relativism by stressing that evidence constrains interpretation, a stance tested in court during the *Irving v. Lipstadt* trial (2000), where Evans served as expert witness. Margaret MacMillan (*The Uses and Abuses of History*, 2008) argued that the best antidote to abuse is not abstinence from public argument but better history: complex, comparative and humble.

**The limits of reclamation.** Reclamation produces its own risks. First, it can harden into new orthodoxies, a concern raised not only by conservatives but by scholars such as Spivak, who warned against essentialising the subaltern, and by Dan Stone, who cautions against flattening distinct genocides. Second, it can be *captured by states*: Hindu-nationalist textbook revisions present themselves as reclaiming a suppressed Indic past, and Ukraine's derussification as decolonisation, so the language of the silenced can legitimise majoritarian projects. Third, recognition without redistribution can be hollow, as the unratified Namibia declaration and Dutch debates over reparations show. Fourth, reclamation is vulnerable to reversal: Argentina, the US base names, the President's House panels and the Smithsonian show that memory gains embedded in executive discretion rather than in law, professional autonomy or broad social consensus can be undone quickly.

**What responsible historiography looks like.** Drawing the strands together, five principles emerge:

1. **Separate the scholarly from the commemorative without pretending they are sealed off.** Historians should engage with commemoration as citizens and experts, but museums and universities need structural autonomy from executive direction. The AHA and OAH statements of 2025–2026 defend that principle; the Third Circuit's June 2026 ruling shows how thin its legal protection can be.
2. **Prefer contextualisation and addition over erasure, but do not treat it as a dogma.** Bristol's museum display of Colston and Cuelgamuros's redesign are better models than either untouched glorification or silent removal. But some sites, such as a monument to a perpetrator at a site of victimisation, may need removal.
3. **Use the criminal law against historical speech sparingly, if at all.** Experience from Russia, Poland and Rwanda suggests that memory laws designed to protect victims can be repurposed to protect states. Narrow, incitement-based hate-speech law is a safer instrument than truth-defining statutes, a lesson relevant to Canada's residential-school debate.
4. **Practise multidirectional, comparative memory with explicit standards.** Comparison is not equation. Rothberg's model works when it rests on rigorous attention to specificity, which also answers the strongest criticism of the "catechism" thesis.
5. **Make the archive itself a site of justice.** Following Trouillot and Hartman, recovering and opening archives, digitising colonial records, protecting the files of truth commissions and funding Indigenous-led research do more long-term good than symbolic gestures. Their loss, as with Canada's court-ordered destruction of some Independent Assessment Process records, is a lasting silencing.

## Recommendations

- **For policymakers:** put museums, archives and heritage bodies under arm's-length, statute-based governance with professional peer review, so that interpretation cannot be rewritten by executive order. Avoid truth-defining memory laws; where needed, rely on general hate-speech provisions.
- **For museums and heritage institutions:** document interpretive decisions transparently, publish the scholarship behind exhibits, and adopt "layered" interpretation (preserving and displaying earlier labels and contested objects) so that changes become part of the historical record rather than silent erasures.
- **For historians and professional associations:** engage the public directly and early, especially around anniversaries such as the 2026 Semiquincentennial. Defend the autonomy of colleagues across the political spectrum. Separate methodological critiques of presentism from political attacks on particular communities.
- **For memory activists and descendant communities:** anchor claims in evidence and archival access, and build durable legal and institutional protections (as Argentina's courts partly did for detention sites) rather than relying on sympathetic governments.

## Caveats

- Several 2025–2026 developments are still unfolding: Philadelphia's petition for rehearing in *City of Philadelphia v. Burgum*, implementation of EO 14416, the Smithsonian leadership transition, the criminalisation of "genocide of the Soviet people" denial in Russia, and NCERT's Class 8 Part II textbooks. Details may change.
- Some figures are contested by the parties themselves. Examples are the 30,000 versus roughly 8,700–9,000 disappeared in Argentina (the first an activist estimate including undocumented cases, the second documented cases), and the characterisation of the Kamloops findings as "anomalies" versus "graves". This analysis reports these as contested rather than adjudicating them.
- Partisan and advocacy sources (White House materials, the World Socialist Web Site, NGOs such as OVD-Info, denialist websites in the Canadian case) were consulted for positions, not treated as neutral evidence. The *Saving America's Story* report itself misdates EO 14253 as 2026 on its title page, a small sign of hasty production.\[96\]
- The scholarly attributions summarise widely cited arguments. Readers using them in formal work should consult the original texts, especially for contested concepts such as Nora's history–memory binary and Rothberg's multidirectionality.

## Conclusion

The reinterpretation of history through present-day lenses is not new. Halbwachs, Hobsbawm and Lowenthal showed long ago that memory always serves the present. What has changed in the 2020s is the *instrumentation*: memory laws with criminal penalties, executive orders addressed to museums, centrally rewritten textbooks, UNESCO listings negotiated as diplomacy, and social-media cycles that outrun scholarship. Ideologisation (Russia, China, India, Poland), instrumentalisation (Putin's Victory Day, Milei's "complete memory", the US Semiquincentennial) and reclamation (Canada's TRC, Benin restitution, Spain's Democratic Memory Law) use the same tools and often borrow each other's language of truth, victimhood and decolonisation.

Commemoration sits at the centre of this circuit. It shapes what historians are funded to study, what publics believe, and what states feel entitled to police. The scholarly literature, from Trouillot's silences to Rothberg's multidirectionality to Sweet's warning about presentism, does not offer an escape from politics. It does offer standards: evidence before identity, comparison without equation, addition before erasure, and institutional autonomy over executive fiat. The record of 2024–2026 suggests these standards are most at risk precisely where they were assumed safest, and that defending them now requires historians to act not just as interpreters of the past but as custodians of the institutions that make honest interpretation possible.
