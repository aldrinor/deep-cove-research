# Laser, IPL and LED in Aesthetic Medicine: Evidence for Photoaging, Brightening and Hyperpigmentation

**The strongest uses are targeted.** Lasers have the clearest role in resurfacing, collagen remodeling and selected focal pigment removal; intense pulsed light (IPL) is particularly useful for superficial brown discoloration combined with redness or visible vessels. LED photobiomodulation offers modest rejuvenation with little procedural recovery, but its pigmentation results are less consistent. For melasma, laser and IPL are adjuncts to photoprotection and medical treatment—not durable cures—and LED remains investigational. The defensible meaning of “brightening” is improvement in uneven tone or diagnosed hyperpigmentation, not permanent generalized bleaching. These distinctions align with recent clinical reviews and professional guidance. [1][2][3]

| Clinical target | Laser | IPL | LED/PBM | Practical judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wrinkles, texture and pores | Ablative or fractional remodeling | Broader, usually subtler rejuvenation | Modest improvement after repeated exposure | Match treatment intensity to the structural problem |
| Redness with brown photodamage | Target-specific laser selection | Treats pigment and vascular features together | Complementary skin-quality treatment | IPL is attractive for mixed superficial discoloration |
| Localized brightening and uneven tone | Pigment fragmentation or fractional treatment | Melanin-targeted tone evening | Early tone and dark-spot signals | Define a measurable facial endpoint |
| Solar lentigines and age spots | Direct evidence for nanosecond and picosecond systems | Cumulative treatment, commonly 3–6 sessions | Not an established lesion-removal treatment | Laser or IPL selection depends strongly on phototype |
| Melasma | Selected refractory disease; relapse risk | Selected epidermal or mixed disease, usually combined care | Conflicting preliminary results | Maintain photoprotection and topical treatment |
| Generalized whitening or bleaching | Lesion treatment does not establish this claim | Tone improvement does not establish this claim | Cosmetic improvement does not establish this claim | Do not promise permanent baseline-color change |

**Scope and route through the evidence.** This global comparative review covers aesthetic-medicine literature and practice current through **13 September 2026**, prioritizing developments from 2020–2026. It first distinguishes the technologies and their delivery parameters, then compares outcomes for photoaging, brightening, age spots and melasma. The final sections assess which innovations meaningfully change treatment selection, how skin phototype changes the benefit–risk balance, and what clinical guidance and regulatory status actually support.

## The principal therapies and how their delivery differs

### Lasers: separate wavelength, pulse duration and fractional delivery

**“Laser” describes several different treatments.** Relevant systems include ablative carbon-dioxide and erbium:YAG lasers, non-ablative erbium-glass and diode lasers, Nd:YAG systems, pigment-targeting Q-switched nanosecond lasers, and picosecond lasers. Fractional delivery is a separate design feature: it divides exposure into microscopic treatment zones rather than treating the entire surface uniformly. A laser can therefore be both fractional and ablative, or fractional and picosecond. [4][5]

**The target determines the useful interaction.** A chromophore is a substance that absorbs light; the principal cutaneous examples are melanin, hemoglobin and water. Pigment lasers exploit melanin absorption, vascular systems target hemoglobin, and resurfacing systems use controlled tissue injury to initiate repair and collagen remodeling. Laser light is concentrated into selected wavelengths, but its effect also depends on pulse duration, spot size, energy distribution and treatment density. [6]

| Laser category | Wavelength or source | Delivery example | Main target or interaction | Current aesthetic role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ablative resurfacing | CO₂ laser source; Er:YAG 2,940 nm | Pulsed, full-field or fractional injury | Tissue-water absorption; ablation and repair | Wrinkles, texture, scars and photodamage |
| Non-ablative fractional | Erbium glass 1,540/1,550 nm; 1,565 nm systems | Microscopic treatment columns; three sessions in one trial | Controlled dermal injury and remodeling | Fine lines, periorbital photodamage and texture |
| Superficial fractional treatment | Thulium 1,927 nm | Multiple microbeams; approximately 200 μm depth in one protocol | Superficial controlled injury | Dyschromia, brown spots and texture |
| Q-switched pigment laser | 532, 694, 755 or 1,064 nm | Nanosecond pulses; examples below | Melanin-associated photoacoustic and thermal effects | Lentigines, freckles and selected melasma |
| Picosecond pigment laser | 532, 730, 755 or 1,064 nm | Picosecond pulses; examples below | Predominantly photomechanical pigment disruption | Focal pigment and selected diffuse pigmentation |
| Fractional picosecond laser | Examples include 1,064 nm | Fractionated ultrashort pulses | Focal optical breakdown and cavitation | Photoaging, pores, scars and dyschromia |

The wavelength and delivery distinctions are described in the erbium review, contemporary pigment trials and fractional-picosecond literature. The 1,927-nm depth figure belongs to a study protocol, rather than a universal setting for every thulium system. [4][7][8]

**Ultrashort pulses change energy confinement.** Q-switched systems deliver high energy over nanoseconds; picosecond systems shorten that interval further. This favors mechanical disruption of pigment-containing structures while reducing the time available for heat to spread. It explains the interest in picosecond treatment for pigmentation, but does not make every picosecond exposure nonthermal or eliminate inflammatory injury. [9]

**Reported settings illustrate device specificity.** In the Korean solar-lentigo trial, a 532-nm picosecond laser used 450-ps pulses and 0.3–0.5 J/cm², whereas the 532-nm Q-switched comparator used 10-ns pulses and 0.6–0.8 J/cm²; both used 3–4-mm spots. Across a melasma combination review, 1,064-nm Q-switched protocols commonly used 0.8–4.0 J/cm², 5–20 ns and 6–10-mm spots. These are descriptions of studied regimens, not interchangeable prescriptions. [9][10]

### IPL: broadband treatment with adjustable filters and pulse trains

**IPL combines several optical targets.** It is a non-laser, noncoherent, polychromatic source, generally generated by a xenon flashlamp. Common outputs span approximately 500–1,200 nm, with ultraviolet components filtered out. Its broad treatment footprint and absorption by both melanin and hemoglobin make it useful for facial fields containing lentigines, background erythema and telangiectasia. [11]

**Filters do not create monochromatic light.** A 590-nm cutoff filter generally removes shorter wavelengths; it does not turn the device into a 590-nm laser. Available cutoff filters include 515, 550, 560, 570, 590, 615, 645, 690 and 755 nm. Filter choice, pulse width, interpulse delay and cooling together determine how much energy reaches the intended target and how much heat accumulates in the epidermis. [11]

| IPL approach | Light delivery | Reported parameter example | Biological purpose | Current application |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Filtered conventional IPL | Broadband flashlamp plus cutoff filter | Common pooled melasma settings: 15–30 J/cm², 3–10 ms | Melanin and hemoglobin heating | Dyschromia, vascular photodamage and selected melasma |
| Double- or triple-pulse IPL | Energy divided across separated pulses | Millisecond pulses with cooling delays | Reduce epidermal heat accumulation | Pigment and vascular treatment |
| Fractionated IPL | Multiple sub-pulses | Six weekly sessions in one melasma comparison | Distribute energy over time | Alternative melasma delivery |
| OPT/AOPT protocols | Controlled pulse shape and timing | Three monthly AOPT-LTL sessions at 10–16 J/cm² | Pigment and vascular-inflammatory targeting | Selected melasma |
| BBL-branded systems | A form of broadband IPL | 560–1,200 nm in a clinical protocol | Multiple superficial chromophores | Photodamage, brown spots and redness |

These categories overlap: branding, pulse engineering and spectral filtering describe different aspects of an IPL platform. [12][13][8]

**Pulse engineering matters clinically.** Wang and colleagues’ 2025 AOPT-LTL cohort used 590/640-nm filters, 10–16 J/cm² and three pulses of 8, 6 and 6 ms, separated by 35–40-ms delays. Fluence was adjusted for Fitzpatrick type, pigment depth and vascular response, with a study-specific temperature ceiling of 38.5°C. This exemplifies controlled delivery rather than a single “IPL dose.” [13]

### LED and photobiomodulation: cellular signaling rather than resurfacing

**LED treatment usually avoids deliberate injury.** Photobiomodulation, or PBM, uses low-irradiance visible or near-infrared light to influence cellular signaling without the intended ablation or coagulation of resurfacing treatments. LEDs are common PBM sources, although low-level lasers can also deliver PBM. Clinic-based arrays, home masks and handheld devices differ in output, geometry, calibration and treatment coverage. [14]

**Red and infrared support repair signaling.** A leading mechanism involves mitochondrial cytochrome-c oxidase, with downstream changes in adenosine triphosphate, reactive oxygen species and nitric-oxide signaling. These processes can influence fibroblasts, keratinocytes, inflammation and extracellular-matrix turnover. Yellow-light pigmentation studies additionally investigate stem-cell factor (SCF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and endothelial signaling. [15][16]

| LED category | Wavelength examples | Delivery or dose example | Proposed target | Present aesthetic role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blue LED | Approximately 405–420 nm | Consumer blue-device examples: 6–40 mW/cm² | Bacterial porphyrins | Primarily acne, not pigment removal |
| Red LED | Approximately 620–700 nm | 660 nm at 3.8 J/cm² in a wrinkle trial | Cellular repair and collagen-related signaling | Fine lines and skin quality |
| Near-infrared LED | Examples: 810, 830 and 850 nm | Often combined with red light | Mitochondrial and inflammatory signaling | Rejuvenation and repair adjunct |
| Amber/yellow LED | Approximately 570–595 nm | 585 nm, 20 mW/cm² and 20 J/cm² | Endothelial and melanogenic signaling | Investigational melasma and tone treatment |
| Multiwavelength LED | 570 or 590 nm plus 620/850 nm | 7.1 mW/cm²; 6.39 J/cm² | Combined superficial and deeper signaling | Exploratory photoaging treatment |

The examples come from clinical studies and reviews; they describe different devices and should not be combined into a new dosing regimen. [14][17][16]

**Dose includes exposure time.** Irradiance measures power per area, usually mW/cm²; radiant exposure or fluence measures energy per area, usually J/cm². For continuous delivery, energy per area equals irradiance in W/cm² multiplied by seconds. Thus, the multiwavelength study’s exposure time is approximately 900 seconds, or 15 minutes—calculated as 6.39 J/cm² ÷ 0.0071 W/cm². The amber pilot’s 20 J/cm² at 20 mW/cm² similarly corresponds to approximately 1,000 seconds. [17][16]

**More energy is not automatically better.** PBM has a dose-dependent, potentially biphasic response: increasing exposure can move a treatment from stimulation toward inhibition or oxidative stress. Surface dose also differs from the dose reaching target cells because pigmentation, scattering, distance and device geometry affect transmission. Consequently, neither a low-output consumer mask nor a high-output clinical array can be judged from wavelength alone. [15]

**Technology selection follows the treatment target.** Structural remodeling favors an appropriate laser, a superficial pigment-plus-vascular field often favors IPL, and modest non-injurious rejuvenation is the most defensible LED role.

## Photoaging and facial rejuvenation

### Lasers: effective remodeling without a universal ablative advantage

**Lasers address several structural features.** Their applications include fine lines, wrinkles, periorbital photodamage, texture irregularity, pores and scars. Ablative treatment removes tissue and initiates repair; non-ablative fractional treatment produces microscopic zones of controlled injury while preserving more of the surface. These approaches offer different treatment experiences even when both improve visible photodamage. [4]

**Pooled trials do not establish superiority.** Seirafianpour and colleagues’ 2022 systematic review included 11 studies qualitatively and four quantitatively, pooling 124 participants. Ablative versus non-ablative treatment showed no statistically significant difference in excellent improvement: odds ratio 0.83, 95% confidence interval 0.24–2.83. Good improvement, fair improvement and side effects were also statistically indistinguishable, with odds ratios of 0.88, 1.13 and 0.82, respectively. [1]

**That result is not equivalence.** The confidence interval for excellent improvement spans substantial effects in either direction. The appropriate judgment is that this pooled evidence does not justify a blanket claim that ablative treatment is better—not that every ablative and non-ablative protocol produces identical outcomes. Selection still depends on wrinkle depth, texture, pigment susceptibility and acceptable recovery.

**A recent comparison supports selective use.** Jia and colleagues’ 2025 randomized split-face study enrolled 18 Asian patients, divided equally into those younger than 45 and those aged 45 or older. Each side received three treatments with either a 1,550-nm or 1,565-nm non-ablative fractional laser at six-week intervals. The older group improved significantly in periorbital photodamage, whereas the younger group did not show significant improvement; the wavelengths did not differ significantly. [18]

**Baseline need affects visible benefit.** The age-dependent result suggests that treatment yield depends partly on the amount or character of photodamage available to improve. It does not establish age 45 as a biological threshold or prove an age-specific laser algorithm. Clinically, it favors documenting the actual wrinkle and texture burden instead of assuming that everyone seeking “rejuvenation” has the same treatable endpoint.

### IPL: strongest when photoaging includes discoloration and vessels

**IPL improves broad photodamage features.** Sales and colleagues’ 2022 systematic review included 16 studies and 637 participants aged 21–80 years, with Fitzpatrick phototypes I–IV. Treatment averaged 4.29 sessions, ranging from three to seven. Reported improvements covered telangiectasia, erythema, lentigines, hyperpigmentation, texture, pores and wrinkles. [2]

**Color improvement is a particular strength.** IPL can simultaneously reduce superficial brown contrast and vascular redness across a facial field. That combination can make skin look more even and less photodamaged without reproducing the tissue-removal effect of ablative resurfacing. Fine superficial lines may improve, but deeper furrows are a less predictable target. [11]

**A direct trial shows complementary effects.** In Almukhtar, Wood and Fabi’s randomized split-face study of 40 subjects, IPL, a 1,440-nm non-ablative fractional diode laser, and sequential IPL plus diode treatment each produced approximately 40%–50% investigator-reported improvement at day 120. Reported blinded Global Aesthetic Improvement results were 90% for the combination, 84% for IPL and 83% for diode treatment. Wrinkles improved significantly in the diode and combination groups; hyperpigmentation and telangiectasia improved significantly in all three. [19]

| Treatment in the 40-subject trial | Day-120 investigator improvement | Reported global aesthetic result | Feature-specific finding | Patient experience |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IPL | Approximately 40%–50% | 84% | Pigmentation and telangiectasia improved | Lower satisfaction than combination |
| 1,440-nm fractional diode | Approximately 40%–50% | 83% | Wrinkles, pigmentation and telangiectasia improved | Lower satisfaction than combination |
| Sequential IPL plus diode | Approximately 40%–50% | 90% | Combined structural and color improvement | Highest subject satisfaction |

These percentages describe the study’s reported aesthetic assessments, not percentages of collagen restored or years of aging reversed. [19]

**The clinical advantage is multidimensional.** The combination’s numerical global advantage was six percentage points over IPL and seven over diode treatment, while subject satisfaction also favored combination care. However, the similar 40%–50% overall investigator estimates show why a single global score should not obscure feature-specific differences. A patient concerned primarily with vessels may value a different result from one concerned primarily with wrinkles.

### LED: modest improvement with repeated treatment

**LED has measurable rejuvenation signals.** A 2025 randomized exploratory study by Yi and colleagues enrolled 30 patients, assigning 15 to 570-nm yellow plus 620-nm red and 850-nm infrared light, and 15 to 590-nm yellow with the same red/infrared components. Both regimens used 7.1 mW/cm² and 6.39 J/cm², three times weekly for eight weeks; 28 participants completed treatment. The 570-nm group showed stronger wrinkle and brown-spot effects than the 590-nm group. [17]

**Objective wrinkle measurements add useful context.** Hernández-Bule and colleagues’ 2024 review tabulated a 2023 split-face trial of 137 participants receiving ten periocular treatments over four weeks at 3.8 J/cm². It reported wrinkle-volume reductions of 31.6% with 660-nm red light and 29.9% with 590-nm amber light. These are regional wrinkle measurements—not a 30% improvement in every component of facial aging. [14]

**Patients often notice skin-quality changes.** A separate 31-participant study summarized in the LED review used nine sessions and reported improved periorbital wrinkles, elasticity, smoothness and firmness; more than half rated improvement at 25%–50%. The American Academy of Dermatology also describes a 90-patient study in which more than 90% noticed some improvement after eight red-light treatments over four weeks. That broad endpoint included softer skin, less redness and lighter dark spots. [15][3]

**“Some improvement” is a modest threshold.** A high proportion of patients noticing a change can coexist with relatively small average changes. For counseling, the most useful pairing is an objective regional measure—such as wrinkle volume or texture—with a patient-selected goal. LED becomes attractive when that goal is modest skin-quality improvement and the patient values minimal recovery more than an intensive resurfacing effect.

### Treatment burden, recovery and observed response

**Low downtime can mean more sessions.** The Yi regimen required 24 sessions over eight weeks. Using the calculated 15-minute exposure, that represents approximately six hours of illumination—24 × 15 minutes—before setup or travel. By comparison, the periorbital fractional-laser study used three treatment visits, although its session duration and recovery cannot be inferred from the LED schedule. [17][18]

| Modality | Mechanism relevant to photoaging | Studied treatment burden | Recovery example | Observed assessment window | Best-supported emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Laser | Controlled injury or photomechanical remodeling | Three fractional sessions in one recent trial | Resurfacing-review erythema: 12.8–15.24 days | Commonly about 12 weeks to four months | Wrinkles, texture and pores |
| IPL | Melanin/hemoglobin targeting with secondary remodeling | Mean 4.29 sessions; range 3–7 | Redness/edema about two days; crusting up to seven | Day 120 in the direct comparison | Brown and vascular photodamage |
| LED/PBM | Cellular repair and inflammatory signaling | Examples: 8, 9, 10 or 24 sessions | No planned downtime for routine red-light use | Eight-week recent trial; older studies extend to six months | Modest skin-quality improvement |

Recovery figures refer to different procedures and endpoints; persistent erythema is not synonymous with time completely away from work. [20][11][3][14]

**Combination treatment is not inherently harsher.** Pour Mohammad and colleagues’ 2023 review included 18 clinical trials and 448 cases. Compared with monotherapy, laser combinations reported pain scores of 4.8 versus 7.18 on a 0–10 scale and erythema durations of 12.8 versus 15.24 days. A plausible explanation is complementary targeting with less reliance on one intensive exposure, but that mechanism was not established across every included regimen. [20]

**Satisfaction measures require careful reading.** That review reported satisfaction-category prevalences of 39%, 25%, 15% and 7% with combination treatment, versus 20%, 25%, 16% and 17% with monotherapy. These separately pooled category estimates should not be treated as a complete distribution from one patient cohort or converted into a universal satisfaction percentage. The more actionable result is that particular combinations can improve patient experience, while their benefit must be demonstrated for the actual component treatments. [20]

**The photoaging decision is feature-specific.** Choose laser remodeling for structural problems, IPL for prominent superficial pigment and vascular changes, and LED for a modest adjunctive result with minimal interruption; the balance changes when the dominant feature or acceptable recovery changes.

## Skin whitening and brightening: what the measurements support

### Brightening is a clinical endpoint, not a single mechanism

**Tone evening is the supported goal.** Laser and IPL studies typically measure reduced spots, localized melanin, facial dyschromia or melasma severity. LED studies sometimes measure tone, dark spots and perceived radiance alongside texture. None of these outcomes should be translated into a percentage of permanent whole-body whitening or an entitlement to depigment otherwise healthy baseline skin. [2][3]

**Different measurements answer different questions.** A melanin index estimates pigment-related optical absorption at a measured site. The individual typology angle, ITA°, is an instrumental skin-color measure, with larger values indicating lighter measured tone. VISIA and related imaging systems quantify selected image features or compare them with reference distributions; a better brown-spot score is not automatically an equivalent change in overall skin color. [21][22]

This distinction prevents a favorable result in one domain from being marketed as generalized bleaching.

### Why brightening can reverse into darkening

**Inflammation can counteract pigment removal.** Laser or IPL exposure may remove or disperse existing melanin while simultaneously provoking inflammation that stimulates new pigment production. Excessive epidermal heating, aggressive treatment density or poorly controlled underlying disease can therefore produce post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH. A short-term lightening result is clinically valuable only if that inflammatory cost remains acceptable. [25]

**Visible light can also stimulate pigmentation.** Experimental exposure evidence summarized in the LED review found darker and more persistent pigmentation after visible light in Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin than in phototype II, with effects lasting up to 14 days. This does not prove that every therapeutic red or yellow LED protocol worsens pigmentation: spectrum, dose and exposure conditions differ. It does establish that “visible,” “non-UV” and “non-invasive” are not synonyms for pigment-neutral. [15]

**Brightening should remain diagnosis-led.** The supported objective is a more even appearance through treatment of specific discoloration; a request for permanent generalized bleaching changes the goal beyond the demonstrated benefits and introduces avoidable pigment-injury risk.

## Solar lentigines, age spots and focal hyperpigmentation

### Picosecond versus Q-switched laser: the clearest direct comparison

**A single treatment can improve lentigines.** Kim and colleagues’ 2020 prospective randomized split-face study enrolled 20 Korean women aged 27–72 years with Fitzpatrick III–V skin and more than five facial solar lentigines on each side. Each received one 532-nm picosecond treatment and one 532-nm Q-switched nanosecond treatment, with efficacy assessed through 12 weeks. Mean blinded quartile-improvement scores were **2.95 versus 1.80**, favoring picosecond treatment. [9]

**The clearance scale supplies the interpretation.** The study scored no improvement as zero, 1%–25% improvement as one, 26%–50% as two, 51%–75% as three, and 76%–100% as four. A mean of 2.95 therefore sits close to the “good improvement” category, while 1.80 lies between poor and fair. Averaging ordinal categories does not justify calling these results 73.75% and 45% lesion clearance.

**Earlier series provide threshold-based response rates.** The same article cites a prior report in which 83% of patients achieved more than 50% improvement after one 532-nm picosecond session, and another in which 93.02% of lesions achieved more than 75% clearance after one treatment. These are useful supported partial answers to “how much clears,” but their patient-level and lesion-level denominators measure different things. They should not be combined into one modern pooled clearance probability. [9]

**Pigmentary safety favored picosecond numerically.** Four-week PIH occurred on 1/20 picosecond-treated sides, or 5%, versus 6/20 Q-switched sides, or 30%. The difference did not reach conventional statistical significance, p=0.057. Satisfaction scores were almost identical, 3.55 versus 3.50, and pain scores were 3.7 versus 3.5 on the study’s numeric scale. [9]

**Objective improvement and satisfaction can diverge.** The trial supports better photographic clearance with its picosecond protocol, but patients did not report a correspondingly large satisfaction advantage. That matters when a more expensive technology is justified primarily by a clinician-rated difference. The potential PIH reduction remains clinically important, especially in pigment-prone skin, while the small trial does not establish a precise risk reduction for every patient.

### Wavelength selection: less tissue injury does not always mean more clearance

**Three wavelengths improved focal lesions.** Liu and colleagues’ 2025 randomized intra-participant trial assigned facial areas in 42 people with freckles or solar lentigines to 730-nm picosecond, 532-nm picosecond or 694-nm Q-switched nanosecond treatment. Treatments were spaced one month apart, with assessments at one, three and six months. All three improved clearance and instrumental skin-color measurements, without significant between-laser differences in global aesthetic ratings, satisfaction or ITA. [7]

**Histology separated the treatments more clearly.** Basal-cell vacuolar degeneration was minimal after 730-nm picosecond treatment, moderate after 532-nm picosecond treatment, and extensive and continuous after 694-nm nanosecond treatment. Pain and resolution of erythema, edema and scabbing also differed. Thus, 730 nm had the least histologic injury in this study without demonstrating a statistically superior clearance outcome. [7]

**Selectivity includes the surrounding epidermis.** Shorter pigment-targeting wavelengths can interact strongly with superficial melanin, including background epidermal pigment. A longer wavelength or shorter pulse may improve the balance between lesion disruption and collateral injury, but the result depends on the lesion, device and delivered exposure. The appropriate interpretation is improved targeting potential—not a universal rule that one wavelength is best for all brown lesions.

### Recurrence, phototype and alternatives

**Longer observation is not permanent clearance.** Modern focal-pigment trials demonstrate improvement over 12 weeks to six months. For ongoing care, the practical distinction is between a treated lesion becoming visible again and new lentigines developing in a persistently sun-exposed field. Photoprotection addresses the continuing exposure that contributes to both the appearance of photodamage and future lesion burden. [7][11]

**Fair-skin guidance supports laser or IPL.** The Global 2025 consensus recommends nanosecond lasers, picosecond lasers and IPL as first-line options for established visible lentigines in fair skin, with assessment generally around three months after treatment. It specifically urges caution with IPL and shorter wavelengths such as 532 nm in Fitzpatrick V–VI because of PIH risk. [27]

**Cryotherapy remains a relevant comparator.** A systematic review of five randomized studies suggests that laser treatment may achieve more improvement than cryotherapy with comparable side effects, but four studies had high risk of bias. This supports considering laser or IPL when cosmetic precision is important, while leaving room for cryotherapy according to lesion, site, resources and patient preference. [28]

**Skin-of-color selection remains conservative.** A PIH-focused review advises avoiding ruby and alexandrite lasers in darker phototypes and describes IPL as generally contraindicated in that setting, whereas the broader global consensus uses more qualified caution. These statements concern different clinical contexts; together they argue for experienced medical assessment rather than importing a fair-skin lentigo protocol into melanin-rich skin. [25]

**For age spots, net appearance decides.** Targeted laser treatment is well supported for discrete lesions and IPL for suitable superficial fields, but clearance must be weighed against PIH, hypopigmentation and the patient’s continuing sun-related lesion burden.

## Melasma: improvement is easier to demonstrate than durable control

### The treatment foundation remains medical and protective

**Melasma is a relapsing biological disorder.** It involves melanocyte activity, ultraviolet and visible-light exposure, hormonal influences, inflammation, vascular changes and dermal remodeling. Basement-membrane disruption can allow pigment into the dermis, where melanophages retain it. Removing visible melanin therefore treats an important manifestation without necessarily suppressing the processes that generate new pigment. [29]

**First-line care can produce substantial improvement.** Ding and colleagues’ 2026 review places strict photoprotection and topical medical treatment—such as 4% hydroquinone or triple-combination cream—at the foundation of care, citing approximately 45%–60% MASI reduction by 16 weeks. Laser and light procedures are additional options for selected refractory cases or patients accepting greater procedural risk. [30]

**Severity thresholds differ between consensus documents.** The Global 2025 consensus defines mild disease as mMASI ≤4 and moderate-to-severe disease as >4. The Latin American 2024 algorithm uses ≤5 and >5, respectively. The practical requirement is to record which scale and classification are being used, then follow change consistently rather than treating these thresholds as interchangeable. [27][31]

### Laser plus IPL: favorable pooled results, not a universal combination rule

**The largest synthesis favors combination treatment.** A 2026 Frontiers in Medicine meta-analysis included 31 controlled studies and 2,801 adults—26 randomized and five non-randomized studies. Combined Q-switched laser plus IPL produced greater MASI reduction, standardized mean difference −1.20, and a reported clinical efficacy proportion of 92.0% versus 77.4% with monotherapy. The study-weighted efficacy risk ratio was 1.10, 95% CI 1.03–1.17. [10]

**Short-term recurrence also favored combination.** Reported recurrence proportions were 8.5% versus 17.8%, with a study-weighted risk ratio of 0.54, 95% CI 0.32–0.90. The group proportions and weighted risk ratios are different statistical summaries; the risk ratios should not be reconstructed by simply dividing the displayed percentages. Efficacy evidence was graded moderate, while recurrence evidence was low because only four studies contributed that outcome. [10]

**Not every patient-centered outcome improved.** Patient satisfaction did not differ significantly, risk ratio 1.09, 95% CI 0.95–1.26, and adverse reactions did not differ significantly, risk ratio 0.92, 95% CI 0.67–1.26. Better pooled clinician-rated response therefore does not automatically establish a better overall treatment experience. [10]

**A counterexample prevents overgeneralization.** The IPL review describes a 20-woman split-face study in mixed melasma: five weekly Q-switched sessions were compared with the same laser course plus three IPL sessions. MASI reductions were 40% and 60%, respectively, but reported recurrence was 11% with monotherapy versus 33% with combination treatment. Microcrusting occurred in 70% of patients, and one combination-treated patient developed hypomelanosis. [12]

**Combination benefit depends on the comparator.** Adding a device to topical treatment is a different question from adding IPL to an existing laser course. Fithria and colleagues’ meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials, involving 461 patients from eight countries, found an overall standardized benefit of −0.55 for device-plus-topical care over topical care alone. It also reported substantially higher adverse-event odds, 8.96, although safety certainty was very low. [33]

**Other syntheses show inconsistent add-on effects.** A 2025 network meta-analysis of 14 trials and 738 women found a favorable direct comparison for intradermal TXA plus Q-switched laser versus intradermal TXA, but not for every oral-TXA/laser comparison. The useful judgment is that complementary treatments can improve selected outcomes—not that more components necessarily create a better regimen. [34]

### LED: biologically plausible, clinically unsettled

**Controlled LED results remain mixed.** Galache and colleagues’ 2025 double-blind non-inferiority pilot randomized 21 women to active amber PBM plus placebo cream or sham PBM plus 5% liposomal TXA for 12 weeks. There were no significant within- or between-group differences in MASI or physician global assessment. Early termination left statistical power at 51%, so the result neither establishes PBM efficacy nor demonstrates non-inferiority. [35]

**Quality of life followed another pattern.** Both groups improved on the melasma quality-of-life measure despite the absence of clinical MASI improvement. Warmth was associated with PBM and burning with TXA. This illustrates why improved well-being, although important, should not be reported as proof of pigment clearance. [35]

**The positive yellow-light trial is narrower.** The 71-woman vitamin-C study reported week-12 mMASI values of 6.51 with combination treatment and 9.91 with vehicle plus light. Its adjusted changes favored vitamin C, as did instrumental tone and satisfaction. However, the common active-light background makes it evidence for that combination comparison rather than definitive evidence that LED alone treats melasma. [21]

**Small mechanistic studies explain the interest.** Dai and colleagues’ 2022 clinical pilot treated ten patients with mild-to-severe melasma using 585 ± 10-nm amber LED, 20 mW/cm² and 20 J/cm² weekly for eight sessions. The investigators reported improvements in erythema and pigmentation, assessed with VISIA, Mexameter and MASI, with follow-up at one and three months. Parallel cell experiments supported reduced angiogenic and melanogenic signaling. [16]

**The current clinical position stays adjunctive.** Red/NIR or amber treatment may eventually prove useful for a defined melasma subtype or inflammatory state, but the present contrast between positive small studies, active-background comparisons and a negative underpowered pilot supports supervised investigational use rather than substitution for established care.

### Relapse prevention and the meaning of recurrence figures

**Recurrence percentages require their denominators.** Ding and colleagues reported recurrence in 150/926 patients, or 16.2%, across heterogeneous follow-up. Q-switched Nd:YAG accounted for 68/150 recurrence events, or 45.3%; that is a share of recurrences, not a 45.3% recurrence risk among everyone treated with that laser. A separate clinical estimate cited in the AOPT-LTL article reports 58.8% recurrence at one year after treatment. [30][13]

**Maintenance begins before the final session.** The IPL review recommends topical management for at least six to 12 months after treatment. The Latin American consensus emphasizes SPF50+ UVA/UVB and visible-light protection, preferably using iron-oxide-containing or pigmented sunscreen, alongside behavior and clothing that reduce exposure. These measures address ongoing triggers instead of assuming pigment removal has reset the disease. [12][31]

**Response should govern escalation.** The Latin American algorithm recommends review after six to eight weeks. With at least 30% mMASI improvement, effective treatment continues toward maximum benefit before transition to maintenance; with less than 30%, treatment is reassessed or adjusted according to severity. This is a consensus decision rule, not an instruction to add laser or IPL automatically. [31]

**Melasma treatment should optimize sustained control.** Laser and IPL have stronger short-term pigment evidence than LED, but their clinical value depends on maintenance, tolerable inflammatory burden and avoidance of pigmentary injury—not simply the largest initial score reduction.

## Which advances from 2020–2026 meaningfully change practice?

### Fractional picosecond treatment changes the tissue interaction

**Photomechanical microinjury is the main advance.** Fractional picosecond systems can produce laser-induced optical breakdown and associated cavitation, creating microscopic epidermal or dermal vacuoles rather than conventional fractional coagulation or ablation. Bigge and Bigge’s 2026 review links these effects with remodeling-related proteins and signaling, including collagens, matrix metalloproteinases and heat-shock proteins. [5]

**The mature uses are not identical.** The review judges clinical support strongest for atrophic acne scars, photoaging, pores and selected non-acne scars. Pigment and melasma applications should therefore be evaluated on their own outcomes rather than borrowing confidence from scar improvement. The practical advance is another way to distribute controlled microinjury, with increasing experience in Asian and darker phototypes. [5]

**Real-world treatment provides a concrete example.** The Vietnamese cohort used fractional 1,064-nm Nd:YAG picosecond treatment at 0.3–0.45 J/cm², 450 ps and 10 Hz through an 8-mm deep-fractional handpiece. Three whole-face sessions were spaced four weeks apart. VISIA improvements were reported from month one through month three, while transient erythema occurred in 68.18% and swelling in 29.55% of the 44 completers. [24]

**Less thermal spread is a hypothesis about advantage.** It is biologically plausible that photomechanical delivery reduces unwanted heating, and the focal-pigment trials provide supporting clinical and histologic observations. However, the contribution of optical breakdown itself versus combined mechanical, acoustic and limited thermal effects remains incompletely resolved. That distinction matters when a marketing claim attributes every improvement to one newly named mechanism. [5]

### Combination regimens: complementary targets versus added complexity

**Topical combinations have the clearest rationale.** A procedure may reduce existing pigment or create fractional microchannels, while a topical agent suppresses melanogenesis or inflammatory signaling. Khan and colleagues’ 2023 meta-analysis of nine randomized studies found a significant MASI reduction with laser plus topical TXA, p<0.0001. The variety of lasers and schedules makes the conclusion more useful at the strategy level than as one standardized prescription. [37]

**Delivery enhancement must be distinguished from signaling.** Fractional-laser microchannels can facilitate topical access; that mechanism differs from yellow-light PBM combined with vitamin C, where the proposed interaction concerns cellular and inflammatory effects. A favorable combination result does not establish which component was responsible or prove that the components interact synergistically. The comparator must separate those questions.

**More elaborate platforms remain investigational.** A UT Southwestern/Sciton protocol combines BBL/IPL with 1,927-nm MOXI and hybrid 1,470/2,940-nm HALO treatment. It plans up to 15 subjects receiving one or two treatments, with a possible second session four to eight weeks later and assessments one and three months after the final treatment. Its anticipated seven- to ten-day downtime is a protocol expectation, not an observed comparative result; Fitzpatrick V–VI are excluded. [38]

**Experimental adjuncts need separate justification.** Fractional CO₂ with exosome-based adjuncts, or microchannel delivery of vitamin C and cysteamine, appears in contemporary melasma discussions. These approaches should be presented as developing strategies, not as established replacements for familiar topical maintenance. Their additional product and procedure components require their own evidence and safety assessment. [29]

**Photodynamic therapy is a separate intervention.** Adding a photosensitizer changes light treatment into a photochemical process that generates reactive species; it is not simply stronger PBM. An older 20-patient comparison summarized in the Actas review reported greater overall and pigment improvement with photodynamic treatment plus IPL than with IPL alone. That example supports the relevance of combination mechanisms, while its age and size distinguish it from recent device-engineering advances. [14][39]

### Imaging and personalization: useful measurement before validated algorithms

**Imaging can improve target description.** VISIA, Mark-Vu, dermoscopy, Mexameter measurements and colorimetry can characterize pigment, vascular features and change over time. Reflectance confocal microscopy, optical coherence tomography and other optical imaging also help study tissue interactions. These tools are most immediately useful for making the endpoint more explicit and documenting whether treatment is helping or provoking injury. [23][5]

**The dual-axis cohort illustrates individualized delivery.** Its guided group included 15 patients with recurrent or persistent facial hyperpigmentation. Superficial lesions received 532-nm picosecond treatment at nominal 275 ps and 0.25–0.30 J/cm²; deeper or mixed patterns received 1,064 nm at nominal 300 ps and 0.7–1.0 J/cm². Treatment intensity and total session number were individualized according to lesion features, skin reactivity and tissue response. [23]

**Its large imaging differences are hypothesis-generating.** The comparison used historical, heterogeneous conventional treatment rather than random assignment to standardized regimens. Same-investigator reassessments in 15 selected cases showed directional agreement of 94.4% and 96.7%, but directional consistency is not absolute measurement agreement or independent observer reliability. Better images and statistically adjusted associations do not establish that an imaging algorithm caused better outcomes. [23]

**Pigmentation-aware dosing is clinically necessary.** A University of São Paulo review emphasizes that darker skin can attenuate delivered light differently and that few PBM trials stratify outcomes by skin tone. It also notes that the cited 2010 and 2022 World Association for Photobiomodulation Therapy guidance does not differentiate dosing by pigmentation. The appropriate response is careful observation and conservative individualized treatment—not an assumed universal mathematical correction factor. [40]

**The advance is greater treatment precision.** Recent engineering and imaging expand the options for matching exposure to tissue and disease, but become practice-changing only when that precision improves a meaningful clinical outcome compared with existing care.

## Safety, evidence strength and regulatory interpretation

### Skin phototype changes both benefit and risk

**Background melanin competes for energy.** This is particularly important for pigment-targeting laser and IPL treatment: the intended lesion and the surrounding epidermis can absorb overlapping wavelengths. An exposure that clears a lesion in fair skin may cause excessive inflammation or pigment loss in darker or recently tanned skin. Fitzpatrick type, recent tanning, prior PIH and lesion depth therefore belong in the treatment decision, not merely in the consent form. [41]

**The principal harms are different outcomes.** PIH is treatment-associated darkening; hypopigmentation is loss of pigment and may be persistent. Burns, blistering, scarring, infection and prolonged erythema are additional procedural harms. Melasma worsening or relapse may reflect inflammation, ongoing disease triggers or both; it should not automatically be interpreted as failure to use enough energy. [42][25]

**Safety denominators matter as much as efficacy.** Ding and colleagues reported adverse events in 419/872 laser-treated melasma patients, or 48.1%, including many transient reactions rather than exclusively serious injury. Two Q-switched patients discontinued, one because of hyperpigmentation and one because of urticaria. The percentage should therefore prompt discussion of event type and severity rather than be presented as a universal serious-complication rate. [30]

**Zero observed events is not zero risk.** The IPL/TXA trial recorded none among 33 completers. An approximate statistical upper 95% bound for an event observed zero times is about 9%—a rule-of-three estimate, 3 ÷33. That calculation does not suggest the true risk is 9%; it shows why a small event-free study cannot establish universal safety. [22]

### Prevention, contraindications and ocular protection

**Risk reduction starts before exposure.** ARPANSA recommends small-area laser or IPL patch testing, with up to 48 hours of observation in higher-risk situations. Conservative settings, appropriate cooling, avoidance of tanning and careful review of tissue response are central controls. A patch test helps identify early reactivity but does not replace monitoring for delayed PIH or the cumulative effects of repeated treatment. [6]

**Medical screening identifies important cautions.** Relevant factors include photosensitivity disorders such as lupus, photosensitizing medications, active dermatitis or infection, suspicious lesions in the treatment field, recent sun exposure, and artificial tanning products. Pregnancy and breastfeeding commonly prompt deferral of elective treatment because pigmentation and results can be less predictable; trial exclusions should not be misrepresented as proof of a universal fetal hazard from every modality. [41][42]

**Eye protection is modality-specific.** Laser eyewear needs optical density appropriate to the operating wavelength. IPL can injure the iris, and periocular use requires medical supervision and suitable internal metal shields where indicated. LED users should follow device-specific eye-protection instructions; ordinary sunglasses are not substitutes for protective goggles. [6][3]

**Blue light adds photochemical concerns.** MHRA guidance identifies retinal photochemical injury primarily within approximately 400–600 nm and notes the importance of exposure duration and cumulative exposure. ARPANSA also warns about blue-LED ocular reactions and sleep-cycle disturbance. These hazards do not imply that every properly used LED device is dangerous; they explain why low thermal intensity does not remove optical-safety requirements. [43][6]

**PIH prophylaxis is not efficacy evidence.** A prevention review found signals favoring selected anti-inflammatory measures, TXA approaches and cooling after energy-device treatment. It included 14 randomized trials, with six assessed at high risk of bias. Such findings support individualized prevention in appropriately selected patients, not routine addition of every prophylactic intervention or a claim that prevention guarantees successful pigment clearance. [44]

### Guidance and regulation are different forms of support

**Professional guidance favors indication-specific care.** The Global 2025 consensus supports nanosecond laser, picosecond laser and IPL treatment for visible lentigines in fair skin, while recommending caution with energy procedures in melasma and PIH. The Latin American consensus places photoprotection, topical treatment, response assessment and maintenance at the center of melasma care. AAD describes red-light therapy mainly as complementary treatment. [27][31][3]

**FDA clearance remains device- and indication-specific.** For devices marketed through the usual 510(k) pathway, substantial equivalence concerns a specified device and intended use. It does not transfer to every wavelength, home mask, pigmentation disorder or whitening claim. FDA’s January 2023 PBM document is explicitly draft, nonbinding guidance—not itself a clearance or an efficacy determination. [45]

**Broad aesthetic labels can mislead.** FDA’s 2011 “low-level laser system for aesthetic use” guidance specifically concerns adipocyte disruption and release of fat and lipids. That document does not establish an indication for melasma, age spots or photoaging. Similarly, a trial registry’s description of a device as approved or cleared should not substitute for the actual jurisdiction-specific labeling. [46]

**Provider rules vary by region.** ARPANSA describes no Australia-wide requirements governing who may deliver cosmetic laser, IPL and LED treatment. It identifies specific laser restrictions in Tasmania, Queensland and Western Australia, IPL licensing in Tasmania, and no comparable LED cosmetic-delivery restrictions. This concerns service oversight, not a declaration that every product or promotional claim is clinically validated. UK MHRA optical-radiation guidance similarly addresses safe use rather than proof of aesthetic benefit. [41][43]

### A practical selection and reassessment sequence

**Document the intended benefit before treatment.** The following sequence brings the evidence into routine selection without implying that one device is the default for every skin concern.

| Step | Information to record | Decision logic | Reassessment or stop rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Define the endpoint | Wrinkle, vascular change, lentigo, melasma or tone | Match treatment to the diagnosed target | Reconsider if diagnosis or goal changes |
| 2. Establish susceptibility | Phototype, tanning, PIH history, medications, photosensitivity | Balance expected benefit against pigment and injury risk | Defer or seek specialist review when risk is excessive |
| 3. Specify the actual protocol | Device, wavelength, pulse, dose, coverage, cooling and course | Use evidence relevant to that exposure | Do not transfer settings between unlike systems |
| 4. Apply protective measures | Patch response, eyewear, cooling and photoprotection | Reduce avoidable optical and inflammatory injury | Pause for unexpected pain, burns or pigment worsening |
| 5. Measure and maintain | Clinical score, images, pigment measure and patient goal | Continue only when net benefit remains favorable | Adjust treatment and maintenance to response |

This sequence reflects regulator safety advice and consensus emphasis on individualized assessment, documentation and follow-up. [6][31]

**Long-term judgments have common deciding conditions.** Comparative durability, generalized-brightness claims, solar-lentigo recurrence and LED relapse prevention would be decided by matched treatment protocols with assessment after cessation, maintenance and sun exposure recorded, validated color or lesion endpoints, phototype-stratified outcomes and at least 12 months of follow-up. For lentigines, that assessment must distinguish recurrence of treated lesions from newly developing lesions; for melasma, it must distinguish relapse after improvement from initial nonresponse.

**The evidence uses different measurement bases.** Most studies are small or short, with heterogeneous devices, combination regimens and limited phototype-stratified analysis; IPL rejuvenation evidence chiefly covers I–IV and melasma evidence is largely female and often East Asian III–V. MASI/mMASI, ordinal clearance scores, imaging indices, colorimetry and satisfaction are not interchangeable. Reported standardized effects are not raw score points, and assessment windows are not guaranteed durations of benefit. Explicit calculations here—the LED exposure time, AOPT mean-score percentage change and zero-event safety bound—are estimates or derived summaries, not additional observations.

**The clinical conclusion is selective confidence.** Light-based aesthetic treatment is most defensible when the diagnosis, optical target and measured outcome align. Lasers and IPL provide valuable options for photoaging and focal pigmentation; LED offers a gentler adjunct for modest rejuvenation. For melasma and requests framed as whitening, sustained disease control and pigment safety should determine success—not the novelty of the device or the speed of initial lightening.
