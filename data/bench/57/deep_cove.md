# Global AI Activity of Major International Consulting Firms, 2023–2026

Major international consulting firms are moving AI from experimentation toward governed, workflow-embedded and increasingly agentic delivery. The clearest disclosed global commitment anchors are Accenture’s **US$3 billion over three years** and Deloitte’s **US$3 billion through fiscal 2030**; PwC’s US$1 billion commitment is US-only, EY reports US$1.4 billion plus US$300 million for EY.ai, and Capgemini’s €2 billion figure is reported through a secondary comparison. [1][2][3]

There is no defensible league table of total investment or impact because firms report different constructs: commitments, acquisitions, alliance spending, bookings, revenue, book of business, internal adoption, client value and survey expectations. Accenture has the clearest disclosed commercial set—US$5.9 billion in AI bookings, approximately US$2.7 billion in Gen/Agentic AI revenue and approximately 6,000 deployments in FY25—while IBM reports a GenAI book of business above US$12.5 billion and BCG reports more than US$30 billion in client value. [4][5]

Across the market, the strategic direction is similar—platforms, agents, responsible AI, ecosystem partnerships and workforce change—but the commercial emphasis differs. Deloitte and KPMG foreground governed agent deployment; PwC emphasizes enterprise-wide workflow redesign; EY emphasizes assurance, trusted data and Microsoft scale; Accenture emphasizes industrialized platforms and acquisitions; the MBB firms combine strategy with build capability; IBM combines a hybrid-cloud product stack with consulting delivery; and Capgemini emphasizes agentic operations and sovereign ecosystems.

## Global investment landscape and cross-firm comparison

### Investment figures measure different things

**The largest global anchors are Accenture and Deloitte.** Accenture announced US$3 billion over three years for its global Data & AI practice on 13 June 2023. Deloitte announced US$3 billion in Generative AI through fiscal year 2030 on 25 March 2025. Both are global commitments, but they have different horizons and announcement dates; neither announcement reports cumulative realized expenditure. [1][2]

**Capgemini’s global figure is secondary-reported.** A 2026 comparison reports a €2 billion three-year global AI commitment announced in July 2023. Capgemini’s primary disclosures establish the strategic direction and later WNS acquisition, but the examined primary material does not provide the same headline commitment in a comparable company announcement. The €2 billion figure should therefore be retained as a secondary-reported commitment, not treated as an audited AI-spending line. [6][7]

**EY reports several separate investment buckets.** EY reported US$1.4 billion invested for the EY.ai launch in September 2023 and a further US$300 million in FY24, bringing reported EY.ai investment to US$1.7 billion. Separately, EY reports a US$1 billion, four-year Assurance technology investment and a US$1 billion-plus, five-year EY–Microsoft initiative announced in 2026. These scopes should not be added: the EY.ai platform, Assurance technology and Microsoft alliance cover overlapping but distinct capability and partnership categories. [8][3][9]

**PwC’s commitment is explicitly US-specific.** PwC US announced on 26 April 2023 that it planned to invest US$1 billion over three years in AI capabilities, including Microsoft and Azure OpenAI offerings. PwC also said the plan would upskill 65,000 PwC US people. The announcement describes a planned commitment rather than realized expenditure, and it should not be compared directly with global commitments from Accenture, Deloitte or Capgemini. [10]

**KPMG’s strongest primary amount is narrower.** KPMG LLP announced a US$100 million investment in its US Google Cloud practice in 2024 and estimated that the investment would drive US$1 billion in incremental growth. The amount covers a practice and alliance, not the global KPMG organization. KPMG’s 2023 generative-AI announcement did not state a dollar amount; a 2026 secondary comparison cites a broader multibillion-dollar Microsoft cloud-and-AI commitment, but the exact amount is not public and should not be merged with the US$100 million figure. [11][12]

**MBB disclosure is partnership-led rather than investment-led.** McKinsey, BCG and Bain do not disclose one comparable firm-wide AI-investment amount in the examined evidence. McKinsey and BCG announced multiyear OpenAI Frontier partnerships in February 2026, with financial details undisclosed. Bain disclosed an investment stake in the OpenAI Deployment Company launched with 19 global partners on 11 May 2026, but did not disclose the stake’s value. A monetary total for these firms would become determinable only if a dated filing or firm release separated AI-only spending, alliance commitments, acquisitions and equity stakes for the same reporting entity and period. [13][14][15]

**IBM’s disclosures are corporate, not consulting-investment totals.** IBM’s 2025 Annual Report reports more than US$8.3 billion in total R&D, more than US$12.5 billion in generative-AI book of business since inception, approximately US$4.5 billion in productivity savings since the beginning of 2023 and US$9.346 billion in completed 2025 acquisitions. None is an IBM Consulting AI-investment total. IBM defines its GenAI book of business through software transactional revenue, SaaS annual contract value and consulting signings, so it is a commercialization measure rather than an investment measure. [4]

### What can be normalized—and what cannot

**A simple annualized comparison is only an estimate.** Dividing the disclosed three-year commitments by three gives Accenture approximately US$1 billion per year, PwC US approximately US$333 million per year and Capgemini approximately €667 million per year; these are arithmetic estimates from announced commitments, not realized annual spending. Deloitte’s “through FY2030” horizon does not provide a sufficiently precise comparable start-and-end period for the same calculation.

**The investment intensity judgment is therefore bounded.** Accenture and Deloitte have the strongest clearly disclosed global commitment anchors. EY has the strongest explicit reported EY.ai capability-build total among the Big Four, but its related amounts cover multiple scopes. Capgemini’s reported global commitment is large but secondary-reported. PwC’s amount is substantial but US-only, and KPMG’s primary amount is a US alliance practice. Bain has demonstrated equity-backed ecosystem commitment, while McKinsey, BCG and IBM emphasize platforms, partnerships, acquisitions or client delivery without one headline AI-investment number.

**The ranking condition is clear.** A defensible ranking would require AI-only realized spending for the same fiscal period, reporting entity, currency basis and treatment of acquisitions and alliances. Until those conditions exist, the strongest answer is a scope-qualified ledger rather than a league table.

### Commercialization is more visible than spending

**Accenture has the clearest commercial output set.** A 2026 secondary synthesis of Accenture’s FY25 disclosures reports US$5.9 billion in AI bookings, approximately US$2.7 billion in Gen/Agentic AI revenue and approximately 6,000 AI deployments, up from a handful in 2023. The metrics cover bookings, revenue and deployments—not the same economic measure—but together they show a visible conversion of investment into commercial flow. [6]

**IBM reports a larger but broader commercial construct.** IBM’s GenAI book of business exceeded US$12.5 billion since inception, but IBM defines that measure through revenue, SaaS contract value and consulting signings. It signals substantial market commercialization, not AI profit, realized investment or IBM Consulting-only revenue. [4]

**BCG reports client value rather than firm revenue.** BCG reports more than US$30 billion in value realized for clients from applied AI and more than 6,000 AI practitioners. This is a client-value claim, not BCG revenue, investment or audited cash return. BCG’s metric is strategically important because it shows the firm’s preferred value narrative, but it cannot be placed above or below Accenture’s revenue without a common denominator. [5]

**The other firms expose scale through different signals.** Deloitte reports more than 500 incubator projects and more than 1,000 pre-built agents. EY reports more than 100 Assurance capabilities or enhancements since 2022 and more than 30 new or enhanced capabilities in 2025. KPMG reports more than 55,000 Gemini Enterprise professionals, nearly 700 internally created no-code agents and dozens of go-to-market-ready agents. PwC reports internal platform adoption and client case metrics. Bain reports internal tool adoption and client productivity. Capgemini reports a quantified client vignette and survey results. These are meaningful outputs, but they are not interchangeable.

**The commercial mechanism is reusable intellectual property.** Firms invest in model access, data engineering, governance, delivery platforms, acquisitions and training. They then package that investment into assets that can be reused across clients: agent libraries, model-selection tools, workflow templates, sector accelerators, managed services and internal assistants.

**Client zero is a common accelerator.** EY’s internal Copilot deployment, IBM’s internal AI-first transformation, KPMG’s Gemini Enterprise adoption, PwC’s firm-wide upskilling and Bain’s Claude rollout all create environments in which the consultancy can test tools before selling implementation. This lowers delivery risk and creates demonstrations, but internal adoption is still an internal output, not proof of client value.

**Ecosystems extend delivery capacity.** The firms rarely build the entire model and infrastructure stack themselves. They combine their own industry methods, data, governance and change-management capabilities with Microsoft, Google Cloud, AWS, NVIDIA, OpenAI, Anthropic, IBM, SAP, Salesforce, Palantir and other partners. The commercial difference lies less in whether a firm has an alliance than in whether the alliance is connected to a reusable delivery platform and measurable production work.

**Managed services make AI recurring rather than episodic.** IBM’s Intelligent Operations, Accenture’s model managed services and SynOps, EY Managed Services, Capgemini’s WNS integration and Deloitte’s operations and audit platforms extend AI beyond a strategy project. The underlying mechanism is a shift from “recommend and hand over” to “design, deploy, operate and optimize,” which creates recurring service revenue but also increases responsibility for monitoring, security and performance.

## Big Four: Deloitte, PwC, EY and KPMG

### Deloitte

**Deloitte’s investment is global and implementation-oriented.** On 25 March 2025, Deloitte announced US$3 billion in global Generative AI investment through fiscal 2030 and expanded its Global AI Simulation Center of Excellence, first launched in Bengaluru. The center is intended to use simulations, digital twins and multi-agent systems to improve decision-making, risk management and return on investment. [2]

Deloitte also reports more than US$2 billion in global technology learning and development initiatives through the Deloitte Technology Academy. That amount is broader than AI and should remain separate from the US$3 billion GenAI commitment. [16]

**The operating model is regionalized experimentation followed by scale.** On 15 April 2024, Deloitte expanded its GenAI incubator network to eight hubs in the United States, Canada, India, Egypt, Malaysia, Poland, Portugal and Romania. The hubs give clients local environments to experiment, develop, deploy and scale GenAI solutions; Deloitte reported more than 500 client projects through the network. [17]

Deloitte’s 2026 agentic practice with Google Cloud extends that model into end-to-end transformation. Announced on 22 April 2026, the practice combines Google Cloud technologies including Gemini Enterprise with Deloitte Ascend and services spanning strategy, process redesign, implementation, governance and adoption. Priority industries are retail, health care, financial services and government and public services. [18]

The conclusion would change if PwC’s announced AI studio and Agent OS programs do not convert design artifacts and internal adoption into independently measured production outcomes.

## IBM and IBM Consulting

### Corporate AI activity versus consulting delivery

### Why these application patterns recur

**Customer operations are the most accessible agentic entry point.** Customer-service workflows have high volumes, repeated intents, structured escalation paths and measurable outcomes such as handling time, transfers, satisfaction and sales. That explains PwC’s contact-center case, McKinsey’s Entel deployment and KPMG’s finance and procurement agents. Deloitte’s 2026 survey also identifies customer support as the area with the highest expected impact for agentic AI. [20]

**Finance and assurance reward controlled data.** Finance, tax and audit contain structured documents, rules, approvals and audit trails. EY’s Assurance Knowledge, KPMG’s pricing-dispute agent, Deloitte Omnia and IBM’s governed platform all exploit that structure. The trade-off is that accuracy, explainability, segregation of duties and human review matter more than raw generation speed.

**Software engineering is a bridge between copilots and agents.** Code documentation, testing, conversion and legacy-code modernization have clear artifacts and relatively fast feedback. Accenture’s switchboard, EY’s Mercedes-Benz case, KPMG’s Intelligent Modernization Toolkit, Bain’s legacy-code work and Capgemini’s FinTech case therefore show some of the earliest measurable results.

**Physical operations require data and process integration.** John Deere’s See & Spray, Rio Tinto’s scheduling platform and Deloitte’s power-and-utilities simulations demonstrate that AI value comes from connecting models to sensors, equipment, workflows and human decisions. The mechanism is not a chatbot; it is a closed or semi-closed sense, decide and act loop.

**Design artifacts precede financial returns.** PepsiCo’s process map and 17 agentic loops, Deloitte’s agent libraries, EY’s process flows and governance artifacts, and BCG’s sector products are outputs in their own right. They reduce uncertainty and create reusable architecture, but they should not be reported as cost savings or revenue unless a measured result exists.

**Financial statements are the strongest evidence for corporate scale.** IBM’s annual report provides audited corporate revenue, R&D, acquisition spending and defined book-of-business measures. **Internal adoption measures show readiness rather than value.** PwC’s internal user reach, KPMG’s 55,000 professionals, EY’s 150,000 Copilot users, Bain’s 7,000 Claude pilot users and Deloitte’s 25,000 Gemini users show deployment capacity. They do not establish external client performance.

### Strategic posture

| Capgemini | Resonance, RAISE, WNS Intelligent Operations and five value pools | Trust by design, governance, data protection and reliable engineering | Supply chain, finance, HR, customer operations and sovereign cloud | Google Cloud, AWS, Microsoft, OpenAI, Mistral AI and SAP |

**The common direction is workflow embedding.** All ten firms are moving from chat interfaces and isolated pilots toward agents connected to enterprise data, applications, controls and operating processes. The practical implication is that a consulting buyer should evaluate data integration, process redesign, monitoring, human escalation and ownership—not only model quality.

**Deloitte and KPMG emphasize governed execution.** Their audit and risk heritage shapes platforms such as Omnia, Workbench, Trusted AI and forward-deployed engineering. This suits regulated sectors, but the model depends on turning governance controls into usable delivery routines rather than additional approval layers.

**PwC emphasizes work redesign.** Its AI-studio recommendation and 20%/80% value rule place operating-model change above software acquisition. This increases the importance of leadership, role redesign and workforce trust, but it also makes results more dependent on client organizational change.

**EY emphasizes assurance-grade foundations.** EY combines governed data, Assurance technology, Microsoft engineering, NVIDIA infrastructure and client-zero internal deployments. The implication is a stronger proposition for finance, tax, audit, risk and regulated use cases where traceability and confidence matter.

**Accenture emphasizes industrialization.** Its combination of platforms, acquisitions, managed services, model customization and commercial metrics suggests the most vertically integrated route from strategy to operating AI. That scale can accelerate delivery but may make the buyer more dependent on Accenture’s ecosystem and proprietary assets.

**McKinsey and BCG emphasize transformation logic.** McKinsey’s Rewired model and BCG’s 10-20-70 model explain why adoption, process change and human capability determine value. Their strategic advantage is diagnosis and redesign; their risk is that the boundary between method, advisory and measurable delivery remains less visible than in a product-led platform.

**Bain emphasizes partner-enabled deployment.** OpenAI and Anthropic relationships give Bain access to frontier models and enterprise deployment resources. Its differentiation is co-design and production enablement rather than ownership of a single platform.

**IBM emphasizes control over the enterprise stack.** Hybrid cloud, watsonx, infrastructure, data, consulting and managed operations allow IBM to connect AI to mission-critical environments. The implication is strong fit for organizations where sovereignty, security, integration and long-lived systems outweigh rapid experimentation.

**Capgemini emphasizes operations and sovereignty.** The WNS acquisition, Resonance framework, RAISE platform and sovereign-cloud partnerships position Capgemini around agentic operations at scale. The opportunity is broad process transformation; the risk is integration complexity after a large acquisition and the need to convert survey-led market momentum into audited client results.

**The counts are signals, not a ranking.** A practitioner, technical employee, credential, course completion, learner enrollment, platform user, pilot participant and trained employee represent different denominators. Capgemini’s 310,000 GenAI trainees cannot be directly compared with Accenture’s 77,000 AI-and-data professionals or BCG’s 6,000 AI practitioners.

**Deloitte and Capgemini emphasize breadth.** Deloitte’s Academy reaches more than 120,000 professionals, while Capgemini reports hundreds of thousands trained in GenAI and Agentic AI. Their opportunity is widespread fluency; their execution risk is that broad access may not create enough specialist talent for architecture, evaluation, security and agent operations.

**Accenture and BCG emphasize specialist capacity.** Accenture combines a large AI-and-data workforce with NVIDIA training, Stanford certification and acquisitions. BCG combines practitioners with BCG X engineers, data scientists, designers and product managers. Their operating advantage is deeper build capability; their measurement challenge is defining who counts as an AI specialist.

**EY and KPMG link learning to governed internal deployment.** EY’s AI Now, badges, Copilot deployment and Assurance platform create a connection between learning and daily workflows. KPMG’s Gemini Enterprise adoption and no-code agent creation create a similar feedback loop. In both cases, access and use are stronger evidence than completion or post-training productivity.

**IBM and PwC have the largest explicit access ambitions.** IBM’s 2-million-learner target is external and global, while PwC’s 65,000-person target is PwC US and its later 90%-plus participation figure is worldwide. Neither measure is equivalent to the number of production-capable consultants.

**The future execution question is role transition.** Deloitte identifies insufficient worker skills as the biggest barrier to integrating AI into workflows. Accenture reports that only 5% of organizations were reskilling at scale despite 94% of workers saying they were ready to learn new GenAI skills. Capgemini’s survey expects 63% of employees to experience a role transition by 2027–2028. These figures are survey-based forecasts, but they point to the same constraint: talent development must change job design, governance and incentives, not only teach prompt use.

### A practical shortlist for business and technology leaders

| Reader need | Firms and offerings to inspect first | Evidence to examine | Selection question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enterprise-scale platforms and managed AI deployment | Accenture AI Navigator and AI Refinery; IBM watsonx and Consulting Advantage | Deployment definitions, managed-service scope, model choice and governance | Who owns the platform, data, models and operating capability after implementation? |
| Governed audit, tax, risk and regulated workflows | Deloitte Omnia and Trustworthy AI; EY Assurance Knowledge and EY Canvas; KPMG Workbench | Control design, audit trails, human review and production quality metrics | How are errors, overrides, bias and regulatory changes monitored? |
| Operating-model redesign and workforce adoption | PwC AI studio, PwC One and Agent OS; McKinsey Rewired; BCG 10-20-70 | Process redesign, role changes, learning completion and adoption measures | Which people-and-process changes are contractually included in delivery? |
| Agentic operations and sovereign ecosystems | Capgemini Resonance, RAISE and WNS; IBM hybrid-by-design; EY Agentic Platform | Data residency, partner dependencies, orchestration, observability and cost per inference | Which workloads require proprietary, open or sovereign models? |
| Broad AI learning and reskilling access | IBM SkillsBuild; Capgemini digital campuses; Deloitte AI Academy; PwC workforce journeys | Completion, certification, role application and post-training performance | How does training connect to production work and career progression? |

**The buyer should compare evidence maturity before comparing percentages.** A named client with a baseline and a production date is stronger than an unnamed case with a large percentage. A measured internal productivity result is stronger than a forecast, but weaker than an independently designed client study. A financial filing is strongest for corporate scale, but it may say little about a specific consulting implementation.

The firms’ disclosed output categories can be read as a sequence:

1. **Capability formation:** investments, acquisitions, partnerships and training.
2. **Reusable assets:** platforms, methods, agents, model catalogs and process libraries.
3. **Internal deployment:** client-zero use, workforce access and tool adoption.
4. **Client implementation:** pilots, proofs of concept and production workflows.
5. **Measured outcome:** time, cost, quality, revenue, productivity or customer metrics.
6. **Independent validation:** external evaluation, controlled studies or assurance.

Most firms have reached the second and third stages across a broad portfolio. Accenture, IBM and selected Big Four practices disclose more commercial and internal deployment evidence. Named client outcomes are increasing, but independent validation remains concentrated at market level rather than individual-firm case level.

The figures use different definitions, units, currencies, reporting entities and measurement bases. Investment amounts may be announced commitments, reported capability build, alliance or practice spending, acquisitions, equity stakes or broader R&D; bookings, revenue, book of business and client value are separate constructs. Workforce figures may count learners, users, credentials, practitioners, technical staff or training participants. Many case metrics are firm- or client-reported, some clients are unnamed, several results are projected or survey-based, and announcements, pilots, internal deployments, production implementations and independently evaluated outcomes are not equivalent. Capgemini Research Institute statistics describe surveyed organizations rather than Capgemini spending or audited client returns, and McKinsey, BCG and IBM do not disclose one comparable firm-wide AI-investment total in the examined evidence. The comparison is current through 13 September 2026; a like-for-like ranking would require common periods, scopes, realized AI-only spending, standardized production definitions and independent outcome measurement.

## Closing judgment

The global consulting market has moved beyond selling AI as a presentation topic. The leading firms now sell combinations of models, data foundations, agents, governance, operating-model redesign, implementation and managed operations.

Accenture is the clearest scale-and-commercialization benchmark. Deloitte, EY and KPMG are strongest where trust, assurance and regulated workflows dominate. PwC is distinctive in linking enterprise AI to work redesign. McKinsey and BCG remain strategy-plus-build firms whose principal contribution is transformation architecture and value capture. Bain is using frontier-model partnerships to accelerate deployment. IBM combines AI products, infrastructure and consulting at unusual depth. Capgemini is building an agentic-operations and sovereign-ecosystem position.

The practical conclusion is not that one firm leads every category. It is that the choice should follow the client’s bottleneck: platform industrialization, regulated control, operating-model change, sector-specific production, hybrid-cloud integration, workforce transformation or business-process operations. The firms’ investment announcements create capacity; their reusable assets create commercialization; their client deployments create evidence; and their talent and governance programs determine whether that evidence becomes durable enterprise value.
