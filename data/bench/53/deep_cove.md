# How the World’s Wealthiest Governments Invest

**Different wealth, different investment strategies:** Wealthy governments invest to preserve national savings, finance retirement, develop industries, and build public infrastructure. Norway’s oil fund emphasizes diversified foreign securities; Singapore and Abu Dhabi divide responsibilities among distinct investment institutions; Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund combines financial returns with domestic transformation; Japan invests pension reserves; and the United States and Germany deploy substantial capital through budgets and public financing institutions. Institutional purpose explains these differences better than fund size alone. [1]

**Two meanings of government wealth:** Economic and fiscal capacity—the ability to generate revenue, borrow, and sustain expenditure—is different from ownership of investment assets. This comparison keeps those dimensions separate and assesses investment strategies through **13 September 2026**.

## Which governments count as the wealthiest?

### Economic capacity and investment wealth answer different questions

**Economic scale measures productive capacity:** GDP measures an economy’s annual production, not government-owned wealth. The World Bank’s current-US-dollar GDP series provides an appropriate economic-scale concept, while government receipts, debt, and financing arrangements describe the public sector’s ability to mobilize resources. [11]

**Net financial wealth measures ownership:** The OECD defines general-government net financial worth as financial assets minus outstanding liabilities, expressed as a percentage of GDP. Its perimeter includes central, state, and local governments and social-security funds. A government can therefore own a large investment portfolio while also carrying substantial debt or pension obligations. [12]

**Fiscal anchors illustrate the distinction:** The United States’ proposed FY2025 budget used GDP of US$29.340 trillion, receipts of US$5.485 trillion, expenditure of US$7.266 trillion, and a deficit of US$1.781 trillion. These figures establish enormous fiscal scale, but not a surplus available for sovereign saving. Debt held by the public was projected at US$29.984 trillion, or 102.2% of GDP, under that particular budget forecast. [13]

**Germany illustrates borrowing-backed investment capacity:** Its fiscal plan starts from general-government debt of 62.5% of GDP in 2024 and authorizes up to €500 billion through the Special Fund for Infrastructure and Climate Neutrality. Its preliminary 2025 federal accounts recorded €388.6 billion of tax revenue and €66.9 billion of net borrowing. These are fiscal resources and financing flows, rather than investment-fund assets. [14][15]

**Other cases enter through public assets:** Norway, Singapore, Abu Dhabi, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Kuwait are selected principally because their governments control exceptionally large investment pools. China combines economic scale with reserve-derived investment capital; Japan adds a major public-pension model. Their fiscal signals include petroleum or LNG surpluses, reserve-backed budget arrangements, state capital contributions, and pension financing—not an assumption that each government ranks similarly by GDP.

## What government investment includes

### Seven categories, seven accounting treatments

**Government investment extends beyond sovereign funds:** A government can acquire financial assets, build a railway, capitalize a company, guarantee a loan, or finance research. These actions involve different ownership rights, risks, and accounting units. The IMF’s standard direct-investment measure is general-government gross fixed capital formation, or GFCF: acquisitions minus disposals of fixed assets during an accounting period. [17]

| Category | Institutional vehicle | Main capital source | Treatment in this comparison | Measurement unit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct public capital spending | Ministries and subnational governments | Taxes and borrowing | Include qualifying capital expenditure | Annual spending flow |
| Sovereign wealth funds | Government-owned investment funds | Resource revenue, surpluses, invested reserves | Include financial investment portfolios | AUM or fund-value stock |
| Public pension reserves | Statutory pension-reserve institutions | Earmarked pension resources and investment income | Include separately from sovereign savings | Reserve-asset stock |
| Foreign-exchange reserves | Central banks and reserve managers | Foreign-currency accumulation | Separate monetary reserves from long-term invested reserves | Reserve stock |
| Development banks | Public promotional-finance institutions | Bonds, public equity, budget and delegated funds | Include loans, equity, and commitments separately | Assets and financing flows |
| State enterprises and holding companies | Government-controlled corporations | Transferred holdings, retained earnings, borrowing, capital injections | Include investment holdings and capital formation | Equity value, assets, or capital spending |
| Ordinary government expenditure | General budgets | Taxes and borrowing | Exclude current transfers, interest, and routine consumption | Annual expenditure flow |

The distinctions follow the IMF accounting perimeter [17], IFSWF sovereign-fund definition [18], Singapore’s institutional taxonomy [19], and KfW’s financial reporting [8].

**Sovereign funds have a narrower definition:** The Santiago Principles definition centers on government ownership, investment in foreign financial assets, and financial objectives. It distinguishes these funds from public pensions ultimately serving beneficiaries and central-bank reserves serving monetary purposes. A broad study of government investment should include those neighboring institutions, but should not relabel them all as sovereign wealth funds. [18]

**National budget definitions also differ:** The US federal-investment classification extends beyond physical capital to research and development, education, training, and the subsidy value of qualifying investment-purpose loans and guarantees. It excludes healthcare and social-service expenditure when current and future benefits cannot readily be separated. This is an accounting boundary, not a judgment that such services lack long-term value. [7]

**Credit support needs separate accounting:** A US$1 billion loan is not necessarily US$1 billion of budget expenditure: the budget may recognize its subsidy cost instead. Likewise, development-bank lending creates a financial claim that can be repaid, whereas a grant generally does not. Comparing their face values without identifying the instrument exaggerates or obscures the public resources at risk.

**GFCF captures only one channel:** The IMF explains that its general-government measure leaves out some investment grants, guarantees, tax concessions, development-bank operations, state-enterprise infrastructure, and public–private partnerships outside the government accounting perimeter. A country can therefore support considerable investment without recording all of it as government fixed-capital formation. [17]

### Stocks, flows, and ownership must stay separate

**An asset stock is accumulated wealth:** Norway’s fund value and Temasek’s net portfolio value describe holdings at a date. Annual investment expenditure describes activity during a period. The same government can spend on infrastructure while holding financial assets, but adding its annual spending to its fund balance produces no meaningful total.

**Bank assets are not owner equity:** KfW reported €540.7 billion of consolidated assets and €40.6 billion of equity at December 2025. Its €580.5 billion “volume of lending” uses another reporting perimeter and is not the amount lent during 2025. The €98 billion of promotional business is an annual activity measure. [8]

**Consolidation can create double counting:** A holding company’s value may already incorporate the subsidiaries it owns. Adding those subsidiaries’ full assets would count some economic interests twice and could include financing supplied by private creditors or minority shareholders. PIF’s audited accounts explicitly eliminate intragroup balances and distinguish subsidiaries, associates, and joint ventures. [4]

**Section takeaway:** Before comparing amounts, identify whether the government owns an asset, supplies financing, guarantees a risk, or spends a budget—and whether the reported number is a stock or a flow.

## How funding determines investment behavior

### Resource revenue: save abroad or build at home?

**Resource exporters face a conversion problem:** Petroleum and gas receipts exchange a finite underground resource for financial income. Saving part of that income in diversified assets can preserve purchasing power after extraction declines and reduce dependence on commodity prices. Norway, Kuwait, Qatar, and Abu Dhabi illustrate different institutional responses to this problem. [20]

**Foreign investment reduces domestic concentration:** Norway’s GPFG invests abroad, separating national financial wealth from the domestic economy that generates petroleum-related revenue. ADIA also receives funds that Abu Dhabi allocates above fiscal requirements and invests globally. The mechanism is balance-sheet diversification: domestic economic weakness need not coincide perfectly with losses across foreign assets. [21][22]

**Domestic development reverses that emphasis:** PIF and Mubadala deliberately use state capital to create local industries, infrastructure, and commercial platforms. This can diversify the *production base* while concentrating the *financial portfolio* domestically. Those outcomes are compatible: building non-oil businesses may improve the economy’s industrial mix even while increasing the fund’s exposure to one country’s construction cycle, regulation, and demand.

**Withdrawal rules determine usable patience:** Kuwait’s Future Generations Fund reinvests income and requires legal sanction for withdrawals, supporting a long horizon. Its General Reserve Fund finances expenditure and smooths fiscal shocks. ADIA, meanwhile, must return funds to its government as needed. These distinctions explain why a savings institution still needs liquidity management. [23][22]

### Reserves and pensions carry different obligations

**Invested reserves seek purchasing-power growth:** GIC aims to preserve and enhance the international purchasing power of Singapore’s reserves. CIC was created to diversify China’s foreign-exchange investments and seek returns within acceptable risk. Both move beyond the liquidity-first function associated with conventional monetary reserves, although their funding arrangements and ownership structures differ. [24][5]

**Singapore pools assets and obligations:** GIC manages a combined government pool that includes funds associated with Special Singapore Government Securities and Central Provident Fund obligations, alongside substantial unencumbered assets. Singapore’s Ministry of Finance explains that this broader balance sheet permits long-term investment while the government meets committed CPF interest rates. GIC’s investment result is therefore not simply the return credited to individual retirement accounts. [25]

**Budget rules can protect investment horizons:** Singapore’s Net Investment Return framework bases permitted reserve-supported spending on expected long-term real returns. It does not compel investment entities to sell assets or realize gains merely to finance the current budget. The mechanism separates a government spending rule from short-term portfolio cash generation. [19]

**Pension reserves serve identified benefits:** GPIF’s mandate concerns stable future pension payments. Its risk tolerance must therefore reflect retirement financing, rather than a government’s desire to acquire strategic businesses. The fund’s 1.9% real investment-return objective is a policy requirement within that pension framework, not a promise of an annual market result. [26]

### Borrowing and transfers are financing, not free wealth

**CIC shows the financing chain clearly:** Its initial US$200 billion capitalization involved foreign-exchange reserves purchased from the central bank against RMB1.55 trillion of Ministry of Finance sovereign bonds. The transaction created an investment institution, but it also involved government financing obligations. Its original registered capital is consequently different from its current assets or net assets. [27]

**PIF uses several capital sources:** Its audited accounts identify divestment proceeds, dividends, investment distributions, borrowing, government asset transfers, and government capital contributions. During 2025, contributions received totaled SAR54.184 billion, while a separate owner-directed transfer returned SAR56.502 billion of net assets to the Ministry of Finance. The net movement from those two items was minus SAR2.318 billion. [4]

**Asset transfers change institutional scale:** In 2024, the Saudi government transferred an 8% ownership interest in Aramco to two wholly owned PIF subsidiaries. Such a transfer can enlarge the fund’s holdings without representing a new investment return or a corresponding increase in consolidated national wealth. Subsequent dividends can support investment, but remain linked to the underlying company’s economics. [4]

**Tax-funded investment has an opportunity cost:** The United States and Germany finance public investment principally through taxation and borrowing. Their relevant test is whether infrastructure, research, or other benefits justify fiscal costs and risks—not whether the government can report a larger portfolio. KfW adds a revolving financing model in which repayments and retained earnings support further promotional activity. [28][8]

**Section takeaway:** Funding matters because it determines both the government’s opportunity cost and the obligations that constrain liquidity, risk-taking, and investment horizons.

## The principal government investment models

### Norway: a foreign savings portfolio

**Vehicle, capital, and purpose:** Norway’s Government Pension Fund Global held NOK21.268 trillion at December 2025. Built from petroleum revenue, its mandate is to safeguard and build financial wealth for future generations while seeking the highest return after costs at acceptable risk. The government first transferred capital in May 1996. [2][29]

**Allocation and governance model:** The Ministry of Finance determines strategy and the benchmark; Norges Bank, through Norges Bank Investment Management, manages the portfolio operationally. Its predominantly listed foreign investments make Norway the clearest example here of diversified financial saving rather than domestic industrial ownership. The “Pension Fund” name does not make it equivalent to GPIF’s benefit-oriented pension reserve.

**Reason for inclusion:** Norway demonstrates how a resource-exporting government can separate the source of national income from the assets held for future generations. That judgment would change if domestic development or short-notice fiscal financing became the dominant portfolio mandate.

### Singapore: reserve management and equity ownership

**GIC manages government-owned reserves:** Its scale is estimated at approximately US$800 billion around March 2025. Its purpose is long-term purchasing-power preservation, and its portfolio spans public and private markets. Government sets the acceptable risk; professional management makes investment decisions. [9][24]

**Temasek owns corporate investment assets:** Incorporated in 1974 and wholly owned through Singapore’s Minister for Finance, Temasek reported S$518 billion of net portfolio value at March 2026. Unlike GIC’s management relationship, Temasek owns the assets on its own balance sheet and seeks long-term shareholder value. [30]

**The institutional split is consequential:** GIC provides broad reserve diversification; Temasek combines Singapore-based portfolio companies with global direct investment and partnerships. From April 2026, Temasek organized these activities through Temasek Singapore, Temasek Global Investments, and Temasek Partnership Solutions. Singapore is included because one government can rationally maintain different investment models rather than force all public capital into one fund. [30]

### Abu Dhabi: global savings and entrepreneurial investment

**ADIA represents diversified financial investment:** Established in 1976, ADIA receives Abu Dhabi funds allocated above fiscal requirements and seeks sustainable long-term returns. Its approximately US$1.1 trillion scale is an industry estimate. Its board is appointed by Abu Dhabi’s Supreme Council for Financial and Economic Affairs. [22][9]

**Mubadala combines returns and industrial development:** Its reported end-2025 AUM was approximately US$385 billion. Its strategy emphasizes private assets, direct minority co-investments, and sectors including energy, advanced technology, infrastructure, healthcare, consumer businesses, and private credit. Its shareholder is the Government of Abu Dhabi. [9][31]

**Ownership roles distinguish the pair:** ADIA describes itself as a financial investor that does not seek operational control of infrastructure assets. Mubadala’s domestic role includes national champions, competitive industries, and high-value jobs; GlobalFoundries illustrates its entrepreneurial technology involvement. The pair shows why “Gulf sovereign fund” is too broad a label to predict investment behavior. [22][32]

## Who makes the decisions?

### Ownership, independence, and oversight

**Political ownership and operational discretion coexist:** Governments legitimately determine what public capital is for. The governance challenge is to distinguish that democratic or sovereign ownership role from individual investment selection, while making managers accountable for risks and results. Formal independence is therefore an institutional arrangement, not proof that political considerations never matter.

| Institution | Owner or mandate setter | Operational responsibility | Oversight and accountability | Principal investment control |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| GIC | Singapore Government | Professional investment management | Government reviews total risk and performance | Client risk appetite; strategic portfolio framework |
| ADIA | Abu Dhabi Government | Investment organization and management | Appointed board and defined internal responsibilities | Strategic allocation bands; liquidity oversight |
| QIA | Qatari state approval chain | Management and investment teams | Board and Supreme Council approval | Reference Portfolio; risk/liquidity limits |

**Norway makes active risk measurable:** Its mandate caps expected tracking error—the expected variability of returns relative to the benchmark—at 1.25 percentage points. The reported year-end 2025 figure was 0.37 percentage point. This constrains departures from the public strategy without pretending to eliminate market risk. [2]

**Singapore distinguishes ownership from deal selection:** Its Ministry of Finance states that government sets mandates, appoints boards, and reviews whole-portfolio risk without directing individual investments. GIC management makes investment decisions despite ministerial board membership; Temasek’s board has no government representation. These are official descriptions of decision rights, while government remains responsible for the institutions’ overall purposes. [19]

**Kuwait separates oversight from public disclosure:** KIA describes board-level independence, external audit, and semiannual AUM statements to the State Audit Bureau. Its governing law restricts public dissemination of information. Consequently, confidential institutional accountability and public transparency should be assessed as separate attributes. [37]

**Strategic mandates embed political choices:** PIF’s domestic transformation role and Mubadala’s national-champion objectives are part of their stated purposes. Stephan Roll’s February 2026 SWP paper argues that close institutional and personnel links in Gulf investment systems can make commercial and foreign-policy motives difficult to distinguish. This is a governance concern, not evidence that every investment lacks commercial logic. [32]

### Benchmarks, managers, and operating constraints

**Reference portfolios serve different functions:** GIC’s former 65% global-equity/35% global-bond Reference Portfolio expressed the client’s risk appetite; GIC explicitly said it was not its performance benchmark. From 2026, its refreshed framework uses a Strategic Portfolio reflecting client risk appetite and long-term return expectations. QIA’s Reference Portfolio similarly connects return objectives with risk and liquidity constraints, but its particular role must be read on its own terms. [24][36]

**Manager models determine costs and control:** ADIA reported 57% internal and 43% external management in 2025, alongside 63% active management and 37% index replication. KIA says it invests mostly through external managers, while Mubadala combines direct co-investment with an endowment arm investing mainly through asset managers. These choices trade internal staffing and governance demands against external fees and specialist access. [41][23][31]

**Partnerships do not confer identical rights:** PIF’s SoftBank Vision Fund interest illustrates the distinction between supplying capital and controlling its deployment. Its accounts identify PIF as a limited partner, with the general partner setting financial and operating policies and PIF holding limited or no removal rights. Direct ownership, co-investment, and a fund commitment therefore expose a government to different governance risks. [4]

**Environmental policies also differ in function:** Norway uses stewardship, ethics, climate dialogue, and risk-based divestment. Temasek targets net-zero portfolio emissions by 2050, but its 2026 report says it is unlikely to achieve its interim 2030 halving target because of hard-to-abate portfolio concentration and transition considerations. KfW applies Paris-aligned sector guidelines, while acknowledging that government-mandated transactions can depart from standard restrictions in individual cases. [44][30][8]

**Risk taxonomies need substantive interpretation:** CIC’s self-assessment covers market, credit, liquidity, country, legal, geopolitical, cybersecurity, compliance, and climate risks. It uses an 11-category taxonomy in one passage and a 12-category taxonomy elsewhere. Counting categories is consequently less informative than examining exposure limits, escalation procedures, and the consequences of adverse scenarios. [27]

**Voluntary principles are a baseline:** The Santiago Principles comprise 24 voluntarily endorsed practices addressing governance, accountability, prudent investment, and operational controls. They provide a useful common language, but their endorsement does not substitute for audited accounts, measurable risk limits, or evidence about individual decision rights. [45]

**Section takeaway:** Strong governance connects public ownership to clear mandates, professional implementation, measurable risk, and credible oversight; adopting an institutional label or voluntary code is only the starting point.

### Asset growth is not investment return

**Norway’s balance grew more slowly:** Fund value rose by NOK1.526 trillion during 2025, from NOK19.742 trillion to NOK21.268 trillion, equivalent to approximately 7.7% asset growth. That differs from the 15.1% investment return because fund value also reflects government flows and exchange-rate effects. In the first half of 2026, fund value reached NOK22.683 trillion. [2][44]

**Temasek’s valuation transition matters:** March 2025 NPV was S$434 billion under the earlier reporting basis but S$469 billion on a mark-to-market basis. Comparing March 2026’s S$518 billion with S$469 billion gives approximately 10.4% like-basis value growth: S$49 billion divided by S$469 billion. Comparing it with S$434 billion would instead produce 19.4%, mixing valuation bases. [47][30]

**Currency changes the investor’s experience:** Temasek’s one-year TSR to March 2026 was 10.5% in Singapore dollars, 14.8% in US dollars, and 12.9% at constant currency. The same underlying investment institution thus generated materially different headline returns depending on the measurement currency. Its 10-year SGD TSR was 7.1%, compared with the 20-year figure of 6.8%. [3]

**GPIF’s accounts permit a useful bridge:** Investment assets rose from ¥249,782.1 billion at March 2025 to ¥293,643.7 billion at March 2026, an increase of ¥43,861.6 billion, or approximately 17.6%. Subtracting reported investment gains of ¥41,399.5 billion leaves an estimated ¥2,462.1 billion residual for net transfers and other accounting movements. That residual is not a separately measured pension-contribution inflow. [6]

**Estimates can supplement official long-run results:** Global SWF estimated ADIA’s end-2025 AUM at US$1.13 trillion, versus US$1.03 trillion a year earlier, and estimated a one-year return of 8.9%. The asset increase is approximately 9.7%, calculated as US$0.10 trillion divided by US$1.03 trillion. These estimates should remain distinct from ADIA’s own provisional 20- and 30-year returns. [48]

**Mubadala’s measures use different horizons:** Industry summaries report 10.7% annually over five years and 10.3% over ten years; both can be true. Its reported 17% AUM growth to US$385 billion implies an estimated prior-year value near US$329 billion—US$385 billion divided by 1.17—and an increase near US$56 billion. Neither the balance-sheet increase nor either return period establishes superiority over a different mandate. [9][46]

**PIF’s accounts explain conflicting headlines:** Its annual report highlights more than doubled net profit, while secondary reporting records declining AUM and a 5.8% shareholder return. Its consolidated accounts include operating companies and banking businesses; profit, comprehensive income, asset transfers, and portfolio returns therefore capture different things. A reported SAR38 billion AUM decline is a balance change, not a 2025 investment-loss percentage. [42][43]

**Costs require the same accounting perimeter:** PIF’s audited 2025 investment-advisory expenses were SAR2.087 billion, within SAR2.189 billion of investment-operation cost of revenue. These are specific expense items, not an all-inclusive management-fee ratio. For comparison, GPFG’s 0.07% is a long-run annual management-cost measure, while GIC reports net-of-cost portfolio returns and GPIF’s cited returns are gross. [4][29]

### Crowding in is conditional, not automatic

**Capital mobilization has a counterfactual:** Private co-investment alongside a sovereign investor is evidence of participation, but not automatically evidence that the private investment would otherwise have disappeared. Public finance genuinely crowds in capital when it resolves a financing, risk, infrastructure, or coordination problem. It can crowd out capital when subsidized terms displace viable private financing or privileged state enterprises restrict competition.

**The empirical evidence is heterogeneous:** Bruegel’s synthesis reports different effects for different investment concepts and institutional settings.

| Evidence | Change examined | Reported result | Meaning for this comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public-capital meta-analyses | 1% more public capital | 0.12–0.16% higher long-run output | Capital-stock association, not a fund return |
| Infrastructure meta-analysis | 1% more infrastructure investment | Output effect from −0.06% to +0.52% | Outcomes vary substantially |
| Italian subsidy evaluation | Beneficiaries versus similar ineligible firms | Investment +39%; employment +17% over six years | Program-specific evidence of additional activity |
| PIF study, 2018–2023 | AUM increases and GDP growth | Reported correlation R = 0.46 | Co-movement, not identified causation |

Sources: Bruegel’s literature synthesis [50] and Kasasbeh’s PIF study [51].

**The PIF study needs careful interpretation:** Kasasbeh also reports a 45% rise in non-oil FDI and a 30% increase in domestic job creation over 2018–2023. These are reported changes during the investment period, rather than estimates against an identified national counterfactual. Oil conditions, other fiscal policies, economic reforms, and broader recovery could also affect the same outcomes. [51]

**State ownership carries agency costs:** Borisova’s 2025 study compared 392 acquisitions by 129 wholly government-owned investment funds with 67,072 private-company acquisitions over 1983–2023. Government funds tended toward foreign targets, smaller stakes, and lower target returns, although sovereign wealth enterprises were an important exception, particularly domestically. The finding concerns acquisition transactions and target-market responses, not total portfolio returns. [52]

**The policy judgment is conditional:** Public capital is most persuasive when it finances a clearly identified gap, imposes commercial discipline where appropriate, and measures delivery against alternatives. The case weakens when a project’s strategic label protects it from cost scrutiny, competition, or exit. A high financial return does not prove additional development benefits, and a development mandate does not excuse unlimited financial losses.

### Financial influence and geopolitical exposure

**Large ownership creates international influence:** A diversified investor such as GPFG can influence corporate practices through voting and engagement without seeking operational control. In the first half of 2026, NBIM reported voting on approximately 76,000 proposals at 7,000 shareholder meetings and conducting company dialogue under its climate strategy. [44]

**Strategic ownership creates different influence:** Mubadala’s technology and industrial investments, QIA’s global corporate and property investments, and PIF’s international company stakes connect public capital with commercial networks and policy relationships. **Geopolitics can restrict diversification:** Country risk, sanctions, investment screening, and technology restrictions can reduce the set of feasible holdings or partners. CIC explicitly incorporates country and geopolitical risks into its framework, while KfW reviews political risks and country exposure. **Foreign saving also has domestic benefits:** Norway’s model contributes to national financial resilience without directly selecting domestic industrial winners. GIC similarly supports Singapore’s purchasing power and reserve-backed spending capacity. These effects differ from jobs at a state-backed factory, but remain economically important: diversification protects the resources available to future governments and citizens.

**Section takeaway:** Government investment can create infrastructure, industries, and influence, but its economic value depends on additionality, governance, and the fiscal risks accepted—not simply the amount deployed.

**Observed institutional changes support the direction:** ADIA raised its private-equity range to 15–20%, while Temasek’s 2026 restructuring distinguished domestic stewardship, global direct investing, and partnerships. These are related adaptations, not evidence that every government is converging on the same allocation. [41][30][24][34]

**Larger transactions change execution demands:** IE University recorded 391 direct-investment transactions over July 2024–December 2025, compared with 473 in the preceding report, while aggregate transaction value increased from US$211 billion to US$404 billion. Those changes imply approximately 17.3% fewer transactions and 91.5% greater aggregate value, calculated from the reported endpoints. In syndicated deals, the value generally represents the whole transaction rather than the sovereign investor’s own contribution. [16]

**Co-investment changes the capability requirement:** Investing alongside private-equity firms can provide access and reduce some intermediation costs, but large investors still need independent diligence, valuation, legal expertise, and the ability to decline attractive-looking deals. The IFSWF’s earlier private-market survey ranked transparency and illiquidity ahead of fees and internal resources as perceived challenges. Those mechanisms remain relevant even as the institutions and transaction sizes evolve. [54]

### AI creates a connected investment theme

**Compute investment extends beyond technology shares:** AI requires data centers, semiconductors, electricity generation, transmission networks, and other infrastructure. The IFSWF’s 2026 review links these assets and finds sovereign direct investment becoming more geographically concentrated between 2018 and 2025, while remaining spread across industries. [55]

**Different assets can share one risk:** A portfolio may appear diversified across chips, data centers, power, and private credit while depending on the same assumptions about AI demand and capital expenditure. Conversely, some regulated energy or infrastructure assets may retain value even if particular technology companies disappoint. Contract terms, counterparties, financing, and alternative uses determine how much economic diversification those investments actually provide.

**Sovereign venture often scales established leaders:** IE’s 2020–2025 sample covered 1,171 sovereign-backed venture deals. Only 3% represented investment before companies reached unicorn status, while 15.8% were allocations after that recognition. This suggests an important role in financing scale rather than consistently identifying unknown early-stage winners. [16]

**Investment potential is not existing wealth:** IE describes MGX’s reported US$50 billion scale as target or investment potential incorporating third-party capital and debt mobilization, rather than independently reported sovereign AUM. The same distinction applies to large infrastructure partnerships: a financing ambition, an equity commitment, and a completed asset portfolio are different quantities. [16]

### Explicit scenarios show the main constraints

**Norway publishes substantial downside scenarios:** Its first-half 2026 presentation modeled fund-value shocks of −35% for an AI correction, −37% for a fragmented world, −32% for a regional debt crisis, and −20% for an extreme-weather event. These are stress tests, not predicted losses. [44]

| Published stress scenario | Modeled fund-value shock | Estimated loss on NOK22.683 trillion | Estimated remaining value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| AI correction | −35% | NOK7.94 trillion | NOK14.74 trillion |
| Fragmented world | −37% | NOK8.39 trillion | NOK14.29 trillion |
| Regional debt crisis | −32% | NOK7.26 trillion | NOK15.42 trillion |
| Extreme-weather event | −20% | NOK4.54 trillion | NOK18.15 trillion |

The monetary values are illustrative estimates: June 2026 fund value of NOK22.683 trillion multiplied by the published shock, with the loss subtracted from that starting value. Calculations use unrounded amounts before presentation rounding.

**The practical lesson is risk tolerance:** A long-term investor can survive temporary losses only if its owner can tolerate them without forcing damaging sales or abandoning the strategy. Large asset values do not remove political pressure when losses are measured in trillions. Liquidity reserves, credible spending rules, and communication become particularly important when domestic finances are also under strain.

**Germany faces an execution constraint:** Its preliminary 2025 investment expenditure of €86.8 billion was €28.8 billion below the €115.6 billion plan. The implied execution ratio was approximately 75.1%: €86.8 billion divided by €115.6 billion. The ministry attributed part of the shortfall to timing and implementation issues, including planned transfers to the Länder. [15]

**A conditional spending range is informative:** Applying that 2025 execution ratio to the €128.7 billion 2026 plan gives an estimated €96.6 billion: €128.7 billion × €86.8 billion/€115.6 billion. Full execution would produce €128.7 billion. The resulting scenario range, **€96.6–128.7 billion**, is conditional on repeating the earlier ratio versus delivering the full plan, not a probability forecast. [49]

**Strategic investors face commercialization tests:** PIF’s emphasis on sustained value creation makes project cash flows, private participation, and returns on existing assets increasingly important. Mubadala’s domestic industrial platforms similarly need competitive businesses rather than permanent dependence on sovereign support. The judgment improves when customers, exports, technology transfer, and independent capital follow; it weakens when projects repeatedly require new public financing without corresponding operating progress.

**The forward-looking balance remains conditional:** Private-market and infrastructure allocations can increase if governments retain funding capacity, managers find credible projects, and illiquid assets deliver competitive net returns. Greater fiscal withdrawals, weaker exits, geopolitical restrictions, or disappointment in AI-related demand would favor liquidity, repricing, and more selective deployment. The evidence supports these drivers more strongly than a single numerical forecast for worldwide sovereign assets.

**Section takeaway:** The main shift is toward larger, more direct, infrastructure-linked and strategic investments, but financing capacity, investable projects, geopolitical access, and execution determine how far it can proceed.

## What the comparison means

**Purpose is the best starting point:** For intergenerational saving, Norway’s foreign diversification provides a clear model. For reserve purchasing power, GIC’s real-return mandate is the relevant reference. For pensions, GPIF must be assessed against benefit financing; for industrial development, PIF and Mubadala must demonstrate commercial progress alongside national objectives; for public goods and financing gaps, US budgets and Germany’s budget–KfW combination provide different instruments.

**No institution escapes trade-offs:** Listed markets offer liquidity and clearer valuation but expose governments to visible market drawdowns. Private investments offer negotiated ownership and long-duration opportunities but increase valuation, exit, and execution risks. Domestic investment can build national capability, while foreign investment diversifies the government’s financial exposure; neither choice is automatically superior.

**Financial and public returns need separation:** A profitable investment can contribute little additional development, while a valuable public project may generate benefits that do not accrue as cash to its financier. The strongest systems specify which objective each institution serves, identify who bears losses, and evaluate financial results separately from infrastructure delivery, employment, resilience, or other public benefits.

**Evidence uses several measurement bases:** The comparison retains a 13 September 2026 endpoint and each observation’s own period, currency, institutional perimeter, and gross/net convention; rounding affects some displayed totals. GIC, ADIA, KIA, and QIA scale figures are estimates or selective disclosures, and all worked estimates and scenarios are labeled. Complete national-wealth ordering would be decided by common-date GDP, revenue, and consolidated assets-minus-liabilities data; complete performance ordering by common-period, common-currency net returns, benchmarks, risk, and fees. Voluntary disclosure and the distinction between reported activity and causal impact remain central to interpreting the results. [56]

**The overall judgment is straightforward:** Wealthy governments do not follow one investment strategy. They build different institutions for different obligations—and their success depends less on accumulating the largest headline asset figure than on matching funding, mandate, portfolio risk, and accountability.
