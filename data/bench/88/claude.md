# How Netflix Adapted "One Hundred Years of Solitude"

Netflix made the "unadaptable" novel work by treating Gabriel García Márquez's own objections as the production brief. It filmed in Spanish, in Colombia, with mostly Colombian talent: Netflix's Paco Ramos told Variety "98% of the people who made this show are from Colombia," and Variety reports "Ninety-seven percent of the cast are Colombian actors". It ran over 16 hours instead of a two-hour feature. It then dealt with the book's specific problems through a few disciplined choices: a narrator who brings the novel's voice back, a strictly linear structure, a physical Macondo that ages across four rebuilt versions, and magical realism kept understated. The result counts as a critical and cultural success and an industrial landmark for Colombia. It was not a global mass hit, though, and Part 2 (August 2026) drew fewer viewers than Part 1.

## TL;DR
- **The key was the format and the rights terms.** García Márquez refused for decades because a feature film was too short and a non-Spanish version "would not do it justice." His sons, Rodrigo García and Gonzalo García Barcha, licensed it to Netflix in March 2019 as a Spanish-language series shot in Colombia, with both sons as executive producers. The writers say the brief was to be faithful, linear, and in Spanish.
- **The craft fixes were specific.** A narrator reads from Melquíades' parchments, which keeps the novel's prose. Colonel Aureliano Buendía anchors Part 1. A 540,000 m² Macondo near Alvarado, Tolima was built and rebuilt four times to show the passage of time. Magic was handled with restraint, often practically. The story was split at the book's midpoint into two eight-episode parts, and Part 2 ended with a feature-length finale on Aug. 26, 2026.
- **"Successful," with caveats.** Part 1 scored about 80 on Metacritic and 84% on Rotten Tomatoes (31 reviews, 90% audience score). It debuted at #3 among Netflix's non-English series with 3.6M views, reached the Top 10 in 38 countries, and won three Platino Awards, including Best Miniseries. Critics in the Spanish-speaking world were more divided. Part 2 reviewed well but did not appear in the global non-English Top 10 in its premiere week, and Netflix has published no official Part 2 viewing figures.

## Key Findings

### 1. Why it was called unadaptable, and why earlier attempts failed
- **The author's objections.** The family's 2019 statement summed up his view: "For decades our father was reluctant to sell the film rights… because he believed that it could not be made under the time constraints of a feature film, or that producing it in a language other than Spanish would not do it justice."\[1\]\[2\] In 1982 he said his reluctance came from "my desire for direct communication with my readers."\[3\] In a 1991 interview he argued that a novel "leaves the reader a margin of creation" to imagine characters and places, and a film takes that away.\[4\]
- **Famous refusals.** He turned down a reported $1 million offer from Anthony Quinn: "Anthony Quinn, with or without his million dollars, will never be Coronel Aureliano Buendía to me or my readers."\[5\] Harvey Weinstein later said he had offered terms for himself and Giuseppe Tornatore.\[6\] García Márquez's mock condition was to "film the entire book, but only release one chapter — two minutes long — each year, for one hundred years."\[7\] His agent Carmen Balcells told Vanity Fair: "He never wanted a film made of One Hundred Years."\[8\] The only earlier screen version was Shūji Terayama's loose Japanese reworking, *Saraba hakobune* (1984), which is not an authorized adaptation.\[9\]
- **Problems in the text itself.** The book covers seven generations and about a century. The same names recur (José Arcadio, Aureliano). It has very little dialogue and jumps backward and forward in time.\[10\]\[11\] Its effect depends on a narrator who reports the miraculous in a flat, everyday tone. Critics have long described this voice as the hardest thing to put on screen.\[12\]

### 2. How the rights deal happened and how the family shaped it
- **Timeline.** Francisco Ramos, Netflix's VP of Latin American content, says talks with the family began in 2018.\[13\] Netflix announced the deal on March 6, 2019, the day García Márquez would have turned 92.\[6\] WME, attorney Shelley Surpin and the Agencia Literaria Carmen Balcells represented the estate.\[2\] After his widow, Mercedes Barcha, died in 2020, the two sons became the gatekeepers.\[13\]\[14\]\[15\]
- **The conditions.** Rodrigo García told El Comercio (Peru): "Mi padre, en vida, decía que, si se pudiera filmar 'Cien años de soledad' en muchas horas, en español y en Colombia, quizás lo consideraría." ("My father said in his lifetime that if it could be filmed over many hours, in Spanish and in Colombia, he might consider it.")\[16\] Netflix agreed that Macondo would "be located in Colombia and built by Colombian hands."\[17\]\[18\] Ramos agreed with the series format: "I didn't think we could tell it in a three-act structure."\[13\] The sons pointed to the "golden age of television" and global audiences' new comfort with subtitled shows as the reasons the timing was right.\[19\]
- **Involved but not controlling.** Head writer Natalia Santa says Netflix and the sons made only three demands of the writers: the adaptation had to be faithful, linear, and in Spanish.\[14\] Director Alex García López says Rodrigo García, himself a filmmaker, told him: "Don't let the weight of my father's book bring you down. Do your thing, follow your heart."\[20\] The family supplied legitimacy and limits, then left the creative work to the team. That combination is a large part of why the result is coherent rather than timid.

### 3. The production machine
- **Creative team.** Writers José Rivera (the Oscar-nominated Puerto Rican screenwriter of *The Motorcycle Diaries*, whom García Márquez once mentored at Sundance), Natalia Santa, Camila Brugés, Albatros González and María Camila Arias.\[1\]\[21\] Part 1 directors: Alex García López of Argentina (episodes 1–3, 7, 8) and Laura Mora of Colombia (episodes 4–6).\[1\]\[18\] For Part 2, Mora became showrunner and directed episodes 1, 2, 5, 6 and the Grand Finale; Carlos Moreno directed the rest.\[22\]\[23\]\[24\] Production designers were Eugenio Caballero and Bárbara Enríquez, with costumes by Catherine Rodríguez and music by Camilo Sanabria and Juancho Valencia.\[18\] Jesús Reyes is the narrator.\[1\]
- **Producer.** Dynamo, the Bogotá company behind *Narcos* production services, *Green Frontier* and *Monos*, had the local infrastructure to run the project. Variety describes it as Netflix's "most ambitious — and expensive — project to date in Latin America."
- **Budget.** Netflix has never disclosed it. Deadline reported that the official 225 billion COP (≈$51.8M) "impact" figure excludes the budget.\[17\] Colombian outlet Las2orillas puts the spend at "cerca de $50 millones de dólares" (close to $50 million), but that figure is unconfirmed.\[25\] The production used Colombia's CINA incentive, a 35% transferable tax credit. Colombian Film Commissioner Silvia Echeverri told Variety: "Without a doubt, 'One Hundred Years of Solitude' is the biggest and most important project to have tapped this incentive."
- **Macondo built from scratch.** The town went up near Alvarado, Tolima, about four hours from Bogotá.\[1\] Netflix gives the plot as over 540,000 m²; set reports say 560,000 m² at Finca Arizona, or roughly 70 football fields. El Tiempo reported that 1,100 workers built it.\[1\]\[18\]\[24\]\[26\] The team constructed **four Macondos** across the two parts so the town could visibly grow and decay.\[1\] Macondo 2 and 3 included the railway station where the banana massacre happens, and Macondo 4, the largest, holds the ending. Walls were finished with a homemade mix of cement, soap and rice husk to imitate rammed earth. The Buendía house interior is about 1,750 m² inside a 3,000 m² tent, dressed in 360° for long tracking shots, and 85% of its furnishings are genuine antiques. Producer Carolina Caicedo said: "Desde el principio, la idea fue construir el pueblo físicamente" ("From the start, the idea was to build the town physically"). The design was researched against 1850–1950 Colombian period detail, down to when electricity arrived.\[24\]
- **Locations.** La Guajira, Magdalena, Cesar, Cundinamarca and Tolima, with Part 2 adding Boyacá.\[1\]\[14\] The crew stayed in Ibagué, booking over 100,000 hotel nights for Part 1.\[18\] Part 1 was shot May–December 2023. Part 2 began filming in February 2025 and took 191 shooting days.\[1\]\[24\]
- **Casting and authenticity.** An open casting call in 2022 drew 10,541 audition applicants for 25 main roles (El Tiempo). El Tiempo counts 44 professional actors (5 of them foreign), 54 non-professional actors and 13 minors. Extras counts conflict: El Tiempo reports about 10,000 extras and doubles, while Variety says "the series finale alone features nearly 20,000 extras." Leads include Claudio Cataño (Colonel Aureliano Buendía), Marco Antonio González and Susana Morales (young José Arcadio Buendía and Úrsula), Diego Vásquez and Marleyda Soto (the older couple), Viña Machado (Pilar Ternera) and Jairo Camargo (Apolinar Moscote).\[1\] The show includes dialogue in Wayuu.\[1\]\[27\]\[28\] The soundtrack features Caribbean traditional musicians such as Los Gaiteros de San Jacinto. Netflix says about 40,000 garments were made for Part 1, 97% from Colombian textiles;\[18\] a later set report gives 34,000 for Part 1 and 30,000 for Part 2.\[24\]

### 4. The creative decisions that solved the adaptation problems
- **The voice: a narrator inside the story.** García López says his "biggest concern" was a novel with "such little dialogue, yet such a profound and unique voice." The fix was a narrator "inspired by a character from the end of the book," meaning Aureliano Babilonia reading Melquíades' parchments. "By having him open the book… you suddenly have the voice of Gabo."\[20\] The famous first line is the first thing spoken.\[11\] Unusually, "our narrator is discovering the story along with the audience… The camera becomes his point of view."\[20\] This let the scripts keep dialogue sparse.\[20\] Time's Judy Berman credited "economical narration and well-placed silences" with keeping the show from feeling "too talky."\[29\]
- **Making the timeline and names legible.** The writers dropped the book's jumps in time in favor of a linear chronology, keeping only the firing-squad flash-forward as a framing device. Part 1 has one spine: in Santa's words, "the transformation of a colonel into a caudillo," with Part 2 showing "what happens to that caudillo."\[14\] The split falls "almost literally at the midpoint of the book," at the end of one generation and the start of the next.\[20\]\[30\] Different actors at different ages, plus the visibly aging Macondo sets, help viewers keep repeated names apart.
- **Magical realism kept matter-of-fact.** García López, whose background is VFX-heavy work like *The Witcher*, said he drew on grounded 1970s cinema. The goal was to stage the levitating, the rain of yellow flowers and the bag of bones as ordinary events.\[31\] VFX vendors were used sparingly. FOLKS added the yellow butterflies, animals and environments in Part 2, and Planet X handled battle effects.\[32\]\[33\]\[34\] Time noted that images like the trickle of blood crossing town "retain their poetic profundity" instead of looking silly.\[29\]
- **Fidelity versus interpretation.** Santa is frank about it: "Every adaptation is a transgression and a necessary reinterpretation… Macondo fits – but all its events do not." The team called the form a telenovela ("the novel is profoundly melodramatic"). They chose to keep the book's darkness, including incest, self-harm and Aureliano's marriage to a child bride, rather than sanitize it.\[14\]\[29\] Part 2 leaned harder into politics. Mora says "Siempre hablan de lo mágico, pero es una obra superpolítica" ("They always talk about the magic, but it's a deeply political work"), and Ramos encouraged her not to fear "lo político del libro" (the book's politics).\[5\]\[35\] The series ends with a near feature-length Grand Finale. Ramos said the project was "always intended" as a two-part, "bookended" adaptation.\[22\]\[36\]

### 5. Was it actually a success?
- **Critics, Part 1 (Dec 2024).** Rotten Tomatoes' Season 1 page shows an 84% Tomatometer from 31 reviews and a 90% Popcornmeter, with the consensus that it "faithfully realizes Gabriel García Márquez's seminal novel with sumptuous polish." Metacritic gives it 80, "generally favorable." Time (Berman) called it "among the best" of the year's "unfilmable" adaptations, though sometimes "almost too faithful."\[29\] The Hollywood Reporter (Daniel Fienberg) said "ambitious and honorable… breathlessly beautiful at times," but noted that word-for-word passages from the book "don't always work."\[1\]\[30\]\[37\] Variety (Aramide Tinubu) called it "one of the most faithful page-to-screen adaptations in recent years," with pacing "too lackadaisical at times."\[38\] The Telegraph gave 5/5 and The Independent called it "pretty much perfect." The Guardian (Jack Seale) gave 3/5, citing "problematic sexual politics,"\[37\] and The Times (Ed Potton) gave 2/5, "gorgeous but lethargic."\[1\]
- **Spanish-language critics were harsher.** In El País, Sergio del Molino called it a "serie horrorosa" (horrendous series).\[39\] Rolling Stone en Español called it "contenido estéril y sin alma" (sterile, soulless content), saying it lost the book's narrative voice.\[9\] Others found it faithful but lacking boldness.\[40\] This split matters: viewers who know the book well were the hardest to please, and the voice and the magic were what they missed most.
- **Viewership.** In its debut week Part 1 drew 3.6M views and 31.1M hours. It ranked #3 among non-English series globally, reached the Top 10 in 38 countries, and was #1 in Colombia.\[41\] Netflix's own engagement reports credit it with 9M views in H2 2024 ("breakouts like… One Hundred Years of Solitude (9M) from Colombia") and another 5M in H1 2025 ("Following the release of last year's One Hundred Years of Solitude (5M)…"). That is solid for a dense, subtitled period drama with no stars, but far below Netflix's biggest hits. Ecuador's La República concluded it was "un éxito cultural de alcance medio-alto, no… un fenómeno masivo global" ("a cultural success with medium-to-high reach, not a mass global phenomenon").
- **Awards.** At the 2025 Platino Awards it won 3 of 8 nominations: Best Miniseries, Best Actor (Claudio Cataño) and Best Supporting Actor (Jairo Camargo).\[1\]\[42\]\[43\] It also earned a 2025 International Emmy nomination for Best Actor (Diego Vásquez) and Peabody and Gotham TV nominations.\[1\]\[44\] In Colombia it swept the India Catalina awards (series, lead actor and actress, script, cinematography, production design, score), and it won two Premios Aura.\[1\]\[45\] I found no record of Golden Globe, Critics' Choice or Primetime Emmy nominations, which fits its foreign-language, streaming-only profile.
- **Part 2 (Aug 5 / Aug 26, 2026).** It was well reviewed on a small sample: 100% on Rotten Tomatoes from 5 reviews, with Metacritic still "tbd."\[46\]\[47\]\[48\] Collider gave it 9/10 and called it an "epic conclusion." Decider found it "a bit muddled" at first.\[37\]\[46\] Argentina's Micropsia called it "más filosa, más directa… menos enredada" ("sharper, more direct… less tangled") than Part 1.\[49\] The Independent (Nick Hilton, 3/5) disagreed: "Márquez was right, his novel is unfilmable."\[37\] Audiences were less engaged. Part 2 missed the global non-English Top 10 in its premiere week, when the #10 title drew 1.5M views,\[50\] though it reportedly opened as the most-watched series in Colombia.\[51\] Netflix has released no official Part 2 figure.\[39\] One Argentine headline claiming "casi 50 millones de visualizaciones" (almost 50 million views) has no source and conflicts with Netflix's own charts, so I disregard it.\[52\]

### 6. Broader significance
- **Netflix's strategy.** The show is the clearest case yet of Netflix's model for Spanish-language prestige content. It buys a canonical IP that Hollywood could not land, accepts local-authenticity conditions, partners with a strong local producer, and draws on national incentives. Omdia data put Colombia at 24.6% of Netflix's Spanish-language viewing hours in late 2024, second only to Spain.\[53\] Netflix has since announced a 10-title Colombian slate.\[54\] Santa is now showrunning *Palacio*, about the 1985 Palace of Justice siege, for Netflix and Dynamo.\[14\]
- **Impact on Colombia.** Netflix says Part 1 added 225 billion COP (≈$51.8M) to Colombian GDP, involved 900+ crew, about 150 artisans and 850+ suppliers.\[18\]\[55\] Across both parts the total passed 525 billion COP, with 970+ suppliers in at least 15 departments and 154,888 hotel nights for Part 2 alone.\[41\] These are Netflix's own GDP-impact estimates, not independent audits. Colombia's CINA incentive quota for 2025 was fully allocated by mid-September, a record, and the series is the main showcase for that boom.\[53\]
- **Lessons for other "unadaptable" books.** (1) Treat the author's objections as a brief, not an obstacle. (2) Long-form TV fixes the problem of length, not the problem of voice, so the voice needs its own device. (3) Simplify structure (linear time, one spine per season) before cutting content. (4) Keep the fantastic physical and casual. (5) Accept that readers will measure the show against their private version of the book. Netflix's 2024 *Pedro Páramo*, beautiful but criticized for losing Rulfo's elusive voice, is the warning case.\[56\]

## Recommendations
- **For viewers:** Watch Part 1 for the founding-and-war arc, which is its strength. Watch Part 2 for the political tragedy of the railway, the banana company and the massacre. Read the book alongside or afterward. The show works best as a companion to it, not a substitute.
- **For adapters and studios:** Copy the structure of this deal: estate involvement with limited creative interference, a local producer, incentive financing and a fixed endpoint. Put most of the investment into the narrative device that carries the author's voice. That is where the strongest criticism landed.
- **For anyone judging "success":** Separate three measures. The critical and awards verdict is strongly positive. The verdict from Colombia's industry and culture is decisively positive. The global audience verdict is moderate, and weaker for Part 2.

## Caveats
- **Budget.** Unknown. Netflix has never disclosed it, and the ~$50M figure is unconfirmed.
- **Viewership.** Official figures exist only for Part 1: its opening weeks plus Netflix's own engagement reports (9M views in H2 2024, 5M in H1 2025). Part 2 data may only appear in Netflix's next engagement report, expected around January 2027.
- **Economic figures.** These are Netflix-commissioned estimates of GDP impact.\[18\]
- **Small Part 2 sample.** Part 2's review scores rest on a handful of reviews, and I found no Part 2 reviews from The Guardian, NYT, Variety, THR, El País, Semana or El Espectador.
- **Conflicting numbers.** Sources differ on costume counts (40,000 vs. 34,000 for Part 1), set area (540,000 vs. 560,000 m²) and the finale's running time ("nearly two hours" vs. "una hora y media", an hour and a half).\[18\]\[22\]\[23\]\[24\]\[57\]
