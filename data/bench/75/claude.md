# Modulating Plasma Metal Ions to Prevent or Treat Cardiovascular Disease: What the Clinical Evidence Actually Shows

## TL;DR

- **One metal intervention has earned a guideline recommendation, and it is a deficiency-correction strategy, not a toxic-metal strategy: intravenous iron for heart-failure patients with iron deficiency (2023 ESC Class I to improve symptoms/quality of life; Class IIa to cut hospitalizations).** Almost every other metal-modulating approach — EDTA chelation, selenium, zinc, calcium, magnesium, copper-lowering, iron reduction — is either unproven, null in definitive trials, or supported only by surrogate endpoints.
- **The field's central lesson is that observational associations have repeatedly failed to translate into positive randomized trials.** Toxic metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic) are now recognized by the American Heart Association (2023) as genuine, causal-looking cardiovascular risk factors, yet the one large trial of removing them pharmacologically — TACT2 (JAMA 2024) — was null. Correcting a true deficiency sometimes helps; supplementing an already-replete population generally does not and can harm.
- **Serum/plasma concentration is frequently a poor biomarker of tissue or total-body metal status** (ferritin is an acute-phase reactant; serum zinc poorly reflects body stores; serum copper mostly reflects ceruloplasmin). This measurement problem, combined with U-shaped dose–response curves, confounding and reverse causation, explains much of the gap between epidemiology and trials. The credible path forward is **precision repletion/removal in genuinely deficient or overloaded individuals**, not population-wide supplementation.

## Mechanistic Rationale: Why Circulating Metals Matter in Cardiovascular Disease

**Iron.** Iron is redox-active and catalyzes hydroxyl-radical formation (Fenton chemistry), the basis of the decades-old "iron hypothesis" that body iron stores promote atherosclerosis. It is also the substrate for **ferroptosis**, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation that is implicated in ischemia–reperfusion injury, doxorubicin cardiotoxicity, and heart failure. Clinically, iron operates at both extremes: hereditary hemochromatosis and transfusional iron overload cause cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia, while **iron deficiency** — present in approximately 50% of heart-failure patients in the outpatient setting and up to 80% in the post-acute HF setting (HFSA Scientific Statement, JACC: Heart Failure 2024) — impairs mitochondrial energetics in cardiomyocytes and independently predicts worse outcomes regardless of anemia. The guideline definition of iron deficiency in heart failure is ferritin <100 µg/L, or ferritin 100–299 µg/L with transferrin saturation <20%.\[1\]

**Copper.** Copper is a cofactor for cytochrome c oxidase, superoxide dismutase and lysyl oxidase. Its dual role mirrors iron's: copper is redox-active and pro-oxidant in excess, yet copper *deficiency* induces cardiac hypertrophy in animal models, and loosely bound ("unbound" or exchangeable) copper II is thought to contribute to hypertrophic and diabetic cardiomyopathy. Most serum copper is bound to ceruloplasmin, so total serum copper is a poor index of the biologically active pool. **Cuproptosis**, a recently described copper-dependent cell-death pathway acting via the TCA cycle, has become a target of mechanistic interest. Copper chelation (e.g., trientine) is hypothesized to improve myocardial copper trafficking and energetics.\[2\]

**Zinc.** Zinc is an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory structural cofactor (including in Cu/Zn-SOD). Zinc deficiency is associated with heart failure and with adverse lipid and glycemic profiles; the zinc/copper ratio has been proposed as a cardiovascular marker. Serum zinc, however, reflects total-body zinc stores poorly, complicating both diagnosis and trial interpretation.

**Magnesium.** Magnesium regulates vascular tone, endothelial function, and cardiac electrical stability (per the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, it is a cofactor in more than 300 enzyme systems that regulate diverse biochemical reactions including protein synthesis, muscle and nerve transmission, and blood pressure regulation). Low magnesium is linked to hypertension, arrhythmia (including post-operative atrial fibrillation), and vascular calcification; it is routinely repleted in acute arrhythmia management.

**Selenium.** Selenium is incorporated into selenoproteins including glutathione peroxidases and thioredoxin reductases that defend against oxidative stress. Severe deficiency causes **Keshan disease**, a dilated cardiomyopathy endemic to selenium-poor regions of China. This established deficiency syndrome motivates the hypothesis that selenium repletion could protect the heart more broadly — but selenium has a narrow therapeutic window and a U-shaped risk curve.

**Calcium.** Beyond its role in excitation–contraction coupling, exogenous calcium (from supplements) can transiently raise serum calcium and has been hypothesized to accelerate vascular calcification and promote arterial thrombosis — the basis for concern that calcium supplements may raise myocardial-infarction risk.

**Toxic (non-essential) metals.** Lead, cadmium, arsenic and mercury have no physiologic role and are now recognized cardiovascular risk factors. They induce oxidative stress and inflammation, cause endothelial dysfunction, displace essential metals from critical proteins, and are linked to hypertension, atherosclerosis, ischemic heart disease, stroke and peripheral artery disease. In its June 2023 scientific statement *Contaminant Metals as Cardiovascular Risk Factors* (Lamas, Navas-Acien, et al., *Journal of the American Heart Association* 2023;12:e029852), the AHA concluded that chronic low-level exposure to lead, cadmium and arsenic is "a significant contributor to cardiovascular disease worldwide,"\[3\] with higher urine levels of arsenic and blood levels of lead and cadmium associated with 15%–85% higher risk for stroke and heart disease. A separate modelling analysis (Larsen & Sánchez-Triana, *Lancet Planetary Health* 2023) estimated that lead exposure contributed to about 5,545,000 cardiovascular deaths among adults in 2019 (95% CI 2,305,000–8,271,000) — roughly 30% of all CVD deaths and about six times the GBD 2019 estimate — with ~90% in low- and middle-income countries; a newer GBD 2023 analysis estimates 3.5 million (2.6–4.4 million) lead-attributable deaths globally in 2023, accounting for 5.8% of all deaths. The WHO's lead fact sheet attributes more than 3.5 million deaths globally in 2023 to lead exposure, primarily due to cardiovascular effects.

## Types of Intervention Proposed

1. **Supplementation** — oral and IV iron; oral zinc, magnesium, selenium; (rarely) copper. The best-developed is IV iron in heart failure.
2. **Chelation therapy** — EDTA (edetate disodium) for lead/cadmium; deferoxamine/deferasirox/deferiprone for iron overload; trientine, tetrathiomolybdate, penicillamine for copper; succimer/DMSA for lead.
3. **Phlebotomy / blood donation** — to lower body iron stores (tested in FeAST).
4. **Dietary modification and food fortification** — e.g., selenium-adequate diets, iron fortification, reducing dietary cadmium/arsenic.
5. **Exposure reduction / environmental remediation** — leaded-fuel and lead-paint phase-outs, water treatment, tobacco avoidance, arsenic-safe wells; the primary strategy for toxic metals.
6. **Emerging/experimental** — ferroptosis inhibitors (ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1), cuproptosis modulators, nanoparticle-based chelators, metal-chaperone molecules, ionophores. These are preclinical.
7. **Zinc/copper ratio modulation** — proposed but not validated in outcome trials.

## Clinical Evidence, Intervention by Intervention

### IV iron in heart failure with iron deficiency — the strongest case, but nuanced

This is the one area where metal modulation has changed guidelines. The evidence is a chain of trials of mixed statistical success whose totality supports benefit on hospitalizations and symptoms, not clearly on mortality:

- **FAIR-HF (NEJM 2009)** — ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) improved the self-reported Patient Global Assessment (OR 2.51, 95% CI 1.75–3.61) and NYHA class (OR\[4\] 2.40, 95% CI 1.55–3.71) over 24 weeks in HFrEF with iron deficiency, with or without anemia.\[4\]
- **CONFIRM-HF** and **AFFIRM-AHF (acute HF)** — FCM improved functional capacity and reduced heart-failure hospitalizations; AFFIRM-AHF narrowly missed its primary endpoint but showed benefit in the prespecified COVID-19 sensitivity analysis.
- **IRONMAN (Lancet 2022)** — IV ferric derisomaltose; primary composite of recurrent HF hospitalization and CV death rate ratio 0.82 (95% CI 0.66–1.02; P=0.070); the prespecified COVID-19 sensitivity analysis was marginally significant (RR 0.76; 95% CI 0.58–1.00; P=0.047).
- **HEART-FID (NEJM 2023)** — in 3,065 patients,\[5\] FCM did not meet its hierarchical primary endpoint of death, HF hospitalizations and 6-minute-walk distance at the prespecified stringent threshold (P=0.019\[6\] vs required <0.01); all-cause death 8.6% vs 10.3%.
- **FAIR-HF2 (JAMA 2025)** — in 1,105 patients,\[7\] FCM reduced the first primary endpoint (CV death or first HF hospitalization) with HR 0.79 (95% CI 0.63–0.99; P=0.04), but this did not clear the trial's prespecified significance threshold; total HF hospitalizations rate ratio 0.80 (95% CI 0.60–1.06; P=0.12); the TSAT <20% subgroup HR was 0.79 (95% CI 0.61–1.02; P=0.07). It was well tolerated and improved quality of life,\[8\] with a notable\[7\] 34% treatment-discontinuation rate.
- **Meta-analysis (Nature Medicine 2025, Anker et al.)** — pooling all six trials (FAIR-HF,\[9\] CONFIRM-HF, AFFIRM-AHF, IRONMAN, HEART-FID, FAIR-HF2; 7,175 patients), IV iron reduced the composite of total HF hospitalizations and CV death. An earlier individual-patient-data meta-analysis of the FCM trials (European Heart Journal 2023) found a rate ratio of 0.86 (95% CI 0.75–0.98; P=0.029) for CV death and total CV hospitalizations, a 17% reduction in total CV hospitalizations (RR 0.83; 95% CI 0.73–0.96) and a 16% reduction in total HF hospitalizations — but no effect on time to CV death (HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.80–1.17).

**Bottom line for IV iron:** repleting a genuine deficiency improves symptoms, functional capacity and quality of life and reduces HF hospitalizations; individual pivotal trials have repeatedly missed their primary endpoints, and a mortality benefit is not established. The 2023 ESC focused update gives IV iron a **Class I (Level A)** recommendation to alleviate symptoms/improve quality of life in symptomatic HFrEF/HFmrEF with iron deficiency, and a **Class IIa (Level A)** recommendation (ferric carboxymaltose or derisomaltose) to reduce HF-hospitalization risk. A FAIR-HF2 sex-specific analysis (European Journal of Heart Failure 2025) suggested benefit concentrated in men —\[10\] hypothesis-generating and requiring validation. Oral iron (e.g., IRONOUT-HF, not detailed here) has generally failed to improve outcomes, underscoring that the route and repletion of true deficiency matter.

In **hemodialysis** patients, the **PIVOTAL trial (NEJM 2019; Macdougall et al., 2,141 patients)** showed proactive high-dose IV iron sucrose was superior to reactive low-dose, with a primary composite (death, MI, stroke, HF hospitalization)\[11\] HR of 0.85 (95% CI 0.73–1.00; P=0.04 for superiority) and a recurrent-events\[12\] rate ratio of 0.77 (95% CI 0.66–0.92); a prespecified heart-failure analysis (JACC: Heart Failure 2021) found a rate ratio of 0.59 (95% CI 0.40–0.87; P=0.0084) for HF events. This is the one iron trial with a (modest) hard-outcome signal, in a population with high iron needs.

### Iron reduction (phlebotomy) — null

The **FeAST trial (JAMA 2007; Zacharski et al., 1,277 veterans with peripheral artery disease)** tested graded phlebotomy to reduce ferritin to ~25 ng/mL. It did **not** reduce all-cause mortality or the composite of death, MI and stroke (P=0.17\[13\] and P=0.20). Secondary/subgroup analyses (younger patients, a later cancer-incidence substudy) generated hypotheses but do not support iron reduction for cardiovascular prevention. Mendelian randomization has been inconsistent to contrary: several MR analyses find higher genetically predicted iron status associated with *lower* coronary disease risk (e.g., OR ~0.995 per unit for coronary atherosclerosis),\[14\] arguing against the simple iron-overload hypothesis of atherosclerosis.

### EDTA chelation for toxic metals — initially promising, then refuted

- **TACT (JAMA 2013; 1,708 post-MI patients)** —\[15\] 40 infusions of a disodium-EDTA-based regimen reduced a composite cardiovascular endpoint by about 18% (modest, borderline), with a notably larger effect in the prespecified diabetic subgroup. These results were controversial on methodological grounds and were treated as hypothesis-generating.
- **TACT2 (JAMA 2024; 1,000 post-MI patients with diabetes)** — designed specifically to replicate the diabetes signal. Despite\[16\] lowering blood lead by 61%, EDTA chelation did **not** reduce major adverse cardiovascular events: HR 0.93 (95% CI 0.76–1.16; P=0.53). The authors concluded the findings\[17\]\[18\] "do not support the clinical use of chelation to reduce CVD risk" in US/Canadian patients with diabetes and prior MI. No safety issues were seen.

**Bottom line:** the most rigorous test of pharmacologically removing toxic metals to prevent cardiovascular events was null. This is a crucial caveat to the toxic-metal risk-factor story: exposure reduction (primary prevention) is well justified, but chelation as secondary prevention is not supported.

### Copper lowering — trientine in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (positive surrogate)

The **TEMPEST trial (phase 2; European Heart Journal 2026; 154 adults with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy)** randomized patients to\[19\] trientine (a selective copper-II chelator) 400 mg twice daily or placebo for 52 weeks. Trientine produced a greater reduction in indexed left ventricular mass:\[20\] between-group difference −3.2 g/m² (95% CI −5.6 to −0.8; P=0.009), with larger effects at higher baseline LV mass, mediated via reduced myocardial cellular mass (−3.9 g/m²; 95% CI −6.8 to −0.9). It was generally well tolerated;\[19\] gastrointestinal effects, anemia and low copper were more frequent. This is a mechanistically important, hypothesis-supporting result on an **imaging surrogate**, not clinical events; a larger outcomes trial would be needed before practice change.

### Selenium — no benefit in replete populations; a disputed Swedish signal

- **Cochrane review (Rees et al., 2013, with subsequent updates; 12 trials, ~20,000 participants)** — selenium supplementation did **not** reduce CVD events (all CVD events RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.95–1.11) or all-cause/CV mortality (CV\[21\] mortality RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.79–1.20), and carried a non-significant signal toward increased type 2 diabetes (RR\[22\] 1.06, 95% CI 0.97–1.15). The review concluded supplementation is not justified for CVD prevention in well-nourished populations.
- Large cancer-prevention trials (**SELECT**, **NPC**) reinforced both the lack of cardiovascular benefit and the diabetogenic safety concern.
- **KiSel-10 (Alehagen et al., Sweden)** — in an elderly population with low baseline selenium,\[23\] four years of combined selenium (200 µg/day) plus coenzyme Q10 reduced cardiovascular mortality, a benefit persisting at 10- and 12-year follow-up\[24\] (e.g., 10-year CV mortality HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.36–0.74). This is frequently cited as the pro-selenium counterpoint, but it is a single, small, combination-intervention trial in a selenium-deficient population and cannot be generalized to replete individuals. Mendelian randomization suggests genetically higher selenium raises type 2 diabetes risk (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.07–1.50)\[25\] with little evidence of a coronary effect.

**Bottom line:** selenium follows the deficiency-correction rule — plausible benefit only where baseline status is low; no benefit, and possible harm, in the replete.

### Magnesium — real surrogate effects, inconsistent hard outcomes

- **Blood pressure:** a meta-analysis of 34 double-blind RCTs (Zhang et al., *Hypertension* 2016;68:324–333; 2,028 participants) found magnesium supplementation (median 368 mg/day, ~3 months) lowered systolic BP by 2.00 mmHg (95% CI 0.43–3.58) and diastolic by 1.78 mmHg (95% CI 0.73–2.82) — small but consistent, with larger effects in deficient/insulin-resistant subgroups. Newer meta-analyses (2024–2025) concur that\[26\] the effect concentrates in hypertensive, hypomagnesemic and medicated patients, with little effect in normotensive individuals.
- **Acute MI:** the history is a cautionary tale about small positive trials. **LIMIT-2 (1992)** suggested a ~24% mortality reduction, but the massive **ISIS-4 (1995; 58,050 patients)** found no benefit (5-week mortality 7.64% magnesium vs 7.24% control),\[27\] and **MAGIC (2002; 6,213 high-risk patients)** confirmed no benefit (30-day death 15.3% vs 15.2%).\[28\] IV magnesium is not used to reduce MI mortality.
- **Post-operative atrial fibrillation:** multiple meta-analyses show prophylactic magnesium reduces post-cardiac-surgery AF (e.g., RR ~0.58–0.64),\[29\]\[30\] though at least one rigorous meta-analysis (Annals of Thoracic Surgery 2013) found no effect (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.61–1.44),\[31\] and heterogeneity is high. Magnesium remains a reasonable, low-cost adjunct for arrhythmia prophylaxis and correcting hypomagnesemia, but is not a disease-modifying CVD therapy.

### Zinc — favorable surrogate markers, no outcome data

Meta-analyses of RCTs (e.g., a GRADE-assessed dose–response review of 75 studies; and T2DM-specific analyses of 14–19 RCTs) show zinc supplementation improves triglycerides, total cholesterol, fasting glucose, HbA1c, CRP and oxidative-stress markers, with the largest lipid effects in deficient and dyslipidemic subgroups; effects on\[32\] blood pressure, LDL and HDL are inconsistent or null. No trial has tested zinc against cardiovascular events. MR evidence is mixed and in some analyses suggests higher zinc is associated with *higher* coronary disease risk — a caution against assuming benefit.

### Calcium — a safety signal, not a therapy

The **Bolland meta-analyses (BMJ 2010, 2011)** found\[33\] calcium supplements without co-administered vitamin D were associated with increased myocardial-infarction risk (pooled RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.01–1.59; P=0.038 in trial-level analysis), with a tendency toward more stroke and death. The WHI reanalysis supported an increased MI risk with calcium±vitamin D in treatment-naïve users. The findings remain debated (self-reported endpoints; no mortality effect; some guideline bodies judge dietary calcium safe), but the practical conclusion is that calcium supplementation is not cardioprotective and may carry modest cardiovascular risk — relevant because calcium is widely consumed for bone health. MR analyses linking genetically higher calcium to coronary disease add concern.

### Toxic-metal exposure reduction — strong rationale, trial evidence limited to surrogates

Given the null TACT2 chelation result, the evidence-based strategy for lead/cadmium/arsenic is **preventing exposure**, not removing accumulated metal. Population measures (leaded-gasoline phase-out, lead-pipe replacement, arsenic-safe water, tobacco control) have strong ecological and mechanistic support and are endorsed by the AHA statement. Chelation with succimer/DMSA is reserved for high-level poisoning; it has not been shown to improve cardiovascular outcomes at the low exposures relevant to population CVD risk. Some observational work (e.g., Strong Heart Study follow-ups) links lead reduction to lower blood pressure, but randomized cardiovascular-outcome evidence is lacking.

### Trace elements in CKD/dialysis

Dialysis patients are frequently deficient in zinc and selenium and have elevated cadmium and lead (Tonelli et al., *BMC Medicine* 2009).\[34\] Zinc deficiency is highly prevalent (roughly\[35\] 40–75% below clinical thresholds in several hemodialysis cohorts). RCTs of zinc (≈45 mg/day) and selenium (200 µg/day) in hemodialysis improve\[36\] CRP, malondialdehyde, SOD and nutritional scores, but **no dialysis trial has demonstrated improved cardiovascular outcomes** — the evidence stops at surrogate markers, and links between low zinc/selenium and mortality are observational.

## Critical Appraisal

**Why observational signals fail in RCTs.** Metal–CVD relationships are typically **U-shaped**: both deficiency and excess are harmful, so linear epidemiological associations mislead, and supplementing people on the flat or ascending part of the curve yields no benefit or harm (selenium and the diabetes signal; zinc and MR coronary signals). **Reverse causation and confounding** are pervasive — low selenium/zinc and abnormal ferritin often reflect inflammation and illness rather than causing it.

**Biomarker validity is a core problem.** Serum concentration frequently fails to reflect tissue or total-body status: **ferritin is an acute-phase reactant** (inflammation inflates it, masking deficiency); **serum zinc poorly tracks body stores**; **serum copper mostly reflects ceruloplasmin** rather than the exchangeable copper thought to be pathogenic. Trials that enroll by serum thresholds may therefore misclassify who is truly deficient or overloaded — diluting any real effect. The IV-iron success story partly reflects a reasonably validated functional definition (ferritin plus transferrin saturation).

**Deficiency-correction vs. supplementing the replete.** The consistent pattern across iron, selenium, zinc and magnesium is that benefit appears when a genuine deficiency is corrected and disappears (or reverses) when replete populations are supplemented. This is the single most important interpretive key to the whole field.

**Safety.** Iron: overload/oxidative injury, and infusion reactions; proactive IV iron in PIVOTAL did not increase infection or stroke. Copper: over-chelation causes copper deficiency, anemia and neutropenia (seen in TEMPEST). Selenium: narrow therapeutic index, toxicity (selenosis), and a diabetogenic signal. Calcium supplements: possible increased MI risk. EDTA: TACT-era safety concerns (historical deaths from rapid/incorrect administration) were not reproduced as harm in the carefully conducted TACT2, but the regimen is burdensome (40 infusions) and unproven. Magnesium: generally safe; risk mainly in renal impairment.

**Where evidence is genuinely strong vs. weak.**
- **Strong (guideline-backed):** IV iron for symptomatic HFrEF/HFmrEF with iron deficiency (ESC Class I for symptoms; Class IIa for hospitalization reduction); proactive IV iron in hemodialysis (PIVOTAL).
- **Promising but surrogate-only:** trientine in HCM (LV mass); magnesium for BP and post-op AF; zinc for lipids/glycemia in deficient/diabetic patients; selenium+CoQ10 in a selenium-deficient elderly cohort.
- **Weak/null/refuted:** EDTA chelation for CVD prevention (TACT2 null); phlebotomy/iron reduction (FeAST null); selenium for primary prevention in the replete (Cochrane); IV magnesium for MI mortality (ISIS-4, MAGIC); calcium supplements (a risk, not a therapy).

## Current State and Future Directions

- **Precision/personalized targeting** is the field's most credible direction: treat only the genuinely deficient (IV iron, selenium in deficient cohorts) or genuinely overloaded (iron chelation in hemochromatosis/transfusional overload), rather than whole populations.
- **Better biomarkers** are needed — exchangeable/labile copper rather than total serum copper; hepcidin and functional iron indices; inflammation-adjusted ferritin; red-cell or tissue measures for zinc/selenium.
- **Ongoing/active questions:** confirmation of the FAIR-HF2 sex-specific findings; whether any IV-iron trial can demonstrate a mortality benefit; a potential outcomes trial of trientine or other copper modulation in cardiomyopathy; and whether population-level toxic-metal exposure reduction measurably lowers CVD (the mechanistically and economically strongest toxic-metal argument, per the Lancet Planetary Health modelling).
- **Emerging biology** — ferroptosis inhibitors (ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1), cuproptosis modulators, nanoparticle chelators and metal-chaperone molecules — remains **preclinical**; none has human cardiovascular-outcome data, and these should be described as experimental.

**Frank bottom line.** The idea that modulating plasma metal ions can prevent or treat cardiovascular disease is only partly borne out. It works, with guideline backing, in one well-defined scenario: correcting iron deficiency in heart failure (and, more modestly, in hemodialysis). Toxic metals are real, causal-looking risk factors best addressed by exposure prevention rather than chelation, which failed in TACT2. For selenium, zinc, magnesium, copper and calcium, the honest reading is that surrogate-marker effects are real but event-level evidence is absent, null, or (for calcium) points to harm. The future belongs to precise repletion or removal in correctly identified deficient or overloaded individuals — supported by better biomarkers than today's serum concentrations — not to population-wide metal supplementation.
