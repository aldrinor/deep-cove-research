# Therapeutic Modulation of Plasma Metal Ions in Cardiovascular Disease: Mechanistic Paradigms, Clinical Trial Evidence, and Translational Feasibility

## Executive Synthesis: The Metallomic Dichotomy in Cardiovascular Medicine

The hypothesis that therapeutic modulation of circulating plasma metal ion concentrations can prevent or treat cardiovascular disease (CVD) has evolved from empirical nutritional supplementation and broad heavy-metal chelation into a precise discipline of cardiovascular metallomics [1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42606681/). Clinical and genetic investigations confirm that both essential trace elements (iron, copper, zinc, selenium, magnesium, calcium, potassium) and non-essential contaminant heavy metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic, titanium) exert profound effects on myocardial bioenergetics, vascular tone, redox homeostasis, and programmed cell death [1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42606681/). However, contemporary randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reveal a stark translational dichotomy: altering extracellular plasma metal concentrations does not reliably translate into clinical cardiovascular benefit unless the intervention directly resolves a rate-limiting intracellular enzymatic defect or an acute electrophysiological instability [1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42606681/).

Across the entirety of clinical cardiology, intravenous (IV) iron repletion in patients with heart failure and reduced or mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF/HFmrEF) stands as the only metallomic intervention supported by robust, guideline-mandated clinical outcome evidence [1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42606681/). By bypassing inflammation-driven gastrointestinal malabsorption, IV ferric carboxymaltose and ferric derisomaltose replenish myocardial and skeletal muscle mitochondrial iron pools, significantly reducing 12-month recurrent heart failure hospitalizations (rate ratio [RR] 0.670.67; 95% CI: 0.49–0.9195\%\text{ CI: }0.49–0.91) and improving functional capacity, although pooled analyses demonstrate a significant sex interaction (ratio of RRs 1.401.40; 95% CI: 1.05–1.8695\%\text{ CI: }1.05–1.86; p=0.025p = 0.025) wherein men derive marked benefit while women experience a neutral average risk ratio of 0.980.98 (95% CI: 0.75–1.2695\%\text{ CI: }0.75–1.26) [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency). Targeted metal modulation has also demonstrated clinical utility in specialized acute or monogenic settings, including subcellular iron chelation and topoisomerase 2β2\beta (Top2β\text{Top2}\beta) inhibition via dexrazoxane to prevent anthracycline-induced ferroptosis and cardiotoxicity [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10866014/), therapeutic phlebotomy and systemic iron chelation in iron-overload cardiomyopathy [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5949916/), and moderate-dose IV magnesium sulfate to suppress lethal arrhythmias and reduce intensive care mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 0.550.55; 95% CI: 0.42–0.7195\%\text{ CI: }0.42–0.71) in critically ill patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1776926/full).

Conversely, broad-spectrum interventions designed to deplete circulating toxic metals or augment extracellular divalent cations in chronic vascular disease have repeatedly failed in rigorous Phase 3 trials. The landmark Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy 2 (TACT2) demonstrated that 40 weekly infusions of disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) reduced blood lead concentrations by 60% in diabetic post-myocardial infarction patients but produced zero reduction in the primary composite endpoint (HR 0.930.93; 95% CI: 0.76–1.1695\%\text{ CI: }0.76–1.16), cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or stroke (HR 0.890.89; 95% CI: 0.66–1.1995\%\text{ CI: }0.66–1.19), or all-cause mortality (HR 0.960.96; 95% CI: 0.71–1.3095\%\text{ CI: }0.71–1.30) over five years [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39141382/). Similarly, the double-blind MAGiCAL-CKD trial established that oral magnesium supplementation in chronic kidney disease (CKD) significantly elevates plasma magnesium yet fails to slow coronary artery calcification (CAC) while increasing gastrointestinal morbidity [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125639/). Most concerningly, bolus oral calcium supplementation—long prescribed for skeletal health—induces acute postprandial hypercalcemia that promotes hypercoagulability and vascular calcification, increasing the relative risk of overall CVD (RR 1.151.15; 95% CI: 1.06–1.2595\%\text{ CI: }1.06–1.25), coronary heart disease (CHD, RR 1.161.16; 95% CI: 1.05–1.2895\%\text{ CI: }1.05–1.28), and myocardial infarction (RR 1.251.25; 95% CI: 1.08–1.4595\%\text{ CI: }1.08–1.45) in meta-analyses of postmenopausal women [24](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/2/368).

## Epidemiological and Genetic Foundations of Cardiovascular Metallomics

The rationale for therapeutic metal modulation originates from prospective multi-metal epidemiological cohorts and multi-omic genetic studies linking circulating trace elements to cardiovascular morbidity [3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1459465/full). In the prospective Dongfeng-Tongji (DFTJ) cohort, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) profiling of 23 baseline fasting plasma metals demonstrated that incident CHD is independently and positively associated with plasma arsenic (extreme quartile adjusted odds ratio [OR] 1.781.78) and titanium (OR 1.321.32; 95% CI: 1.03–1.6995\%\text{ CI: }1.03–1.69; p-trend=0.04p\text{-trend} = 0.04), while exhibiting an inverse association with plasma selenium [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5933370/). Single-metal associations with barium and aluminum attenuated to the null in multi-metal models, underscoring the necessity of accounting for co-exposure correlations (r=−0.08 to 0.72r = -0.08\text{ to }0.72) [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5933370/).

Crucially, essential trace elements rarely exhibit linear dose-response relationships; instead, they follow U-shaped or J-shaped physiological curves where both deficiency and excess drive pathology [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). Among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in the DFTJ cohort, plasma iron, selenium, and copper were closely tied to all-cause and CVD mortality, with plasma copper displaying a pronounced J-shaped mortality curve [27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10005369/). While copper is indispensable for mitochondrial respiration and antioxidant enzymes, elevated circulating free copper catalyzes excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). Furthermore, because ceruloplasmin serves as the primary plasma copper-binding protein and circulating copper levels rise as renal function declines, higher plasma copper interacts synergistically with lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) to amplify mortality risk [27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10005369/).

Multi-element mixture modeling further cautions against indiscriminate trace element elevation [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3921/11/10/1991). When principal component analysis (PCA), quantile-based g-computation, and trace element risk scores (TERS) were applied to combined plasma concentrations of zinc, copper, selenium, and manganese, the primary component (PC1—accounting for 32.9%32.9\% of variance and heavily loaded by plasma zinc [0.8250.825] and selenium [0.7910.791]) was directly and significantly associated with elevated total cholesterol (p<0.002p < 0.002), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, p=0.007p = 0.007), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, p<0.001p < 0.001), and systolic blood pressure (SBP, p=0.048p = 0.048) [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3921/11/10/1991). In urinary mixture analyses, combined trace element exposure correlated directly with elevated fasting plasma glucose (β=0.14\beta = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.03–0.2695\%\text{ CI: }0.03–0.26; p=0.014p = 0.014) [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3921/11/10/1991). These findings indicate that supraphysiological co-elevation of essential antioxidant metals in plasma can perturb lipid and glycemic homeostasis [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3921/11/10/1991).

To separate causal biological pathways from environmental confounding, two-sample, multivariate, and Bayesian-weighted Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses have evaluated 13 genetically predicted trace elements across 20 distinct cardiovascular diseases, integrating 4,907 circulating plasma proteins, 1,400 serum metabolites, 731 immune cell phenotypes, and 473 intestinal flora traits as mediators [3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1459465/full). These genetic analyses confirmed 19 robust causal relationships without horizontal pleiotropy [3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1459465/full). Notably, mediated MR revealed that genetically higher plasma potassium causally reduces the risk of ischemic heart disease by downregulating the expression of circulating plasma proteins BDH2 (3-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase 2) and C1R (complement C1r subcomponent), as well as lowering serum levels of the metabolite 4-methoxyphenyl sulfate [3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1459465/full). This multi-omic evidence establishes that extracellular cations modulate ischemic risk not merely through membrane electrophysiology, but by regulating hepatic and vascular proteomic and metabolomic networks [3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1459465/full).

## Iron Homeostasis: Intravenous Repletion, Systemic Depletion, and Ferroptosis Inhibition

Iron represents the most clinically mature target in cardiovascular metallomics, requiring diametrically opposed interventions depending on whether the myocardium suffers from bioenergetic starvation or oxidative overload [1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42606681/).

### Intravenous Iron Repletion in Heart Failure

Iron deficiency affects 50%50\% to 80%80\% of patients with heart failure [12](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/48/5077/7251520). Beyond impairing erythropoiesis, myocardial iron deficiency directly compromises oxidative phosphorylation, reduces myocardial oxygen consumption, and disrupts cellular immune mechanisms, leading to diminished exercise tolerance, poor quality of life (QoL), recurrent hospitalizations, and increased mortality [12](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/48/5077/7251520). In heart failure trials and clinical guidelines, iron deficiency is diagnosed via two laboratory parameters: absolute iron deficiency, defined as serum ferritin <100 ng/mL<100\text{ ng/mL} (reflecting depleted stores), or functional iron deficiency, defined as serum ferritin 100–299 ng/mL100–299\text{ ng/mL} accompanied by a transferrin saturation (TSAT) <20%<20\% (reflecting inadequate circulating iron bioavailable for energy metabolism) [29](https://dzhk.de/en/newsroom/news/latest-news/article/fair-hf2-studie-und-begleitende-metaanalyse-liefern-neue-erkenntnisse-zur-intravenoesen-eisengabe-bei-herzschwaeche).

A foundational translational lesson in heart failure management is the complete divergence in efficacy between oral and intravenous iron supplementation [14](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2021/03/03/17/59/affirming-what-we-know-about-iv-iron-in-patients-with-acute-heart-failure). In the IRONOUT-HF trial, high-dose oral iron polysaccharide failed to improve exercise capacity at 16 weeks [14](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2021/03/03/17/59/affirming-what-we-know-about-iv-iron-in-patients-with-acute-heart-failure). Chronic heart failure induces systemic low-grade inflammation that upregulates hepatic hepcidin, which degrades ferroportin on duodenal enterocytes and reticuloendothelial macrophages, blocking gastrointestinal iron absorption and trapping iron stores. Consequently, intravenous iron formulations—specifically ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) and ferric derisomaltose—are required to bypass the gastrointestinal tract and directly saturate circulating transferrin [12](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/48/5077/7251520).

Early foundational RCTs, including FAIR-HF and CONFIRM-HF (N=304N=304), demonstrated that IV FCM significantly improved 6-minute walk test (6MWT) distance, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, and QoL, while reducing heart failure hospitalizations [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency). Subsequent large-scale outcome trials tested whether IV iron reduces hard clinical events across acute and chronic settings. In the AFFIRM-AHF trial, initiating IV FCM at hospital discharge in patients hospitalized for acute heart failure with concomitant iron deficiency reduced recurrent heart failure hospitalizations by 25%25\%, without affecting 1-year mortality [12](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/48/5077/7251520). Similarly, the open-label, blinded-endpoint UK IRONMAN trial randomized patients with HFrEF and iron deficiency to IV ferric derisomaltose or usual care, yielding a primary composite rate ratio of 0.820.82 (95% CI: 0.66–1.0295\%\text{ CI: }0.66–1.02; p=0.070p = 0.070) for recurrent heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular death [12](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/48/5077/7251520). Because both AFFIRM-AHF and IRONMAN were disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic, prespecified COVID-19 sensitivity analyses were conducted, in which the primary outcome reductions achieved statistical significance in both trials [12](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/48/5077/7251520). The subsequent HEART-FID study—the largest and longest-duration Phase 4 trial evaluating IV FCM in HFrEF with iron deficiency—narrowly missed its prespecified statistical significance threshold for the hierarchical composite primary endpoint, though individual patient data meta-analyses combining CONFIRM-HF, AFFIRM-AHF, and HEART-FID confirmed significant reductions in cardiovascular and heart failure hospitalizations [10](https://newsroom.csl.com/2023-08-28-Ferinject-R-granted-upgraded-recommendations-in-2023-ESC-heart-failure-guidelines). Most recently, the FAIR-HF2 trial enrolled 1,1051,105 patients with HFrEF and iron deficiency across six European countries over 17 months, demonstrating that targeted IV FCM supplementation reduced cardiovascular death or first heart failure hospitalization by 21%21\% compared to placebo [29](https://dzhk.de/en/newsroom/news/latest-news/article/fair-hf2-studie-und-begleitende-metaanalyse-liefern-neue-erkenntnisse-zur-intravenoesen-eisengabe-bei-herzschwaeche). Although this primary reduction did not reach formal statistical significance and no distinct benefit was isolated in the subgroup with TSAT <20%<20\%, the therapy was well tolerated, with serious adverse event rates equivalent to placebo [29](https://dzhk.de/en/newsroom/news/latest-news/article/fair-hf2-studie-und-begleitende-metaanalyse-liefern-neue-erkenntnisse-zur-intravenoesen-eisengabe-bei-herzschwaeche).

Synthesizing this entire body of evidence, a comprehensive 2025 *Nature Medicine*  systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis encompassing >7,000>7,000 patients with heart failure (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] <50%<50\%) and iron deficiency confirmed that IV iron significantly reduces the composite endpoint of total (first and recurrent) heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality by 28%28\% at 12 months and 19%19\% across complete follow-up [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency). At 12 months, recurrent heart failure hospitalizations fell significantly (RR 0.670.67; 95% CI: 0.49–0.9195\%\text{ CI: }0.49–0.91; p=0.021p = 0.021), accompanied by favorable trends in cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.800.80; 95% CI: 0.61–1.0395\%\text{ CI: }0.61–1.03; p=0.071p = 0.071) and all-cause mortality (HR 0.820.82; 95% CI: 0.65–1.0395\%\text{ CI: }0.65–1.03; p=0.073p = 0.073) [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency). Across complete trial follow-ups, mortality trends attenuated (cardiovascular mortality HR 0.870.87, p=0.096p = 0.096; all-cause mortality HR 0.920.92, p=0.221p = 0.221), reflecting the early impact of high-dose loading infusions that fully correct iron deficiency during the first year, followed by under-dosing or COVID-19 disruptions in later trial phases [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency). Reassuringly, IV iron showed no excess risk of infection (OR 1.021.02; 95% CI: 0.66–1.5995\%\text{ CI: }0.66–1.59) or serious adverse events (OR 0.910.91; 95% CI: 0.70–1.1595\%\text{ CI: }0.70–1.15) [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency).

Crucially, subgroup analyses in this >7,000>7,000-patient meta-analysis revealed that while treatment efficacy was uniform across age, ischemic vs. non-ischemic etiology, NYHA class, eGFR, hemoglobin, ferritin, and TSAT strata, a statistically significant interaction emerged for biological sex (ratio of relative risks [RRR] 1.401.40; 95% CI: 1.05–1.8695\%\text{ CI: }1.05–1.86; p=0.025p = 0.025; I2=23%I^2 = 23\%) [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency). Men derived substantial clinical benefit from IV iron, whereas women on average experienced no reduction in the primary composite outcome (RR 0.980.98; 95% CI: 0.75–1.2695\%\text{ CI: }0.75–1.26) [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency). This sex dimorphism suggests that standard ferritin and TSAT thresholds may misclassify functional iron status in female myocardium or that sex hormones differentially regulate cardiac hepcidin-ferroportin signaling [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency).

In clinical practice, the 2023 Focused Update of the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Heart Failure Guidelines assigns a Class I (Level of Evidence A) recommendation to IV iron supplementation in symptomatic patients with HFrEF and HFmrEF and iron deficiency to alleviate symptoms and improve quality of life, and an upgraded Class IIa (Level of Evidence A) recommendation specifically for ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose to reduce the risk of heart failure hospitalization [9](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2023/08/29/14/58/2023-focused-update-esc-guidelines-hf-esc-2023). Similarly, the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Heart Failure Guidelines assign a Class IIa recommendation for IV iron replacement in HFrEF with iron deficiency (with or without anemia) to improve functional status and QoL [33](https://achpccg.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Heidenreich-et-al_2022_AHA-ACC-HFSA-Guideline-for-the-Management-of-Heart-Failure-A-Report-of-the-American-College-of-Cardiology-American-Heart-Association-Joint-Committee-on-Clinical-Practice-Guidelines.pdf). Conversely, in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), while IV iron reliably normalizes serum iron indices, clinical benefit remains unproven due to sparse and largely uncontrolled evidence [35](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373394863_2023_Focused_Update_of_the_2021_ESC_Guidelines_for_the_diagnosis_and_treatment_of_acute_and_chronic_heart_failure).

### Systemic Iron Depletion and Subcellular Chelation

At the opposite extreme of the J-shaped curve, myocardial iron overload—arising from hereditary hemochromatosis or secondary transfusion-dependent anemias—catalyzes hydroxyl radical formation via the Fenton reaction, driving dilated or restrictive cardiomyopathy and lethal arrhythmias [17](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-7737/13/9/689). In these conditions, therapeutic depletion of systemic and myocardial iron is mandatory and highly effective [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5949916/). In non-anemic patients with cardiac hemochromatosis who do not have severe congestive heart failure, therapeutic phlebotomy is the first-line intervention [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5949916/). Initiated when serum ferritin reaches ≥300 μg/L\ge 300\ \mu\text{g/L} in men or ≥200 μg/L\ge 200\ \mu\text{g/L} in women regardless of symptoms, weekly removal of 1 unit of blood (450–500 mL450–500\text{ mL}, depleting 200–250 mg200–250\text{ mg} of elemental iron) mobilizes tissue iron stores for erythropoiesis [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5949916/). Phlebotomy is continued until serum ferritin reaches 10–20 μg/L10–20\ \mu\text{g/L}, and subsequently maintained at ferritin <50 ng/mL<50\text{ ng/mL} and TSAT <30%<30\% [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5949916/). Timely phlebotomy directly lowers myocardial iron content and reverses structural remodeling, improving LVEF, fractional shortening, left ventricular mass, and left atrial dimensions [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5949916/). In patients with secondary iron-overload disorders accompanied by anemia, or those with severe congestive heart failure who cannot tolerate volume shifts or hematocrit drops below 80%80\% of baseline, parenteral or oral iron chelation therapy is the treatment of choice [18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5949916/). Maintaining serum ferritin below 100 ng/dL100\text{ ng/dL} via chelation mitigates iron-induced cardiac arrhythmias and halts cardiomyopathy progression [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-3846/6/3/17). Notably, outside of true systemic iron overload, iron chelation can be deleterious; Phase 2 RCTs of the oral iron chelator deferiprone failed to improve motor function in Parkinson's disease and accelerated cognitive decline in early Alzheimer's disease [36](https://employer.myhealthtoolkitvt.com/web/public/brands/medicalpolicy/external-policies/chelation-therapy-for-off-label-uses/).

Subcellular iron chelation also plays a critical role in cardio-oncology. Anthracyclines such as doxorubicin (DOX) induce dose-dependent cardiotoxicity through a convergence of mitochondrial iron overload and nuclear DNA damage [16](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2024.1406247/full). DOX accumulates within cardiomyocyte mitochondria by binding with high affinity to cardiolipin on the inner mitochondrial membrane, where reductases and NADPH oxidases (predominantly NOX2\text{NOX2} and NOX4\text{NOX4}) reduce its anthraquinone ring into semiquinone radicals, triggering superoxide (O2−\text{O}_2^-) and hydroxyl radical (OH−\text{OH}^-) cascades [17](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-7737/13/9/689). Concurrently, anthracyclines cause intracellular ferrous iron (Fe2+\text{Fe}^{2+}) accumulation, which drives Fenton-mediated lipid peroxidation of mitochondrial membranes and executes ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death [17](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-7737/13/9/689). Simultaneously, DOX intercalates nuclear DNA and poisons Topoisomerase 2β2\beta (Top2β\text{Top2}\beta)—the isoform constitutively expressed in quiescent, differentiated cardiomyocytes—forming ternary Top2β–DOX–DNA\text{Top2}\beta\text{–DOX–DNA} cleavage complexes that induce double-strand DNA breaks and apoptosis [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10866014/). Dexrazoxane (DZR) is the sole FDA-approved agent that prevents anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10866014/). Its clinical efficacy stems from a dual mechanism: upon intracellular hydrolysis into an EDTA-like open-ring chelator, DZR sequesters labile mitochondrial iron to suppress Fenton chemistry and ferroptosis, while simultaneously binding the ATPase domain of Top2β\text{Top2}\beta to stabilize its closed dimeric conformation and trigger proteasomal degradation of Top2β\text{Top2}\beta, thereby physically blocking DOX from forming lethal DNA cleavage complexes [15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10866014/).

## Chelation of Non-Essential Heavy Metals vs. Selective Copper Modulation

### Contaminant Heavy Metals and the Failure of EDTA Chelation in TACT2

A June 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Statement formally recognized chronic low-to-moderate environmental exposure to non-essential contaminant metals—specifically lead, cadmium, and arsenic—as a major, modifiable determinant of coronary artery disease, stroke, and peripheral artery disease (PAD) [39](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/chronic-exposure-to-lead-cadmium-and-arsenic-increases-risk-of-cardiovascular-disease). Absorbed via the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts from aging lead water service lines, residual leaded gasoline and paint in soil, tobacco smoke, fertilizers, batteries, and contaminated groundwater, these metals have no physiological role and disproportionately burden socioeconomically disadvantaged and minority populations [40](https://www.msmc.com/heavy-metals-are-bad-for-your-heart/). Because lead and cadmium accumulate in bone and visceral organs over decades, investigators hypothesized that active pharmacological depletion using intravenous disodium EDTA could reduce recurrent atherosclerotic events [21](https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/tact2/).

In 2013, the original Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy (TACT) reported that 40 infusions of an EDTA-based chelation regimen modestly reduced cardiovascular events by 18%18\% in post-MI patients, with prespecified and post-hoc subgroup analyses by Escolar et al. (2014) and Ujueta et al. (2020) suggesting a pronounced event reduction among patients with diabetes mellitus and PAD [21](https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/tact2/). Because EDTA chelates divalent cations and increases urinary heavy metal excretion, TACT2 was launched by the National Institutes of Health to definitively test whether repeating this regimen in diabetic post-MI patients would replicate the survival benefit and correlate with lead and cadmium depletion [21](https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/tact2/).

Published in *JAMA*  in late 2024 (Lamas et al.), TACT2 was a multicenter, double-masked, 2×22 \times 2 factorial randomized clinical trial enrolling 1,0001,000 participants (959959 in the primary analysis; median age 6767 years; 27%27\% female) aged ≥50\ge 50 years with diabetes and a qualifying MI at least 6 weeks prior (median 5 years post-MI) [21](https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/tact2/). Patients were randomized to 40 weekly intravenous infusions of active EDTA solution (up to 3 g3\text{ g} disodium EDTA adjusted for creatinine clearance, combined with 7 g7\text{ g} ascorbic acid, 2 g2\text{ g} magnesium chloride, 100 mg100\text{ mg} procaine hydrochloride, 2,500 U2,500\text{ U} unfractionated heparin, 2 mEq2\text{ mEq} potassium chloride, 840 mg840\text{ mg} sodium bicarbonate, and B-vitamins) versus placebo infusions, alongside a factorial randomization to high-dose oral multivitamins and minerals (OMVM) or oral placebo over a 5-year follow-up [21](https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/tact2/).

Despite 68% compliance with all 40 infusions and indisputable biochemical target engagement—reducing median blood lead levels from a baseline of 9.2 μg/L9.2\ \mu\text{g/L} by −5.5 μg/L-5.5\ \mu\text{g/L} in the EDTA group versus −0.6 μg/L-0.6\ \mu\text{g/L} in the placebo group (p<0.001p < 0.001)—TACT2 was unequivocally negative across every clinical endpoint [22](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2024/04/05/04/40/tact2). The primary composite endpoint of all-cause death, MI, stroke, coronary revascularization, or hospitalization for unstable angina occurred in 35.6%35.6\% (172172 patients) of the EDTA group versus 35.7%35.7\% (170170 patients) of the placebo group, yielding an adjusted HR of 0.930.93 (95% CI: 0.76–1.1695\%\text{ CI: }0.76–1.16; p=0.53p = 0.53) [22](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2024/04/05/04/40/tact2). Similarly, the hard secondary composite endpoint of cardiovascular death, MI, or stroke occurred in 18.4%18.4\% of the EDTA group versus 19.7%19.7\% of the placebo group (HR 0.890.89; 95% CI: 0.66–1.1995\%\text{ CI: }0.66–1.19), and all-cause mortality was virtually identical at 17.4%17.4\% versus 17.6%17.6\% (HR 0.960.96; 95% CI: 0.71–1.3095\%\text{ CI: }0.71–1.30) [22](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2024/04/05/04/40/tact2).

Three mechanistic and epidemiological factors explain why substantial blood lead depletion failed to yield clinical benefit in TACT2. First, while EDTA cleared circulating blood lead, it failed to chronically deplete tissue cadmium stores, as median urinary cadmium (0.30 μg/g0.30\ \mu\text{g/g} creatinine at baseline) changed by −0.04 vs. 0 μg/g-0.04\text{ vs. }0\ \mu\text{g/g} creatinine (p=0.15p = 0.15) [22](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2024/04/05/04/40/tact2). Second, public health abatement reduced baseline population lead exposure over the decade separating TACT1 and TACT2, leaving TACT2 participants with a low baseline blood lead level (9.2 μg/L9.2\ \mu\text{g/L}) and well-controlled conventional risk factors (median HbA1c 7.2%7.2\%, median LDL-C 73 mg/dL73\text{ mg/dL}) [22](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2024/04/05/04/40/tact2). Third, chronic heavy metal toxicity inflicts irreversible structural arterial remodeling and epigenetic alterations over decades; clearing the extracellular blood compartment late in life after a myocardial infarction cannot reverse entrenched fibrocalcific plaque biology. Consequently, EDTA chelation is not indicated for atherosclerotic CVD regardless of diabetic status, shifting preventive focus toward environmental abatement, water filtration, and exploratory trials of oral nutritional agents like folate and N-acetylcysteine that may facilitate endogenous heavy metal detoxification [22](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2024/04/05/04/40/tact2).

### Copper Dyshomeostasis, Cuproptosis, and Selective Divalent Chelation

Unlike contaminant heavy metals, copper is a vital catalytic cofactor whose intracellular compartmentalization and valence state (Cu+\text{Cu}^+ vs. Cu2+\text{Cu}^{2+}) dictate whether it preserves or destroys the cardiovascular system [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). Within healthy cardiomyocytes, monovalent copper (Cu+\text{Cu}^+) activates cytochrome c oxidase (CCO) in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, is delivered via the copper chaperone for superoxide dismutase (CCS) to SOD1\text{SOD1} to detoxify superoxide radicals, activates lysyl oxidase (LOX) to cross-link extracellular matrix collagen and elastin, and stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor 1α1\alpha (HIF-1α\text{HIF-1}\alpha) to drive vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) transcription and angiogenesis [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). Cellular redox safety is buffered by metallothionein (MT) and glutathione (GSH) [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm).

Pathological dysregulation of copper drives cardiovascular disease through two opposing mechanisms [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). The first mechanism is cuproptosis, a distinct, copper-dependent form of regulated cell death triggered when excess copper ions accumulate inside mitochondria [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). In atherosclerosis, disturbed arterial blood flow acts on the endothelial copper uptake transporter CTR1\text{CTR1} (which functions as a mechanosensor), recruiting the mitochondrial transporter SLC25A3\text{SLC25A3} to plasma membrane caveolae and lipid rafts to shuttle excess copper directly into mitochondria [46](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/400119496_Cuproptosis_and_Its_Impact_on_Cardiovascular_Health_Mechanisms_and_Therapeutic_Opportunities). Similarly, myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, sepsis, and hyperglycemia upregulate copper influx and ferredoxin 1 (FDX1\text{FDX1}) expression [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). FDX1\text{FDX1} reduces Cu2+\text{Cu}^{2+} to the more toxic Cu+\text{Cu}^+, which directly binds to lipoylated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes—specifically dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT\text{DLAT})—inducing disulfide-bond-dependent oligomerization and insoluble aggregation of lipoylated proteins alongside the destabilization and loss of iron-sulfur (Fe-S\text{Fe-S}) cluster proteins [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). This dual insult triggers acute mitochondrial shrinkage, plasma membrane rupture, and fatal proteotoxic stress, accelerating endothelial dysfunction, vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) loss in aortic aneurysms, and infarct expansion [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). Conversely, the second mechanism involves ischemic myocardial copper depletion and mislocalization [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). Paradoxically, during prolonged myocardial infarction, myocardial tissue copper concentrations progressively decline while serum copper levels simultaneously rise [45](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2025.1525585/full). This efflux of copper from ischemic cardiomyocytes into the circulation deactivates HIF-1α\text{HIF-1}\alpha binding to hypoxic response elements, halting VEGF-mediated angiogenesis and promoting cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm). Preclinical models demonstrate that targeted copper repletion or small molecules that modulate copper transport—such as paeoniflorin downregulating FDX1\text{FDX1} or phillygenin promoting autophagy-lysosome degradation of CTR1\text{CTR1}—can restore angiogenic capacity or block acute reperfusion cuproptosis [45](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2025.1525585/full).

Because generic copper depletion would impair intracellular Cu+\text{Cu}^+ enzymes, translational efforts have focused on trientine (triethylenetetramine [TETA]), an orally active chelator that selectively binds extracellular and coronary divalent copper (Cu2+\text{Cu}^{2+})—the oxidative species elevated in diabetic and hypertrophic states—without stripping essential monovalent Cu+\text{Cu}^+ [44](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3602174/). ## Physiological Divalent Cations: The Magnesium and Calcium Paradoxes

### Intravenous Magnesium in Acute Myocardial Infarction

Magnesium functions as an endogenous physiological calcium channel antagonist, inhibiting L-type calcium influx to attenuate calcium-induced mitochondrial injury during ischemia, suppressing systemic catecholamine release, promoting coronary vasodilation, and stabilizing myocardial potassium and calcium channels against reentry and triggered automaticity [7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1776926/full). Despite this compelling pharmacology, clinical trials of intravenous magnesium in AMI historically produced one of cardiology's sharpest controversies [7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1776926/full).

In the early 1990s, the Second Leicester Intravenous Magnesium Intervention Trial (LIMIT-2) and several smaller RCTs (typically infusing magnesium for 48 hours) demonstrated significant reductions in early post-MI mortality [20](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/bitstreams/d59dc43a-5fc9-42ef-b79b-29883a9a8d20/download). However, the subsequent Fourth International Study of Infarct Survival (ISIS-4)—a mega-trial randomizing 58,05058,050 patients with suspected AMI to a 24-hour infusion of intravenous magnesium sulfate—found zero reduction in 35-day mortality [20](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/bitstreams/d59dc43a-5fc9-42ef-b79b-29883a9a8d20/download). A Cochrane systematic review of 26 RCTs (spanning doses of 8 to 80 mmol8\text{ to }80\text{ mmol} over 5 hours to 14 days5\text{ hours to }14\text{ days}) highlighted this statistical heterogeneity: a fixed-effect meta-analysis (heavily weighted by ISIS-4) showed no mortality benefit (OR 0.990.99; 95% CI: 0.94–1.0495\%\text{ CI: }0.94–1.04), whereas a random-effects model indicated a 34%34\% mortality reduction (OR 0.660.66; 95% CI: 0.53–0.8295\%\text{ CI: }0.53–0.82) [20](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/bitstreams/d59dc43a-5fc9-42ef-b79b-29883a9a8d20/download). Across both statistical models, however, IV magnesium consistently and significantly decreased ventricular tachycardia (fixed-effect OR 0.450.45; 95% CI: 0.31–0.6695\%\text{ CI: }0.31–0.66; random-effects OR 0.400.40; 95% CI: 0.19–0.8495\%\text{ CI: }0.19–0.84) and severe arrhythmias requiring treatment (Lown grades 2–5: fixed-effect OR 0.720.72; 95% CI: 0.60–0.8595\%\text{ CI: }0.60–0.85; random-effects OR 0.510.51; 95% CI: 0.33–0.7995\%\text{ CI: }0.33–0.79), with no protective effect on cardiogenic shock or high-grade heart block [20](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/bitstreams/d59dc43a-5fc9-42ef-b79b-29883a9a8d20/download).

The reconciliation of LIMIT-2 and ISIS-4 rests on reperfusion kinetics [20](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/bitstreams/d59dc43a-5fc9-42ef-b79b-29883a9a8d20/download). Preclinical reperfusion models demonstrate that magnesium protects cardiomyocytes against lethal calcium overload only if elevated extracellular magnesium is present at the exact moment of coronary reflow [20](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/bitstreams/d59dc43a-5fc9-42ef-b79b-29883a9a8d20/download). In LIMIT-2, magnesium was administered prior to or during reperfusion; in ISIS-4, magnesium infusions were initiated late—after the initial lytic phase of fibrinolytic therapy—when reperfusion injury had already occurred [20](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/bitstreams/d59dc43a-5fc9-42ef-b79b-29883a9a8d20/download).

Furthermore, while routine magnesium infusion in stable, reperfused AMI patients is obsolete, a large 2026 propensity-score-matched cohort analysis of the MIMIC-IV (v3.1) database evaluating 5,6505,650 critically ill patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) with AMI (characterized by hemodynamic instability, severe arrhythmias, or acute heart failure) demonstrated that IV magnesium sulfate administration was independently associated with a 45%45\% reduction in ICU mortality (HR 0.550.55; 95% CI: 0.42–0.7195\%\text{ CI: }0.42–0.71; p<0.001p < 0.001), an inverse probability of treatment weighting HR of 0.560.56 (95% CI: 0.47–0.6895\%\text{ CI: }0.47–0.68), and an absolute restricted mean survival time (RMST) gain of 1.64 days1.64\text{ days} at 14 days (E-value =2.66= 2.66) [7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1776926/full). Dose-response modeling revealed a therapeutic window in which survival benefit was maximized at moderate cumulative doses of 2.0–8.0 g2.0–8.0\text{ g} administered over 1.08–3.21 days1.08–3.21\text{ days} [7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1776926/full). Thus, in high-acuity ICU settings where catecholamine surges and malignant ventricular arrhythmias predominate, targeted IV magnesium repletion remains highly efficacious [7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1776926/full).

### Oral Magnesium in Vascular Calcification and Arterial Stiffness

In chronic kidney disease and diabetes, hyperphosphatemia and uremic toxins trigger vascular smooth muscle cells to dedifferentiate into an osteogenic phenotype via the sodium-phosphate cotransporter Pit-1\text{Pit-1}, accelerating medial and intimal vascular calcification [57](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jasn/abstract/10.1681/asn.2018111150~a-randomized-trial-of-magnesium-oxide-and-oral-carbon). Because magnesium inhibits phosphate-induced VSMC calcification *in vitro*  via TRPM7\text{TRPM7} channels and suppresses osteogenic transcription factors [58](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/bmjopen/7/6/e016795.full.pdf), oral magnesium supplementation was widely proposed as an inexpensive anti-calcification therapy [58](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/bmjopen/7/6/e016795.full.pdf).

An early 2-year, open-label, 2×22 \times 2 factorial randomized trial by Sakaguchi et al. in stage 3–4 CKD patients with risk factors for coronary artery calcification was terminated prematurely after an interim analysis of 125125 patients (9696 completing follow-up) showed that oral magnesium oxide (MgO\text{MgO}) significantly blunted the median percentage increase in CAC score compared to control (11.3%11.3\% vs. 39.5%39.5\%), reducing the proportion of patients with annualized CAC progression ≥15%\ge 15\% from 62.0%62.0\% to 23.9%23.9\% (though without slowing thoracic aorta calcification and with a 27%27\% vs. 17%17\% dropout rate due to diarrhea) [57](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jasn/abstract/10.1681/asn.2018111150~a-randomized-trial-of-magnesium-oxide-and-oral-carbon).

However, when tested under rigorous double-blind, placebo-controlled conditions, oral magnesium failed to alter vascular calcification or stiffness. In the 2023 multicenter, double-blind MAGiCAL-CKD trial, 148148 patients with predialysis CKD (median eGFR 25 mL/min/1.73 m225\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2, range 15–45 mL/min15–45\text{ mL/min}) were randomized to slow-release oral magnesium hydroxide (15 mmol15\text{ mmol} twice daily, 30 mmol/day30\text{ mmol/day} total) or matching placebo for 12 months (135135 completing week 52 CAC imaging) [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125639/). Despite achieving a statistically significant elevation in plasma magnesium in the active arm, baseline-adjusted 12-month CAC scores did not differ between groups, nor across prespecified subgroups stratified by baseline CAC>0\text{CAC} > 0, diabetes, or tertiles of serum calcification propensity [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125639/). Moreover, magnesium supplementation caused substantial gastrointestinal adverse effects (3535 patients [47%47\%] vs. 99 patients in placebo) and a numerically higher incidence of major adverse events, with 55 deaths and 66 cardiovascular events in the magnesium group compared to 22 deaths and 00 cardiovascular events in the placebo group [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125639/). Similarly, in a 2026 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Meer et al. evaluating 7474 older individuals with T2DM, peripheral medial arterial calcification (MAC), and stiff arteries, 6-month oral magnesium citrate supplementation (350 mg/day350\text{ mg/day}) significantly increased serum magnesium and 24-hour urinary magnesium excretion (from 3.303.30 to 4.76 mmol/L4.76\text{ mmol/L} vs. ∼4.30 mmol/L\sim 4.30\text{ mmol/L} in placebo; p<0.001p < 0.001) [59](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/server/api/core/bitstreams/5346ca75-81a1-48de-bb16-1994be5f2be1/content). Yet supplementation failed to improve serum calciprotein particle crystallization maturation time (T50T_{50} overall treatment effect β=4 min\beta = 4\text{ min}; 95% CI: −12 to 2195\%\text{ CI: }-12\text{ to }21; p=0.625p = 0.625) and failed to reduce carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV\text{cfPWV} treatment effect β=0.8 m/s\beta = 0.8\text{ m/s}; 95% CI: 0.1 to 1.595\%\text{ CI: }0.1\text{ to }1.5; p=0.029p = 0.029, favoring placebo due to a decrease in the placebo arm) [59](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/server/api/core/bitstreams/5346ca75-81a1-48de-bb16-1994be5f2be1/content). Together, MAGiCAL-CKD and the Meer et al. trial establish a critical mechanistic principle: elevating plasma and urinary magnesium via oral supplementation in normomagnesemic or moderately hypomagnesemic patients is insufficient to reverse established hydroxyapatite crystal growth in the arterial wall once osteogenic VSMC transformation has occurred [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125639/).

### The Calcium Supplementation Hazard

The most striking example of iatrogenic harm resulting from plasma metal ion modulation is the calcium supplementation paradox. In observational meta-analyses of dietary calcium intake (ranging from 200 to 1,500 mg/day200\text{ to }1,500\text{ mg/day}, derived predominantly from dairy food matrices), dietary calcium exhibits no adverse association with CVD and frequently follows a protective U-shaped curve, with the lowest cardiovascular mortality observed at approximately 800 mg/day800\text{ mg/day} and a 15%15\% to 17%17\% reduction in stroke and CVD risk at moderate intakes (<1,000–1,400 mg/day<1,000–1,400\text{ mg/day}) [60](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07315724.2019.1649219). Within a dairy matrix, slow digestive absorption and accompanying nutrients prevent abrupt spikes in circulating ionized calcium [60](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07315724.2019.1649219).

In contrast, oral calcium supplements (500 to 1,400 mg/day500\text{ to }1,400\text{ mg/day} of elemental calcium as carbonate or citrate) are absorbed as a rapid bolus, transiently elevating extracellular ionized plasma calcium concentrations for several hours post-ingestion [24](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/2/368). Experimental and clinical studies indicate that acute hypercalcemia activates calcium-sensing receptors (CaSR\text{CaSR}) on platelets, increasing blood coagulability and arterial thrombus formation while accelerating vascular calcification [26](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.d2040). While calcium supplements produce a negligible reduction in systolic blood pressure (0.15 to 0.4 mmHg0.15\text{ to }0.4\text{ mmHg} per 100 mg100\text{ mg} intake) and inconsistent effects on serum lipids [25](https://www.uspharmacist.com/article/calcium-supplementation-and-cardiovascular-risk), large-scale syntheses demonstrate a significant hazard for ischemic cardiovascular events [24](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/2/368). In patient-level and trial-level meta-analyses of calcium supplements alone (>18,000>18,000 participants) conducted by Bolland et al. (*BMJ*  2010), supplementation increased the incidence of myocardial infarction by approximately 30%30\%, yielding a 5-year number needed to harm (NNH\text{NNH}) of 6969 for one incident MI [25](https://www.uspharmacist.com/article/calcium-supplementation-and-cardiovascular-risk). When Bolland et al. (*BMJ*  2011) reanalyzed the Women's Health Initiative Calcium/Vitamin D (WHI CaD) dataset—restricting the analysis to women not taking personal, non-protocol calcium supplements at baseline—and pooled trial-level data across 29,00029,000 participants, calcium with or without vitamin D significantly increased the relative risk of myocardial infarction (RR 1.251.25; 95% CI: 1.08–1.4595\%\text{ CI: }1.08–1.45; p=0.003p = 0.003), stroke (RR 1.151.15; 95% CI: 1.00–1.3295\%\text{ CI: }1.00–1.32; p=0.06p = 0.06), and the composite of MI or stroke (RR 1.151.15; 95% CI: 1.04–1.2795\%\text{ CI: }1.04–1.27; p=0.008p = 0.008) [26](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.d2040). Quantifying the net clinical trade-off, treating 1,0001,000 patients with calcium (with or without vitamin D) for 5 years causes 6 additional myocardial infarctions or strokes while preventing only 3 fractures (number needed to treat [NNT]=302[\text{NNT}] = 302 for fracture) [26](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.d2040). Corroborating these findings, a 2021 random-effects meta-analysis by Myung et al. evaluating 13 double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs (14 trial arms, N=28,935N = 28,935, 92.8%92.8\% postmenopausal women, mean age 66.366.3 years) confirmed that calcium supplementation significantly increased total CVD risk by 15%15\% (RR 1.151.15; 95% CI: 1.06–1.2595\%\text{ CI: }1.06–1.25; I2=0.0%I^2 = 0.0\%) and CHD risk by 16%16\% (RR 1.161.16; 95% CI: 1.05–1.2895\%\text{ CI: }1.05–1.28; I2=0.0%I^2 = 0.0\%), with a non-significant trend toward cerebrovascular disease (RR 1.131.13; 95% CI: 0.97–1.3195\%\text{ CI: }0.97–1.31) [24](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/2/368). Furthermore, a nationwide retrospective cohort study of the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) with a median follow-up of 6.766.76 years similarly found higher cumulative incidences of acute MI (4.6%4.6\% vs. 4.1%4.1\%), ischemic stroke (11.9%11.9\% vs. 11.0%11.0\%), and all-cause death (adjusted HR 1.471.47; 95% CI: 1.40–1.5495\%\text{ CI: }1.40–1.54) among calcium supplement users [62](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9230596/). Although several competing meta-analyses—including Lewis et al. (2015), Chung et al. (2016), and a 2023 *Heart, Lung and Circulation*  synthesis of 12 RCTs—reported null associations between calcium supplementation and verified CHD hospitalization or mortality [62](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9230596/), the consistency of the MI signal in non-confounded placebo-controlled cohorts has shifted clinical consensus away from routine calcium supplementation toward obtaining calcium strictly through dietary sources [25](https://www.uspharmacist.com/article/calcium-supplementation-and-cardiovascular-risk).

## Essential Antioxidant Trace Elements: Selenium and Zinc

### Selenium and the KiSel-10 Paradigm

Selenium exerts cardiovascular protection primarily through its incorporation as selenocysteine into antioxidant selenoproteins—including glutathione peroxidases (GPx\text{GPx}), thioredoxin reductases (TrxR\text{TrxR}), and selenoprotein P—which neutralize lipid hydroperoxides, preserve endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, and protect mitochondrial respiratory complexes [65](https://seleniumfacts.com/index.php/kisel-10-study). Because selenoprotein synthesis requires sufficient circulating selenium alongside ubiquinone (coenzyme Q10\text{Q}_{10} [CoQ10\text{CoQ}_{10}], which regenerates reduced antioxidant forms and supports mitochondrial ATP synthesis), combined supplementation has been tested in populations with low baseline soil selenium [65](https://seleniumfacts.com/index.php/kisel-10-study).

The landmark KiSel-10 trial (Alehagen et al.) randomized independently living elderly Swedish citizens to a daily combination of 200 μg200\ \mu\text{g} organic selenium yeast (*SelenoPrecise* ) and 200 mg200\text{ mg} coenzyme Q10\text{Q}_{10} (*Bio-Quinone* ) versus placebo [66](https://www.pharmanord.co.uk/news/kisel-10-heart-health-study). Active supplementation reduced cardiovascular mortality by 53%53\%, significantly lowered plasma NT-proBNP\text{NT-proBNP} (reflecting reduced myocardial wall stress), and improved echocardiographic cardiac function and quality of life [66](https://www.pharmanord.co.uk/news/kisel-10-heart-health-study). Remarkably, a 12-year longitudinal follow-up demonstrated that the survival advantage persisted long after the intervention phase ended, with a cumulative cardiovascular mortality rate of 28.1%28.1\% in the selenium plus CoQ10\text{CoQ}_{10} group compared to 38.7%38.7\% in the placebo group [66](https://www.pharmanord.co.uk/news/kisel-10-heart-health-study).

However, two rigorous caveats govern the clinical translation of KiSel-10. First, because KiSel-10 administered selenium and CoQ10\text{CoQ}_{10} as a single combined intervention without separate monotherapy arms, the non-factorial trial design cannot isolate the independent cardioprotective effect of plasma selenium modulation from that of CoQ10\text{CoQ}_{10} [69](https://biohackblueprint.org/blogs/coq10/coq10-and-selenium). Second, baseline selenium status dictates position along a U-shaped risk curve: northern European cohorts have notoriously low baseline soil and plasma selenium (<70–85 μg/L<70–85\ \mu\text{g/L}), operating on the ascending limb of selenoprotein synthesis. In selenium-replete populations, epidemiological cohorts demonstrate a U-shaped curve where additional selenium supplementation fails to increase selenoprotein activity and instead correlates with dyslipidemia and hyperglycemic risk, as evidenced by the PCA mixture analyses linking combined high plasma selenium and zinc to elevated LDL-C and blood pressure [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5933370/).

### Zinc Homeostasis and Cardiometabolic Risk Factor Modification

Zinc plays a multifaceted structural and regulatory role in cardiovascular physiology [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). Intracellularly, zinc serves as the catalytic cofactor for copper-zinc superoxide dismutase (Cu/Zn-SOD\text{Cu/Zn-SOD}) and induces the transcription of metallothioneins (MTs\text{MTs}), which scavenge hydroxyl radicals [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). Zinc also exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects by activating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPAR-α\text{PPAR-}\alpha and PPAR-γ\text{PPAR-}\gamma) and the zinc-finger protein A20\text{A20}, which inhibit NF-κB\text{NF-}\kappa\text{B}-inducing kinase (NIK\text{NIK}) and IKK\text{IKK}, thereby suppressing downstream transcription of IL-1β\text{IL-1}\beta, IL-6\text{IL-6}, TNF-α\text{TNF-}\alpha, MCP-1\text{MCP-1}, iNOS\text{iNOS}, and COX2\text{COX2} [71](https://www.mdpi.com/1422-0067/24/8/7152). Within the vascular endothelium, zinc and metallothionein are required to maintain endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS\text{eNOS}) coupling and nitric oxide (NO\text{NO}) vasodilatory function [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). In cardiomyocytes, zinc transporters of the SLC30A\text{SLC30A} (ZnT\text{ZnT}) and SLC39A\text{SLC39A} (ZIP\text{ZIP}) families—polymorphisms of which (ZnT1, ZnT4–7, ZnT9\text{ZnT1, ZnT4–7, ZnT9}) are linked to T2DM and dyslipidemia—regulate cytoplasmic zinc waves [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). Specifically, zinc influx via TRPC6\text{TRPC6} channels enhances β\beta-adrenergic receptor-mediated Gs\text{G}_\text{s} signaling to augment myocardial contractility, while cytosolic zinc regulates sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium release by modulating ryanodine receptor 2 (RyR2\text{RyR2}) and MG23\text{MG23} channel gating during ischemia [71](https://www.mdpi.com/1422-0067/24/8/7152).

Clinical evidence for oral zinc supplementation—synthesized across multiple GRADE-assessed systematic reviews and dose-response meta-analyses of RCTs (including Pompano & Boy 2020; *Biological Trace Element Research*  2020; and *Journal of Trace Elements in Medicine and Biology*  2023)—demonstrates robust efficacy for modifying surrogate cardiometabolic risk factors, alongside a notable absence of hard clinical outcome trials [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). Meta-analyses confirm that oral zinc supplementation significantly reduces fasting blood glucose (FBG), homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR\text{HOMA-IR}), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c\text{HbA1c}), total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglycerides, while elevating atheroprotective HDL-C in insulin-resistant, obese, and T2DM populations [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). Pooled RCT data (spanning doses of 11 to 50 mg/day11\text{ to }50\text{ mg/day} over 2 to 72 weeks2\text{ to }72\text{ weeks}) also consistently demonstrate significant reductions in circulating C-reactive protein (CRP\text{CRP}) and lipid peroxidation products, with variable reductions in IL-6\text{IL-6}, while ongoing clinical trials (such as NCT05000762 at Wayne State University) are evaluating whether oral zinc supplementation in patients with T2DM or prediabetes (HbA1c 6%–9%\text{HbA1c } 6\%–9\%) directly reduces *in vivo*  platelet aggregation and vascular morbidity [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). Crucially, the dose-response meta-analysis by Pompano and Boy revealed a striking dose-duration inversion: low-dose zinc supplementation (<25 mg/day<25\text{ mg/day}, specifically 9.2–25 mg/day9.2–25\text{ mg/day}) and long-duration interventions (≥12 weeks\ge 12\text{ weeks}) produce larger effect sizes across a wider array of risk factors (FBG, HOMA-IR\text{HOMA-IR}, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL-C) than high-dose boluses (≥25 mg/day\ge 25\text{ mg/day}) or short-duration regimens (<12 weeks<12\text{ weeks}) [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). High-dose zinc boluses saturate intestinal transporters, reducing fractional absorption and potentially interfering with copper homeostasis. Consequently, agronomic biofortification of staple food crops (which delivers an additional 5–9 mg/day5–9\text{ mg/day} of elemental zinc divided physiologically across daily meals) is emerging as a superior population-level preventive strategy compared to high-dose pharmacological pills [70](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.686078/full). Importantly, neither low- nor high-dose zinc supplementation significantly reduces systolic or diastolic blood pressure in pooled RCT analyses [73](https://www.ovid.com/journals/adinu/fulltext/10.1093/advances/nmaa087~effects-of-dose-and-duration-of-zinc-interventions-on-risk).

### Translational Principles and Future Outlook

A synthesis of the mechanistic and clinical trial landscape yields four overarching principles that govern the future of cardiovascular metallomics. Foremost among these is the imperative of overcoming the compartmentalization barrier. The recurring failure of oral or systemic metal modulation—such as oral iron in IRONOUT-HF, oral magnesium in MAGiCAL-CKD, and IV EDTA in TACT2—demonstrates that extracellular plasma concentration is frequently decoupled from intracellular or vascular wall bioactivity [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125639/). Future therapeutics must target the specific transmembrane transporters and chaperones that govern subcellular distribution, such as inhibiting endothelial CTR1\text{CTR1} or mitochondrial SLC25A3\text{SLC25A3} and FDX1\text{FDX1} to prevent cuproptosis during myocardial reperfusion [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm), or restoring copper chaperones to reactivate HIF-1α\text{HIF-1}\alpha-driven angiogenesis in chronic ischemic myocardium where tissue copper is depleted despite elevated serum copper [2](https://www.ijbs.com/v22p2842.htm).

Equally critical is the role of pharmacokinetic delivery rate and food-matrix integration. The sharp divergence between dietary calcium (cardiovascularly safe or protective) and bolus calcium supplements (which elevate MI and CHD risk by 15%–30%15\%–30\%) [24](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/13/2/368), alongside the superiority of low-dose, long-duration zinc supplementation over high-dose boluses [73](https://www.ovid.com/journals/adinu/fulltext/10.1093/advances/nmaa087~effects-of-dose-and-duration-of-zinc-interventions-on-risk), proves that transient supraphysiological spikes in plasma cations trigger prothrombotic, pro-calcific, and dyslipidemic counter-regulatory cascades [26](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.d2040). Preventive nutritional strategies must therefore prioritize slow-release food-matrix delivery and agronomic crop biofortification over high-dose pharmacological boluses [60](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07315724.2019.1649219).

Furthermore, clinical trial failures underscore the irreversibility of late-stage structural pathology via extracellular cation manipulation. Both TACT2 and MAGiCAL-CKD illustrate that once chronic environmental heavy metal exposure or uremic mineral bone disorder has established advanced fibrocalcific plaques in the coronary arteries, late-stage manipulation of plasma lead or magnesium cannot reverse the entrenched structural lesion [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125639/). Against contaminant metals like lead, cadmium, and arsenic, primary prevention via municipal water infrastructure abatement and environmental policy remains far more effective than post-MI pharmacological chelation [22](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/clinical-trials/2024/04/05/04/40/tact2).

Finally, the clinical deployment of metal-modulating therapies requires precision stratification by baseline nutritional status, clinical acuity, and biological sex. Selenium supplementation is beneficial in deficient populations (KiSel-10) but potentially deleterious in replete individuals due to U-shaped risk curves [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5933370/). Intravenous magnesium fails when given late in unselected AMI (ISIS-4) but reduces mortality by 45%45\% when administered within a precise dose window (2.0–8.0 g2.0–8.0\text{ g}) to critically ill, hemodynamically unstable AMI patients in the ICU [7](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2026.1776926/full). Likewise, the discovery of a significant sex interaction in IV iron therapy for HFrEF—where men experience substantial reductions in hospitalizations and cardiovascular death while women show a neutral risk ratio of 0.980.98 [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency)—mandates sex-specific recalibration of diagnostic ferritin and transferrin saturation thresholds in upcoming heart failure trials [11](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390316318_Systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_intravenous_iron_therapy_for_patients_with_heart_failure_and_iron_deficiency).
