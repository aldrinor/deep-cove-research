# AI Fashion Design, Creative Diversity, and Designers’ Rights

**AI creates a credible convergence risk:** As of **13 September 2026**, the evidence supports concern that AI fashion-design tools can accelerate imitation and steer designers toward familiar aesthetics. It does not establish that these tools have caused industry-wide creative homogenization. Fashion studies also show faster experimentation, variable outputs, and useful assistance with early-stage design. The decisive distinction is between generating more alternatives for one designer and preserving diversity across different designers’ collections. [1][2]

**Copyright disputes need layered remedies:** The most workable response combines lawful training access, consent and licensing where required, collective arrangements that include independent designers, usable rights reservations, training transparency, provenance, output safeguards, and affordable enforcement. Copying a protected textile print, imitating a general aesthetic, and claiming copyright in an AI-assisted design are different legal problems; each needs a different evidentiary and remedial response. [3]

| Claim or situation | Decisive finding | Evidence that matters | First practical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Industry-wide homogenization | Credible risk; causation unproven | Between-designer diversity and novelty | Preserve independent creative direction |
| Training-data use | Permission and exceptions vary | Source, access, license, reservation | Seek disclosure and rights clearance |
| Exact or close output copying | Protected expression is central | Original work, output, commercial use | Preserve evidence; seek removal |
| Style or aesthetic resemblance | Style alone is a weaker copyright claim | Specific expression or other protected rights | Assess design rights and misleading attribution |
| AI-assisted authorship | Human creative control matters | Original inputs, edits, arrangements | Document the human contribution |

**The report follows the decisions:** First, it compares the tools and workflows actually involved. It then weighs the evidence on creative convergence, examines independent designers’ opportunities and exposure, separates the legal claims and named disputes, compares US, EU, and UK rules, and evaluates the remedies that can protect designers without suppressing useful experimentation.

## AI fashion-design tools and their actual uses

**Different workflows create different risks:** “AI fashion design” includes concept generation, image transformation, brand-specific asset production, virtual presentation, and automated trend selection. A tool that helps a designer explore an original sketch does not perform the same function as a system alleged to identify successful products and send copies into production. Treating both as one technology obscures where creative choices—and potential infringement—occur.

### Tools for concepts, variations, and production communication

**Seven documented offerings illustrate the differences:** CALA and DALL·E connect prompts and reference images to fashion concepts; Midjourney supports iterative visual exploration; Firefly Custom Models emphasize organizational consistency; The New Black combines fashion-specific generation and documentation; Runway supports moving-image presentation; and Stable Diffusion supplies general image generation, including through DreamStudio.

| Tool and provider | User inputs | Main operation | Outputs | Fashion use context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CALA — CALA | Text, templates, reference images | Generate six alternatives; regenerate | Apparel, accessory, shoe concepts | Independent designers and brand teams |
| DALL·E — OpenAI | Text and references through CALA | Translate descriptions into visuals | Product-design images and variations | CALA-integrated ideation |
| Firefly Custom Models — Adobe | Brand-approved assets and prompts | Fine-tune, test, refine, share | Brand-aligned creative assets | Enterprise creative and marketing teams |
| The New Black — The New Black | Prompts, sketches, product photos | Generate, transform, visualize | Designs, model imagery, tech packs | Fashion brands and design teams |
| Midjourney — Midjourney, Inc. | Keywords, parameters, image references | Generate, describe, vary | Concept images and visual references | Early-stage fashion exploration |
| Runway — Runway | Images, concepts, presentation material | Develop moving-image presentation | Fashion video and virtual-show material | Company and educational workflows |
| Stable Diffusion/DreamStudio — Stability AI | Prompts and visual references | Generate and transform images | Apparel imagery and other visuals | General visual creation and fashion tests |

These capabilities are described in CALA’s announcement, Adobe’s product documentation, The New Black’s product site, and fashion-design studies. They identify functions, not equivalent levels of manufacturing accuracy or commercial success. [4][5][6][7][8]

**CALA makes selection explicit:** Its October 2022 announcement describes choosing one of dozens of product templates, entering descriptors, receiving **six designs**, regenerating alternatives, and selecting a direction for further modification with teammates. Users can also upload an existing design to generate related variations for a cohesive collection. The human decision is therefore not merely whether to generate: it includes defining the brief, rejecting alternatives, modifying the chosen concept, and deciding what to produce. [4]

**Its use spans business sizes:** Apparel Resources reports more than **40 businesses and freelance designers**, including NBA teams and KidSuper, using CALA. It also describes an affiliated network of **20 plants in 10 countries**, including Vietnam, China, Turkey, Italy, and Portugal. That connects visual ideation with a global production network, although the user count does not measure how much design work each customer delegates to AI. [9]

**Midjourney offers a different interaction:** The 2024 fashion study describes `/imagine` producing four preliminary images, `/describe` translating reference-image characteristics into text, and the variation function producing four additional images. Designers repeatedly adjust language, references, and parameters before selecting useful results. Reference weighting and consistent seeds can strengthen similarity, while other settings encourage less predictable alternatives: the interface supports both consistency and exploration. [2]

**Fashion specialization changes the deliverable:** The New Black promotes fabric and color changes, flat and ghost-mannequin images, generated models, virtual try-on, Shopify connections, and tech packs containing size charts, grading, bills of material, and flat sketches. A tech pack is production documentation; its generation is a different claim from producing an attractive concept image. Designers still need to evaluate whether the specifications accurately describe a constructible garment. [6]

**Runway often enters later:** In the Artificial A(i)rchive laboratory at Politecnico di Milano, work moved from archival research and moodboards through sketches, materials, digital patterns, and CLO3D virtual prototypes before Runway supported video or runway direction. This illustrates AI distributed across a workflow, rather than one system independently designing and manufacturing a collection. [7]

### Training, uploaded inputs, and output rights

**The business terms differ materially:** Training provenance concerns the material used to develop a model. Input handling concerns what happens to a designer’s uploaded sketch, photograph, prompt, or confidential collection. Output allocation concerns the relationship between platform and customer. These questions should be separated when selecting a service.

| Offering | Documented training or input practice | Output-rights position | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| CALA | Prompts and uploaded references guide generation | Integration agreement and local law govern | Clear reference rights and confidentiality |
| Standalone DALL·E | Text-prompt generation; CALA integration documented | Applicable product agreement and local law govern | Distinguish platform from integration terms |
| Adobe Firefly | Licensed and public-domain foundation training described | Enterprise customer owns output between the parties | Check statutory protection and indemnity scope |
| The New Black | Prompt, sketch, reference, and product-photo inputs | Commercial use promoted; dated agreement governs allocation | Match permissions to intended sale and publication |
| Midjourney | Public, user-interaction, and synthetic data described | Ownership qualified by law; broad platform license | Consider publicity, remixing, and confidentiality |
| Runway | Fashion presentation inputs documented | Applicable service agreement governs allocation | Separate presentation rights from garment rights |
| Stable Diffusion/DreamStudio | Training challenged in artist litigation | Model license and hosted-service terms govern | Identify the actual model and deployment |

**Adobe provides a useful contractual comparison:** Its 2024 enterprise FAQ says Firefly foundation models were trained on licensed material such as Adobe Stock and public-domain content. It says Adobe does not plan to include enterprise inputs and outputs in foundation-model training, while allowing customer-specific fine-tuning and identifying feedback or improvement-program exceptions. That is a defined enterprise data-use position, not a statement that a service never stores content. [10]

**Ownership is not the same as exclusivity:** The same FAQ allocates Firefly output to the enterprise customer as between Adobe and that customer, but says local law determines copyright protection and outputs are not guaranteed unique or exclusive. Eligible customers can purchase conditional infringement indemnity—financial protection against specified claims—with exclusions and liability limits. A contractual allocation, an enforceable copyright, and insurance-like risk protection are therefore separate benefits. [10]

**Midjourney takes a more public-facing approach:** Its terms effective 27 May 2026 make content publicly viewable and remixable by default and grant Midjourney a perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free license over inputs and generated assets. Users own assets to the fullest extent possible under applicable law, subject to third-party rights and the agreement. Companies exceeding **US$1 million in annual revenue** need a Pro or Mega plan for company ownership; Stealth is described as best efforts, and shared spaces remain visible to their participants. [11]

**Its training disclosure gives scale, not clearance:** Midjourney’s March 2026 summary reports **more than 1 billion images**, **more than 1 million hours of video**, and **less than 1 billion text tokens**. It describes collection from 2023 onward, user interactions and synthetic data, and says its crawlers respect robots.txt and exclude opted-out sources. These are provider disclosures about collection and processing, distinct from a work-by-work determination of permission. [12]

**Contract-sensitive questions have one deciding condition:** For CALA, its DALL·E integration, The New Black, Runway, Stable Diffusion deployments, and Yoona.ai, exact retention, deletion, training-reuse permissions, commercial restrictions, attribution obligations, exclusivity, and indemnity turn on the dated agreement, applicable product configuration, and any supporting data-use or audit record. A designer should require those commitments to cover the actual workflow, including connected services, before uploading an unreleased collection.

## What the evidence says about creative homogenization

**More images do not settle diversity:** Creative homogenization means a reduction in meaningful differences between designers’ outputs: increasingly similar silhouettes, motifs, palettes, compositions, body representations, or collection identities. It is not synonymous with high output volume, attractive rendering, or the ability to generate many variations.

### Measuring the right kind of similarity

**Three levels must remain separate:** Variation within one designer’s session, distinctiveness between different designers, and diversity among products that consumers actually see can move in different directions. A designer might explore more alternatives while the market becomes dominated by similar best-selling looks. Conversely, visually consistent tools could help more niche designers bring distinctive concepts to market.

| Indicator | What it measures | What would support homogenization | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Between-designer similarity | Resemblance across portfolios | Greater similarity under comparable briefs | Whether protected expression was copied |
| Novelty against prior collections | Distance from existing design patterns | Fewer genuinely different combinations | Commercial usefulness |
| Representation diversity | Range of bodies, identities, traditions | Narrower representation after AI adoption | Manufacturing fit or accessibility |
| Market-visible diversity | Variety receiving exposure or sales | Concentration around fewer aesthetic clusters | The diversity of discarded concepts |

**Comparison needs a counterfactual:** The relevant question is not whether two AI-generated dresses look alike, but whether comparable designers would have produced more distinct work without the tool. Common briefs, shared seasonal trends, fabrics, price points, manufacturing constraints, and buyer preferences can independently cause convergence. AI’s causal contribution is the additional convergence attributable to its use after those influences are considered.

**Image similarity is only a proxy:** A photograph’s background, model, lighting, and camera angle can dominate an automated visual comparison. For fashion, useful analysis must distinguish the garment from its presentation and separate ordinary category features from distinctive design choices. Two white shirts may be visually close but differ substantially in construction; two differently photographed garments may reproduce the same protected print.

**The strongest results concern assistance and control:** The available fashion-specific studies examine ideation, prompt following, fidelity, usability, and designer interaction. Together they support the usefulness of AI-assisted visual work and identify constraints on expressive control.

Participants used Midjourney and DALL·E with text and sketches. Their sketch-experiment ratings favored realistic visualization more than originality or control. [13]

| 1 | Visual fidelity and realism | 4.25 |

**Polish can outrun creative agency:** Visual fidelity exceeded originality by **1.00 scale point**, calculated as 4.25 − 3.25. That descriptive gap helps explain why impressive imagery can coexist with dissatisfaction: a tool may make a concept look finished before it gives the designer adequate control over what makes it distinctive. It does not, by itself, show that different designers’ collections became more alike. [13]

**Task specialization produces a second contrast:** Tocchetti and colleagues initially compared OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, Stability AI’s DreamStudio, Leonardo AI’s service, and The New Black. Their detailed validation used **450 archival fashion images plus 40 web-sourced images**, with **350 tests split into 200 archival and 150 web tests**. Experts assessed fidelity to prompts and accuracy relative to source images. [14]

| Task | Measure | ChatGPT | The New Black |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free-text to image | Expert-rated fidelity | 72.5% | 70.0% |
| Guided text to image | Expert-rated fidelity | 67.8% | 73.7% |
| Image to image | Expert-rated fidelity | 62.5% | 69.5% |

**Better fidelity can serve opposite purposes:** The New Black performed better on guided prompts and image transformation, while ChatGPT led on free-text generation. Fidelity can help a designer preserve an original intention; it can also make reproducing somebody else’s reference easier. The score describes successful instruction following, not whether the instruction was creative, authorized, or commercially desirable.

**Sketch translation remained demanding:** In the same study, ChatGPT achieved **36% image-to-sketch accuracy** and **66% sketch-to-image fidelity**, but **32% sketch-to-image accuracy**. The New Black sometimes applied a monochrome filter rather than creating the requested sketch and was excluded from those task comparisons. This separates convincing appearance from reliable structural interpretation. [14]

**Midjourney demonstrates real experimentation:** The action-research project generated 272 images during concept development, 244 during design development, and approximately 151 during validation. The calculated total is **approximately 667 images**: 272 + 244 + approximately 151. Repeated prompts and variations produced different outputs, while designers repeatedly evaluated and refined them. [2]

**Consumer approval measures another outcome:** Its validation involved **174 US university students** assessing selected hemp-fashion images. Mean attitude was **5.78 on a seven-point scale**, alongside positive functional, expressive, and aesthetic perceptions. That supports the appeal of selected concepts to that sample; it does not turn the number of generated images into a measure of cultural diversity or prove that the pictured garments would perform as depicted. [2]

**Automatic metrics answer narrower questions:** The IEEE evaluation uses CLIP Score, Image Reward, and Inception Score alongside human judgment. These concern text–image compatibility, predicted preferences, and characteristics of image quality or distribution. Similarly, the apparel-design preprint’s approximately **0.2 LPIPS** result measures perceptual resemblance to a reference, while its approximately **80% basic-construction** and **55% detailed-construction** classification results concern recognition. None is interchangeable with a between-designer creativity measure. [8][15]

### Why convergence remains plausible

**Defaults can become shared starting points:** Ledendal, Petersson McIntyre, and Christoforidou’s 2026 walkthrough of four fashion and textile platforms describes creative work shifting from sketching and draping toward prompt-writing. Interfaces, suggested options, and aesthetic defaults configure what users are encouraged to make. If many designers accept similar starting points and make limited changes, distinct creative intentions can converge before commercial selection begins. [1]

**Training patterns shape available possibilities:** A model’s output reflects patterns learned from its training material, combined with user inputs and generation settings. The fashion literature identifies risks of dominant-style reproduction and defaults that marginalize larger bodies, disabled people, atypical body shapes, or less represented identities. A system may generate numerous images while repeatedly centering the same narrow assumptions about whose body and aesthetic count as fashionable. [13][1]

**Brand consistency has two possible effects:** Firefly Custom Models and The New Black explicitly promote consistent brand imagery. Consistency within a brand is not necessarily homogenization across brands: separate models grounded in different original archives could preserve distinct identities. The risk increases when firms use the same generic references, select the same popular aesthetics, or repeatedly generate from outdated interpretations of their own identity. [5][6]

**Commercial feedback can amplify repetition:** The plausible chain is shared aesthetic inputs, familiar generated options, selection for immediate engagement, and rapid scaling of successful looks. SHEIN describes starting new products in batches of **100–200 pieces** before increasing production according to demand. That demonstrates a feedback-based business model; the separate claim that such a system systematically copies independent designers comes from litigation allegations, not from the batch size itself. [16]

**Randomness does not guarantee distinctiveness:** Different generated images can occupy the same narrow aesthetic territory. Changing buttons, backgrounds, or surface details may increase the number of outputs without meaningfully expanding silhouettes, cultural references, or design approaches. Conversely, a skilled designer can use a familiar model to combine unusual references and then transform the result through drawing, draping, material experimentation, and editing.

### Counterexamples and the resulting judgment

**Named experiments show several creative purposes:** The fashion-industry literature documents AI collaborations and presentation experiments that are better understood as exploration than as evidence of a single industry aesthetic.

| Named example | Period | Documented use | What it illuminates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acne Studios and Robbie Barrat | 2020 | AI-related menswear collaboration | Designer–artist experimentation |
| Moncler, Maison Meta, and WeSayHi | 2023 | London Fashion Week visuals | Campaign and presentation applications |
| Valentino Essential campaign | January 2023 | AI-related campaign experimentation | Marketing imagery rather than garment authorship |
| AI Fashion Week, Brooklyn | 2023 | More than 350 submissions | Participation and concept exploration |

Named fashion experiments [7]

**The causal judgment is conditional:** The homogenization conclusion would strengthen if comparable AI-assisted portfolios became more similar than non-AI portfolios after accounting for briefs, trends, model choice, references, and human editing. It would weaken if AI expanded between-designer diversity or enabled more distinctive niche collections to reach buyers. Until then, the defensible judgment is that convergence mechanisms are credible, while access and experimentation provide genuine counterweights.

**Section judgment:** AI can produce more variation within a session and still narrow the market’s aesthetic range; the evidence supports managing that risk, not declaring a proven industry-wide monoculture.

## What independent designers gain—and what they risk losing

**The distribution of benefits matters:** Independent designers can gain affordable visualization and communication capabilities while simultaneously facing stronger competition from high-volume operators. Lower production costs do not automatically translate into higher designer income, greater visibility, or more bargaining power.

### Access, productivity, and the physical garment

**Lower barriers can support originality:** CALA’s stated purpose includes helping experienced designers overcome creative blocks and enabling people without advanced sketching skills to communicate ideas. For an independent designer, visualizing an unusual concept before commissioning expensive development can make experimentation more feasible. The benefit is strongest when AI helps express a distinct intention rather than supplies the intention itself. [4]

**Cost comparisons need matching units:** A Cutter Consortium practitioner article places some collection-photography budgets at **US$50,000–100,000** and describes AI image generation as costing “a fraction of a cent.” A collection-level photography budget includes services that a generation cost does not necessarily replace, so those anchors should not be converted into a percentage saving. The meaningful opportunity is cheaper concept testing and visual communication, with subscriptions, selection, correction, product accuracy, and rights clearance still part of the workflow. [17]

**Small-brand accounts suggest augmentation:** A Botika practitioner interview describes one-person operations working at roughly **half the earlier budget**, testing demand before production, and communicating visually with factories. The same interview says AI had replaced no complete creative role in the interviewee’s account of work at Hermès: creative direction and drawing remained central, while research and photo editing changed substantially. [18]

**A compelling rendering is not construction:** Material behavior, fit, grading, seams, finishing, and production tolerances remain consequential. Cutter identifies gaps in material simulation and production readiness, while the fashion tests show difficulty with buttons, necklines, exact colors, and intricate construction. Independent designers should therefore assess AI by fewer failed samples, clearer specifications, or better decisions—not simply by images generated per hour. [17][15]

### Imitation, visibility, and bargaining power

**Imitation compresses the time advantage:** Cassey Ho, founder and head designer of Blogilates and Popflex, told InvestigateTV that designs typically take **1.5–3 years** from concept to market. Her pirouette skort launched in 2022, sold out within days, and gained further attention after Taylor Swift wore the original in 2024. Ho reported subsequent knockoffs and suspected manipulation of her promotional material; those experiences illustrate exposure following visibility, without establishing which copying involved AI. [16]

**Discovery can become a vulnerability:** An independent designer needs public visibility to attract customers and licensees. The same visibility makes successful motifs, images, and products easier for competitors to identify. If automated systems rapidly detect demand and imitate marketable features, the creator can bear the costs of invention and audience building while a larger operator captures some subsequent demand.

**Scale affects attention as well as price:** The competition is not limited to one original and one copy. High-volume generated listings and promotional images can compete for search positions, recommendation slots, and advertising attention. A Virginia Law Review analysis identifies platform incentives favoring scale and volume as a general mechanism; for fashion, the implication is that easier content production can intensify competition for discovery even when the outputs are not infringing. [19]

**Enforcement costs change practical rights:** Giana’s complaint alleges that one artist identified at least **nine products** using her work but could not afford individual litigation. As an adjacent graphic-design example, Dani Donovan reported approximately **2,500 fake listings removed in under a year** and continuing expenditure on monitoring. Donovan’s suspicion of automated listing generation should remain separate from the documented burden she described. [20][16]

**Attribution has economic significance:** Credit can direct buyers toward an original designer, help establish provenance, and strengthen a licensing negotiation. But a generated image does not automatically identify the creators whose work influenced training, and an AI-use label does not automatically credit a particular designer. Provenance can preserve a recorded creative history; it cannot by itself distribute attention or revenue fairly.

**Licensing power is uneven:** Large firms can negotiate around extensive archives, recognizable brands, and substantial legal resources. An individual designer offers a smaller repertoire and may face a standard-form platform agreement. Collective representation and interoperable evidence can improve that position by reducing the costs of proving ownership, signaling permissions, and negotiating use. [3]

## Copyright disputes: separate the claims before choosing a remedy

**Algorithms are not the legal counterparty:** Disputes concern the people and companies collecting data, training or modifying models, operating services, commissioning outputs, manufacturing products, and distributing listings. The responsible defendant and applicable defense depend on which act is challenged.

### Training, outputs, style, and authorship

**Training claims concern upstream copying:** The US Copyright Office distinguishes collection and curation, training, retrieval-augmented generation, and outputs. Downloading, storing, converting, and assembling copyrighted works can implicate reproduction rights before any consumer-facing output exists. The provider may invoke a license, a statutory exception, or fair use, depending on the jurisdiction and facts. [3]

**Output claims concern particular expression:** A designer alleging that a generated print reproduces an original illustration needs to identify the protected work and the expression reproduced, alongside the relevant display, sale, or distribution. The model’s general ability to generate images is not the infringement. Equally, a training license does not automatically authorize selling recognizable reproductions of individual works.

**Style is not a universal monopoly:** General ideas, techniques, moods, and aesthetics ordinarily receive weaker copyright protection than a particular original expression. “A romantic dress with layered tulle” is different from reproducing a designer’s original illustration or distinctive protected surface pattern. But a product described as a “dupe” can still implicate a design patent, registered design, trademark, trade dress, or other applicable right; the label does not decide legality. [16][24]

**Economic harm can exceed copyright’s reach:** A designer may lose commissions because inexpensive outputs imitate an aesthetic without copying legally protected expression. That is relevant to market structure, contract policy, and potentially unfair competition, but it does not automatically establish copyright infringement. Conversely, even a single sale of an unauthorized protected print can present a concrete infringement claim without proving any wider homogenization.

**Authorship asks a different question:** US guidance distinguishes AI assistance from AI replacing human expression. Human-authored material carried into an output, creative selection or arrangement, and expressive modifications can qualify for protection case by case. Prompts alone generally do not supply sufficient control under current generally available technology. Employment, commissioning, and assignment arrangements may then determine who owns the protectable human contribution. [25]

**A hypothetical shows the separation:** Suppose a designer supplies an original hand-drawn print, uses a generator to visualize it on a coat, and then redraws the composition and develops the garment manually. The original print and qualifying human revisions may remain protectable even if some generated details are not. If the reference instead reproduces somebody else’s protected print without a lawful basis, the resulting image may infringe even though the user cannot claim copyright in its machine-generated elements.

**Attribution needs its own evidence:** Copyright-management-information claims concern particular identifying information and its removal or alteration, together with required legal elements. False endorsement concerns misleading associations. Moral rights may protect attribution or integrity under national law. These theories should not be collapsed into the proposition that every stylistic resemblance must carry the imitated designer’s name.

**Andersen preserves questions rather than answers:** The October 2023 order retained a direct copyright theory while dismissing other claims with opportunities to amend. The August 2024 order allowed core direct copyright, induced-infringement, and certain Lanham Act theories to proceed, while dismissing DMCA §1202 claims. Accepting a theory as plausible at the pleading stage does not establish that model weights contain infringing copies or resolve fair use. [31]

**Its attribution issue was distinct:** The litigation analysis identifies problems specifying the copyright-management information allegedly removed and satisfying the relevant knowledge requirements. The lesson for designers is concrete: preserve the original files, embedded identifiers, publication records, accused outputs, and circumstances of removal. A broad claim of being stylistically imitated does not replace those elements. [34]

**Getty demonstrates territorial differences:** The reported UK outcome on **4 November 2025** involved training outside the UK, the treatment of model weights under the secondary-infringement theory, and narrow trademark findings concerning generated watermarks. The primary training claim had been dropped; the remaining copyright theory failed. In the US proceeding, Outside IP Counsel’s pre-cutoff account reports that copyright and trademark claims proceeded after an **23 April 2026** ruling, while a false-CMI claim was dismissed. Neither result establishes universal legality or illegality for image-model training. [32]

**Thaler establishes a narrower boundary:** The D.C. Circuit ruled on **18 March 2025** that copyright requires a human author; the Supreme Court denied review on **2 March 2026**. Thaler had identified his Creativity Machine as sole author and disclaimed traditional human authorship. The decision therefore does not prohibit protection for works made with AI assistance. The cited Allen challenge, involving more than **600 iterative prompts**, illustrates why effort and iteration must still be connected to control over final expression. [33]

## United States, European Union, and United Kingdom rules

**There is no global permission slip:** A cross-border fashion workflow can encounter different rules for training, authorship, garment appearance, attribution, and platform conduct. The location of training, the market in which a model is offered, and the place where an output is sold can each matter.

### Authorship, fashion protection, and training

| Legal question | United States | European Union | United Kingdom |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI-assisted authorship | Human expression and control assessed case by case | Human intellectual creation and creative choices central | Human-authored assistance distinguished from computer-generated works |
| AI-only output | Human-author requirement excludes AI as sole author | Generally outside human-centered copyright protection | Statutory computer-generated-work category retained in the current framework |
| Fashion protection | Separable artwork; other IP may protect product features | Design protection covers fashion-product appearance; copyright may overlap | Qualifying artistic works and applicable product-design rights |
| Commercial training | Fair-use analysis; no dedicated TDM exception | Article 4 TDM framework with rights reservation | Noncommercial-research exception does not broadly authorize commercial training |
| Research mining | Fair use depends on circumstances | Article 3 exception for qualifying institutions and purposes | Section 29A: lawful access and noncommercial research |

US copyrightability guidance [25] Comparative training rules [35] Fashion-IP comparison [24] UK consultation [36]

**US garment copyright is constrained:** Clothing is a useful article. Under the separability approach discussed in *Star Athletica v. Varsity Brands* in 2017, qualifying artistic features can receive copyright protection without creating a general monopoly over the garment’s practical form. Original textile prints, illustrations, photographs, and other separable expression therefore often present clearer copyright claims than an ordinary clothing silhouette. Trademarks, trade dress, and design patents protect different interests. [24]

**Fashion includes several protectable objects:** WIPO identifies original textile prints, surface patterns, sketches, lookbooks, photographs, and videos as potentially copyright-protected creative output. A dispute over a copied promotional photograph is consequently not answered by saying that clothing receives limited copyright protection. Nor does ownership of the photograph necessarily establish ownership of every underlying garment design. [37]

**EU design rights provide another route:** The comparative fashion analysis uses Italy’s implementation of EU design law to illustrate protection encompassing clothing, shoes, bags, and accessories without the US copyright useful-article barrier. That comparison concerns design protection and should not be converted into a claim that all fashion aesthetics are copyrighted throughout Europe. Rights still depend on the relevant eligibility requirements and the subject matter claimed. [24]

**US training turns on four factors:** Fair use considers purpose and character, the nature of the work, the amount and substantiality used, and market effects. The Copyright Office’s May 2025 report anticipates that some training uses will qualify and others will not. It distinguishes analysis or research with non-substitutive outputs from commercially producing competing expression, particularly through unlawful acquisition. Its recommendation is an analytical framework, not a judgment resolving every provider’s conduct. [3]

**EU mining exceptions have different conditions:** Article 3 of the Copyright in the Digital Single Market Directive addresses qualifying scientific research by research organizations and cultural-heritage institutions with lawful access. Article 4 provides a broader text-and-data-mining exception, subject to appropriate rights reservation, including machine-readable reservation for online material. Whether particular generative-AI activities fall within every part of this framework remains a substantive interpretive issue; the AI Act does not create a replacement copyright exception. [35]

**The UK remains distinct from both:** Section 29A permits mining for noncommercial research with lawful access, and the UK lacks the US’s broad fair-use doctrine. The government’s originally preferred commercial exception with opt-out was rejected as the preferred direction in its **18 March 2026** report. A consultation proposal should therefore not be treated as an enacted commercial-training permission. [23]

**UK computer-generated protection is also distinct:** Its statutory category dates to 1988 and attributes authorship to the person making the arrangements necessary for creation. The March 2026 government report proposes removing that protection absent evidence of continuing value, while retaining protection for AI-assisted human creation. A proposal to remove a right is not its repeal, and the category does not make the AI itself the author. [23]

### Liability, attribution, disclosure, and provenance

| Issue | United States | European Union | United Kingdom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infringing outputs | Protected expression and applicable liability rules | Existing copyright rights and exceptions apply | Reproduction of a substantial protected part may infringe |
| Platforms and operators | Direct and secondary liability; hosting procedures are distinct | Copyright rules plus applicable intermediary obligations | Responsibility depends on the act and territorial connection |
| Attribution | Particular statutory, contractual, and metadata claims | Moral rights vary across Member States | Attribution and integrity rules are qualified |
| Training disclosure | Contracts and litigation provide important routes | GPAI copyright policies and public training summaries | Transparency remains a central policy direction |
| Provenance | Credentials can support evidence | Credentials complement legal disclosure duties | Traceability supports proposed controls and redress |

US training report [3] EU copyright study [22] UK legal-policy consultation [36]

**EU transparency has an operational timetable:** Article 53 requires general-purpose AI providers to maintain copyright-compliance policies and publish sufficiently detailed training-content summaries. The obligations apply from **2 August 2025**, with summaries for models placed on the market before that date due by **2 August 2027**. The Commission describes enforcement for summary failures from **2 August 2026**; the transition means not every model has the same immediate deadline. [38]

**The summary is more than a slogan:** The Commission’s template covers public and private datasets, scraped sources, user data, and synthetic data. For scraping, it calls for crawler names, collection periods, content descriptions, and leading source domains. Updates are expected at six-month intervals or sooner after materially significant additional training. The Commission guidance describes potential penalties of up to **3% of annual worldwide turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher**. [38]

**Transparency does not decide infringement:** A summary can help a designer identify a plausible source or evaluate whether a reservation was respected. It is not necessarily a work-level inventory, a license, or proof that a particular output infringes. The duties also extend to relevant open-source GPAI providers; open release does not erase the copyright-policy and training-summary requirements. [38]

**Provider conduct and hosting remain separate:** A notice-and-takedown mechanism for user uploads does not automatically answer whether the provider’s own training or generation conduct is lawful. In the EU, the Digital Services Act and relevant copyright-platform provisions may apply when their service-specific conditions are met. Across jurisdictions, a contract assigning responsibility to users cannot simply determine a third-party rightsholder’s statutory claim. [35]

**Disclosure also has different audiences:** Training summaries inform rightsholders and regulators. Provenance credentials inform downstream users about an asset’s recorded history. AI Act output-disclosure duties have their own scope, including particular concerns around deepfakes; they should not be reduced to a claim that every fashion image must carry an identical public label. Copyright registration disclosures concern yet another question: which parts the applicant claims as human-authored expression. [22]

**Section judgment:** Use an interoperable operational standard for evidence and permissions, but apply jurisdiction-specific law to authorship, training, product protection, and liability.

## Practical and legal ways to resolve the disputes

**The solution should follow the harm:** Upstream permission disputes need source and license evidence. Reproduction disputes need output controls and remedies. Attribution needs preserved identity records. Economic exclusion may need collective bargaining and platform changes. No one mechanism performs all four functions.

### Consent, licensing, reservations, and remuneration

**Direct licenses should be specific:** A license for a designer’s archive should identify the works, authorized training or fine-tuning uses, users, duration, payment, confidentiality, reporting, and treatment of recognizable reproductions. The designer must also have authority over the material licensed: owning a garment does not necessarily include the photographer’s rights, a collaborator’s artwork, or a model’s image permissions.

| Input-side option | Responsible actors | Enforceability route | Independent-designer benefit | Access and cost trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct consent and licensing | Designer and developer | Written agreement | Control and negotiated payment | High transaction costs for small repertoires |
| Voluntary collective licensing | Designers and collecting body | Mandate plus blanket license | Shared bargaining and administration | Catalog gaps and governance costs |
| Extended collective licensing | Legislature and authorized body | Sector-specific statutory framework | Coverage for harder-to-reach creators | Representation and opt-out safeguards essential |
| Compulsory statutory licensing | Legislature and administrator | Legally defined permission and rates | Payment despite fragmented ownership | Reduces individual refusal rights |
| Commercialization levy | Legislature and collecting body | Statutory collection and allocation | Shares commercial value | Allocation and innovation effects contested |
| Machine-readable reservation | Rightsholders and providers | Law, contracts, compliance records | Signals refusal or negotiated access | Detection and persistence burdens |

US Copyright Office licensing analysis [3] Collective-licensing assessment [39] EU remuneration proposals [22]

**Voluntary collective licensing is the practical complement:** Designers can authorize a collective management organization to negotiate a repertoire license, administer payment, and enforce agreed terms. This reduces the need for thousands of individual negotiations and can make smaller archives worth licensing. But a voluntary scheme licenses only the rights actually represented; it should not promise developers clearance over an entire industry. [40]

**Participation must include the smallest creators:** Simple enrollment, transparent deductions, understandable payment statements, independent oversight, and representation for emerging designers are essential design choices. A system that distributes most money according to existing fame or sales could reinforce the concentration it is meant to address. The relevant governance objective is inclusion, not merely collecting a large total.

**The policy alternatives genuinely disagree:** France’s CSPLA task force favors preserving consent through individual licensing supplemented by voluntary collective management. Nicola Lucchi’s European Parliament-commissioned study supports statutory remuneration mechanisms. The US Copyright Office recommends allowing voluntary markets to develop and considering targeted extended collective licensing if specific market failures emerge. These are different approaches to control, scalability, and timing—not one settled institutional consensus. [41][22][3]

**Start with voluntary sector arrangements:** For fashion, direct licenses for identifiable archives and voluntary pooled licenses for smaller repertoires are the most immediately workable combination. Extended licensing or a levy becomes more defensible if excluded designers, persistent transaction failures, or uncompensated commercial substitution demonstrate a problem that voluntary arrangements do not solve. That escalation should preserve lawful research and avoid automatically converting every exclusive right into a payment-only claim.

**A levy is not a copying license:** A commercialization levy could collect money when AI services earn revenue rather than require all payments before experimentation. Its appeal is broad access and potentially wider participation. But collection does not solve how to allocate money among creators, distinguish existing licenses, or authorize recognizable output reproductions. Those questions require an explicit legal scope and transparent distribution rules. [39]

### Make reservations usable across platforms

**Opt-outs must follow the work:** A designer should not have to negotiate a separate technical language with every model provider. Reservations need to distinguish search indexing from AI training and work across reposts, renamed files, and different hosts. EUIPO identifies three complementary approaches.

| Reservation approach | Who can apply it | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Location-based | Website manager | Signals policy at the source | Reposts can escape the signal |
| Asset-based | File creator or controller | Travels with the digital asset | Metadata can be stripped |
| Work-based registry | Rightsholder and repository | Identifies a work across locations | Depends on identification and adoption |

EUIPO’s reservation guidance [42]

**Use combinations rather than one flag:** Website instructions, embedded metadata, and a work-level registry can reinforce one another. Providers should record the signal observed at collection and the action taken. That evidence makes compliance contestable: a creator can challenge a particular failure, and a provider can demonstrate when and how it respected a reservation.

**Do not promise automatic retroactive removal:** Stopping future collection, deleting a training copy, suppressing a particular output, and removing a learned influence from an already-trained model are different interventions. Agreements and compliance systems should specify which action is promised and what evidence demonstrates completion. Otherwise, “opt out” risks sounding more comprehensive than the remedy actually delivered.

### Attribution, provenance, audits, and output controls

**Traceability should serve both parties:** Designers need evidence of origin and use; developers need a defensible record of permission and processing. These records can reduce factual disputes without making every dataset publicly downloadable.

| Measure | Accountable actor | How it operates | Enforcement route | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attribution | Designers, licensors, platforms | Preserve creator and source identifiers | Contract and applicable attribution rules | Credit does not replace permission |
| Provenance | Tools and marketplaces | Sign origin and editing records | Contract and platform standards | Incomplete histories remain possible |
| Dataset audits | Providers and auditors | Examine sources, licenses, reservations | Regulatory or contractual access | Cost and confidentiality |
| Model controls | Developers | Deduplicate, test memorization, filter reproductions | Product commitments and legal duties | False positives and residual copying |
| Human review | Brands and marketplaces | Examine high-risk matches and final assets | Internal accountability and complaint review | Labor and inconsistent decisions |
| Notice and action | Platforms | Preserve, remove, review, hear counter-notices | Platform rules and applicable law | Repeated reporting burden |
| Mediation or arbitration | Parties and neutral institutions | Resolve evidence, payment, removal, licensing | Settlement or enforceable award | Consent, fees, and power imbalance |

C2PA specification [43] EU transparency template [38] Output-control analysis [39] WIPO dispute resolution [44]

**Provenance is evidence, not permission:** C2PA Content Credentials cryptographically bind an asset to recorded assertions about origin, edits, AI involvement, and potentially training permissions. A signed manifest can help establish who made an assertion and whether it has been altered. It does not make an inaccurate rights claim true, grant a copyright license, or act as digital-rights-management technology preventing reuse. [43]

**Credit should reflect actual contribution:** Preserve the independent designer’s identity when their original sketch, print, or licensed asset is incorporated. Keep a record of the model and meaningful human revisions. Do not infer a particular designer’s authorship from a style prompt, and do not label a generated imitation “by” that designer without a proper basis; misleading credit can create a new problem while appearing to solve attribution.

**Audits should test consequential claims:** Providers should retain source identifiers, acquisition dates, license scopes, reservation checks, dataset transformations, and applicable model versions. Public summaries can expose broad patterns; confidential independent audits can test selected claims without publishing sensitive archives or trade secrets. An audit should distinguish a general policy from compliance for a particular collection or disputed work.

**Evidence duties can rebalance litigation:** CSPLA proposes a rebuttable presumption of use where serious indications make inclusion plausible, allowing the provider to produce contrary evidence. Such a mechanism could help creators facing inaccessible records, but it is a proposed evidentiary reform—not permission to presume that every similar output infringes. The safeguard is a meaningful trigger and an opportunity to rebut it. [41]

**Output controls address a separate risk:** Deduplication can reduce repeated training examples; memorization tests can probe whether protected material reappears; filters can intercept high-confidence reproductions. The legal analysis nevertheless identifies residual memorization risk even with strong mitigation. A lawful training pipeline therefore still needs output safeguards, and an output filter does not retrospectively establish lawful acquisition. [39]

**Human review should be targeted:** Close matches, recognizable logos or watermarks, unusually specific references, and production-bound assets merit greater scrutiny than exploratory thumbnails. Reviewers should distinguish generic fashion features from protected expression and allow appeals against false matches. Blanket suppression of all stylistic resemblance would restrict ordinary influence and experimentation without accurately tracking infringement.

**Diversity requires additional creative choices:** A copyright-compliant model can still generate repetitive work. Brands should preserve independent research, use varied lawful references, question body and identity defaults, and evaluate whether finalists—not merely discarded generations—remain distinctive. Custom models should be refreshed with current, human-directed work rather than repeatedly treating yesterday’s generated selections as the complete definition of the brand. The practitioner warning about generic or outdated inputs illustrates why this matters. [18]

### Affordable enforcement and negotiated resolution

**Takedowns should preserve evidence first:** A designer’s complaint should identify the original work, ownership basis, accused listing or output, publication and discovery dates, and the relevant similarity. Platforms should preserve associated records, provide a reasoned decision, and address repeated infringement rather than simply deleting one listing while equivalent copies recur.

**Platforms already possess useful mechanisms:** Amazon describes automated screening, expert reviewers, and Brand Registry reporting; Temu describes investigation, removal, monitoring databases, and suspension or banning of repeat violators. These are practical intervention points, but their effectiveness depends on accessible reporting and consistent follow-through. The recommended improvement is to reduce repeated evidentiary work for the same established rights claim. [16]

**Counter-notices protect legitimate creators too:** Midjourney’s terms describe possible restoration within **10–14 business days** after a compliant counter-notification unless the complainant initiates court action and gives notice. That procedure is not a determination of ownership or a promise of compensation. It also shows why poorly founded claims against a general aesthetic can harm other independent designers. [11]

**Mediation can resolve more than liability:** Parties can agree on removal, compensation, credit, future licensing, source disclosure, revised controls, or monitoring. WIPO describes an AI-related technology dispute resolved through removal within **two weeks**, and a separate US–European software-and-hardware dispute settled in **just over one month**. These are concrete examples of possible speed, not expected resolution times for fashion claims. [44]

**Arbitration needs accessibility safeguards:** Midjourney’s customer terms provide a **30-day good-faith settlement period** followed by expedited arbitration, with specified venue and fee arrangements. Such clauses govern agreeing parties; they do not automatically bind an outside designer who never accepted them. A fair industry system should offer proportionate fees, remote participation, independent neutrals, and access to urgent court remedies rather than make expensive private proceedings the price of asserting rights. [11]

**Section judgment:** Resolve permission, payment, evidence, reproduction, and redress together, while keeping each obligation specific enough to enforce.

## The most workable package—and the direct answer

**Combine interventions rather than choosing one:** Law supplies enforceable minimums, contracts allocate permissions and risks, technical systems preserve evidence and reduce copying, platforms can act quickly, and collective governance lowers transaction costs. Each becomes more useful when the others correct its weaknesses.

| Intervention class | Rights and diversity contribution | Innovation and access effect | US–EU–UK feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal | Compels disclosure and supports remedies | Can clarify access; reform can be slow | Requires jurisdiction-specific implementation |
| Contractual | Defines consent, payment, confidentiality, indemnity | Flexible; bargaining power can be unequal | Immediately usable across borders |
| Technical | Supports reservations, provenance, reproduction controls | Preserves useful generation; adds compliance work | Interoperable standards can travel |
| Platform | Removes copies and addresses repeated abuse | Fast at scale; false positives need appeals | Depends on service role and local duties |
| Industry governance | Pools rights, administration, and dispute support | Broadens participation; risks gatekeeper capture | Voluntary schemes first; statutory extensions differ |

These are comparative policy judgments grounded in the documented mechanisms, not experimentally measured performance scores. [3][23][39]

### Five implementation priorities

**The ordering follows practical dependencies:** Designers need usable evidence before they can negotiate or enforce rights efficiently. Permission and payment then need to be paired with records of authorship, output safeguards, and accessible dispute resolution. The priorities are a recommended sequence, not a numerical ranking of proven economic returns.

1. **Establish interoperable transparency and reservations.** Providers should publish applicable training summaries, preserve source and permission records, and honor usable rights signals. Regulators should enforce existing duties, while contracts supply comparable commitments elsewhere.

2. **License identifiable archives and include smaller repertoires.** Use direct agreements where individual negotiation is practical and voluntary collective arrangements where it is not. Consider extended licensing or statutory remuneration only with a demonstrated problem, inclusive governance, and a suitable legal basis.

3. **Preserve provenance and human decisions.** Designers and tools should retain original sketches, reference permissions, selected outputs, revisions, and final expressive changes. Credentials should support this record rather than substitute for it.

4. **Control reproductions before and after publication.** Developers should test memorization and close-copy risks; brands should review consequential outputs; marketplaces should provide notice, counter-notice, repeat-infringer controls, and proportionate ranking safeguards against mass-duplicated listings.

5. **Make redress affordable from the outset.** Industry bodies, platforms, and public institutions should support evidence preservation, first-line assistance, mediation, and proportionate proceedings, with court access preserved where necessary.

**The package protects access as well:** Smaller developers need attainable licenses and clear compliance expectations, not arrangements available only to companies able to purchase exclusive access to major archives. Independent designers need a place in those repertoires, not a system where protecting their work requires withdrawing from public visibility. Transparent, nonexclusive licensing and shared administrative infrastructure can serve both interests.

**Copyright cannot carry the whole burden:** Preventing copied expression is different from sustaining creative diversity. A perfectly licensed system could still optimize every collection toward the same popular look. Conversely, a diverse set of outputs could contain individual infringements. Human creative direction, inclusive representation, diverse lawful references, and fair marketplace discovery therefore remain necessary alongside legal compliance.

**Confidence follows the evidence:** This assessment is bounded at **13 September 2026**, excluding later product terms and the meta-analysis published on 14 September. Study counts refer to participants, images, or tests—not industry prevalence; practitioner cost examples use different measurement bases, and qualitative intervention comparisons are policy judgments. Confidence is higher for documented workflows, legal boundaries, and dated procedural events than for causal homogenization, regional income effects, or displacement; complaints establish allegations, and settlements establish negotiated resolution rather than liability.

**Are AI tools homogenizing fashion?** They create credible mechanisms for convergence and faster imitation, especially when shared defaults feed engagement-driven selection and rapid production. The strongest supported answer is not that industry-wide homogenization has been demonstrated, but that it is a serious, governable risk alongside real gains in access and experimentation.

**How should disputes be resolved?** Hold the relevant developers, operators, brands, and marketplaces accountable for their own acts; distinguish lawful inspiration from protected-expression copying; and combine permission, fair payment, traceability, output controls, and affordable redress. The goal is neither ownership of every aesthetic nor unrestricted appropriation, but useful AI-assisted creativity in which independent designers retain agency, recognition, and a realistic ability to earn from their work.
