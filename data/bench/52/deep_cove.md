# The Investment Philosophies of Duan Yongping, Warren Buffett, and Charlie Munger

**A shared foundation, different emphases:** Duan Yongping, Warren Buffett, and Charlie Munger treat investing as owning businesses: understand how a company makes money, pay a sensible price relative to its future cash generation, hold while the economics remain attractive, and avoid mistakes that threaten survival. Duan emphasizes business models, corporate culture, and self-knowledge; Buffett emphasizes intrinsic value, durable competitive advantages, management, and capital allocation; Munger emphasizes multidisciplinary reasoning, incentives, inversion, and choosing a few exceptional opportunities. Their philosophies overlap substantially, but their distinctive contributions address different ways an investment can go wrong. [1][2][3]

| Investor and context | Distinctive emphasis | Concrete illustration | Practical verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duan Yongping: Chinese operating roots; cross-border equities | Business model, culture, understanding | Apple held about 14 years by January 2025 | Know the business well enough to remain an owner |
| Warren Buffett: Berkshire’s U.S. and international businesses | Intrinsic value, moats, capital allocation | Coca-Cola dividends: US$75 million in 1994; US$704 million in 2022 | Buy durable economics and allocate their cash intelligently |
| Charlie Munger: partnership, Berkshire, Daily Journal | Mental models, inversion, opportunity cost | Costco’s quality at roughly 12–13 times earnings, recalled in 2018 | Eliminate weak choices; concentrate selectively |

The examples come from Duan’s January 2025 university discussion, Buffett’s letter for 2022, and Munger’s 2018 shareholder-meeting remarks. [4][2][5]

**The central questions are practical:** What does each investor buy, how do quality and price interact, what counts as risk, and when should ownership end? The profiles below answer those questions before comparing all three on the same dimensions. The final sections explain their documented relationships and how their approaches developed over their careers, using evidence available through September 13, 2026.

## Duan Yongping: understandable businesses, strong culture, and patient ownership

**Duan’s central investment rule:** Buy an understandable business with an attractive business model and the right people, at an acceptable price, then remain an owner while the investment case holds. In the translated Stanford Q&A, he summarized this as “right business with right people at right price,” explaining that “business” meant the business model and “people” meant corporate culture. He explicitly placed business and people ahead of price in importance. [6]

**His perspective crosses market boundaries:** Duan’s experience began in Chinese consumer electronics, while his investments include Chinese businesses and U.S.-listed companies such as NetEase and Apple. This makes his philosophy better understood as an entrepreneur’s approach to ownership across markets than as a strategy confined to Chinese stocks or technology shares. His documented examples also include Moutai, Tencent, PDD, General Electric, and a costly speculative position in Baidu. [7][1]

The principal applications show both the strengths of his method and the experiences that sharpened its boundaries.

| Investment | Period or setting | Reported action or value | Principle illustrated |
|---|---|---|---|
| NetEase | 2001 purchase | About US$1 per share; roughly hundredfold gain reported | Industry knowledge and undervaluation |
| Apple | January 2011 to January 2025 | Initial allocation about 10%; ownership about 14 years | Understanding supports patience |
| Tencent | Recollection of a 2018 purchase | Small initial investment | Business model, culture, and fair price |
| PDD | Interview Q&A and January 2025 account | Venture-style commitment; stated ten-year intention | Different uncertainty warrants different sizing |
| General Electric | 2008 purchase; later reassessment | Bought, exited, later rejected the business model | Revise an earlier judgment |
| Baidu | May 10, 2010 self-report | US$150–200 million lost across short positions | Stop speculation and preserve capital |

These are attributed investment accounts, not a model portfolio: the NetEase account is reproduced by Futunn; Apple, Tencent, and PDD appear in interview reproductions and the Zhejiang discussion; the GE reassessment appears in a later interview; and the Baidu loss appears in Duan’s dated Chinese Q&A. [8][9][10][11][1]

### Business quality and corporate culture

**Business quality precedes apparent cheapness:** Duan’s first question is not whether a share has fallen, but whether the underlying company has a good way of making money. On April 6, 2012, he described a moat—a durable competitive advantage—as part of the business model and a good business model as protection for future cash flow. That connects qualitative business understanding directly to valuation: the model matters because it helps explain whether future profits can endure. [1]

**Culture affects the business’s durability:** In Duan’s vocabulary, corporate culture is more than a statement of values. It concerns the organization’s priorities, honesty, treatment of customers, and capacity to recognize and correct mistakes. His interview discussions connect good culture with earlier problem detection and correction, making culture a practical influence on business survival rather than an ornamental consideration. [10]

**Apple illustrates the operating connection:** In a January 22, 2011 post, reproduced in the Chinese compilation, Duan highlighted Apple’s user experience, emerging platform, concentrated product development, scale-related costs, distribution efficiency, and market opportunity. These were observations about how the business worked, not merely a prediction that smartphones would become popular. His consumer-electronics experience helped him interpret the relationship between product differentiation, customer loyalty, and profitability. [1]

**The analysis extended beyond hardware:** By January 2013, Duan described Apple as a business model he had long wanted to achieve in his own industry. He emphasized that the platform and organization could remain strong beyond Steve Jobs’s direct involvement. The investment judgment therefore depended on identifying an institution with durable economics, rather than assuming a brilliant founder would remain indispensable forever. [1]

**GE shows why culture alone fails:** Duan’s earlier enthusiasm for General Electric rested partly on years of following its corporate culture. In his later retrospective, he said he would no longer make the investment because he judged its business model unattractive and acknowledged that his earlier understanding had been weaker. The useful lesson is not that culture is irrelevant, but that admiration for a company’s stated values cannot substitute for understanding its economics. [1][11]

### Valuation, price, and opportunity cost

**Quality does not eliminate valuation:** On April 5, 2012, Duan defined buying shares as buying a company and buying the company as buying its discounted future net cash flow over its entire life—not merely the next three or five years. The next day, he added that some good businesses become too expensive, in which case the appropriate response is to wait. These statements rule out interpreting his business-first language as indifference to price. [1]

**Opportunity cost determines attractiveness:** Opportunity cost means what the investor gives up by choosing one use of capital instead of the best acceptable alternative. Duan’s reproduced valuation framework says that the necessary discount to intrinsic value need not always be 40% or 50%; the answer depends on the investor’s alternatives. His question is therefore not simply “Is this company good?” but “Is this a sufficiently attractive use of my money?” [8]

**His Apple arithmetic makes this concrete:** In a reproduced discussion using 2019 figures, Duan considered approximately US$1 trillion of Apple equity value, about US$200 billion of net cash, and roughly US$50 billion of annual profit. The calculated net-of-cash earnings yield was about **6.25%**: US$50 billion ÷ (US$1 trillion − US$200 billion). That is an earnings-based valuation shortcut, not a forecast of the shareholder’s total return. [9]

**The same asset can rank differently:** In that discussion, Apple’s appeal depended on the durability of its earnings over more than twenty years and whether the investor had better alternatives. Duan contrasted an investor without opportunities above roughly 6% annually with one who could reasonably find opportunities above 10%. The distinction explains why two rational investors can admire the same business yet make different allocation decisions. [9]

**Tencent shows the growth-price trade-off:** In the reproduced Tencent discussion, Duan did not describe 37 times earnings as cheap relative to Apple or Moutai. His willingness to regard the valuation as reasonable depended on a historical scenario involving approximately 15% growth over ten years and annual profit of RMB200–300 billion within ten to fifteen years. Those figures were conditions in his investment argument, not a current forecast or a universal acceptable earnings multiple. [10]

**Rough valuation still requires substance:** Duan frequently describes valuation as a rough estimate rather than a finely calibrated calculation. In his April 25, 2010 discussion, he explained that approximate valuation helps assess downside, while qualitative understanding often explains the largest gains. The reasoning is coherent: if an investment only looks attractive after fragile assumptions are refined to several decimal places, its apparent bargain may be too dependent on precision the investor does not possess. [1]

### Circle of competence and margin of safety

**Self-honesty defines the investment boundary:** Duan’s circle of competence consists of businesses whose economics and future cash flows he can meaningfully assess. In a September 30, 2018 entry, he explained that this is not an arbitrary circle drawn around familiar industries; it begins with being honest about what one knows and does not know. Understanding one business deeply can be more useful than possessing superficial familiarity with many. [1]

**NetEase illustrates specific knowledge:** In his April 5, 2012 account, Duan said many investors doubted the size of the gaming market, whereas his long experience in the industry made him confident that it was large. He did not claim to know its ultimate size. His advantage was therefore a better-grounded judgment about the business opportunity, not complete foresight. [1]

**Understanding supports both buying and holding:** Duan uses “margin of safety” more broadly than a price discount alone. He repeatedly connects it with understanding the business and the changes that could damage it. A low price is less protective when the investor has misunderstood the business; conversely, knowledge of durable economics can make an ordinary price fluctuation much less consequential. [1]

**Banks demonstrate investor-specific boundaries:** Duan has often contrasted Buffett’s familiarity with insurance and banking with his own stronger familiarity with games and consumer electronics. But this was not an immutable prohibition on every bank investment: in a May 29, 2011 Q&A, he explained a Wells Fargo investment by saying he had read and accepted Buffett’s reasoning, while also rejecting the idea of indiscriminately following another investor. The boundary concerns comprehension of a particular case, not a permanent industry blacklist. [1]

**Selective inactivity follows from that boundary:** On April 6, 2012, Duan said that over roughly ten investing years he had understood fewer than ten companies and invested heavily in five—about one substantial commitment every two years, excluding rejected and mistaken investments. His willingness to miss opportunities follows from the asymmetry between omission and damage: failing to buy a winner leaves existing capital intact; a major error can impair future choices. [1]

### Long-term holding, risk management, and temperament

**Long-term ownership is conditional:** In the translated Stanford Q&A, Duan said he generally did not intend to sell after buying unless profitability deteriorated or capital had a better use. At Zhejiang University on January 5, 2025, he said he had owned Apple since 2011—about fourteen years—and that without understanding its business, culture, and model he would have left much earlier. [12][4]

**The intention is not a promise:** In the November 11, 2025 interview reported by Sina Finance, Duan explicitly rejected equating value investing with never selling. Long-term holding describes the intention at purchase, while opportunity cost remains relevant afterward. This distinction separates patience from stubbornness: the investor should resist market noise without refusing to update a mistaken business judgment. [13]

**Leverage threatens control over time:** Duan’s personal-equity discipline rejects margin borrowing and short-selling. Borrowing against a position can allow a price decline to force liquidation before the business thesis has time to work. In a July 7, 2006 discussion reproduced in the compilation, he made precisely this connection: a long-term undertaking can become short-term speculation when financing makes price movements decisive. [1]

**His mistakes explain the firmness:** In the May 10, 2010 Baidu account, Duan attributed a US$150–200 million loss across accounts to short-selling that began small, became competitive, and ended in a short squeeze. He emphasized not only the direct loss but also the depletion of cash that could have funded better opportunities during the financial crisis. Risk management here protects both present wealth and future freedom of action. [1]

**Essential capital has a different purpose:** The January 2025 event report summarizes Duan’s preference for an illustrative 10%–25% annual return rather than a possible 500% gain accompanied by the possibility of losing everything. The governing principle is not a promised return range; it is refusing to risk money one needs in pursuit of money one does not need. He also distinguished ordinary investment from venture-style commitments whose uncertainty and purpose differ. [4]

**An ordinary mind means rational composure:** Duan’s expression *pingchang xin*, often translated as an “ordinary mind,” connects calmness with rationality and long-term thinking. In a May 30, 2010 entry, he explicitly compared it with Munger’s emphasis on rationality. His accounts also link composure to using surplus capital and understanding the holding, rather than trying to manufacture courage while carrying an unsuitable financial exposure. [1]

**Stopping errors complements patient holding:** His “Stop Doing List” is a way of thinking: once an action is recognized as wrong, stop it rather than allowing short-term temptation or pride to sustain it. The September 2018 explanation presents this as accumulated discipline, not a formula. A long holding period is valuable only when the investment remains sound; it does not redeem a clearly broken thesis. [12]

### What his investment examples actually show

**NetEase combines conviction with imperfect foresight:** The reproduced account describes a 2001 entry near US$1 per share and a gain of roughly a hundredfold. Separately, Duan’s November 2025 recollection describes a full-sized purchase and a twentyfold rise in six months. His explanation emphasizes gaming knowledge, the team, the business model, and cash exceeding market value; he also acknowledged that understanding the eventual earnings more fully would have changed how much he bought and how long he held. [8][13]

**Apple demonstrates a developing commitment:** One reproduced retrospective dates his first Apple purchase to January 19, 2011, at approximately US$310 ÷ 7, or US$44 per share on that source’s adjusted basis, with an initial allocation near 10%. Contemporary entries later in 2011 discuss a position around 25% and a ceiling near 30% while he assessed the implications of Jobs’s departure. A July 2012 entry reports approximately 60%–70%, varying by account. Concentration developed alongside his assessment of the business rather than emerging from a fixed position-size formula. [9][1]

**PDD is a useful counterexample:** In the reproduced Q&A, Duan described PDD as a small venture-style investment, contrasted its uncertainty with his clearer NetEase judgment, and stated a ten-year holding intention. In January 2025, he also described an early, pre-listing commitment invited by Huang Zheng, when future profitability was uncertain, with a willingness to direct gains toward philanthropy. This is a deliberately different kind of commitment, not evidence that every investment he made satisfied the same certainty threshold. [10][4]

**The practical lesson from Duan:** Business understanding, culture, price, position size, and financial resilience must fit together; patience is the result of that fit, not a substitute for it.

## Warren Buffett: intrinsic value, durable economics, and capital allocation

**Buffett’s mature investment philosophy:** Own understandable businesses with durable economic advantages and trustworthy managers when the price offers an attractive relationship to intrinsic value. His letter for 2022, dated February 25, 2023, describes Berkshire and Munger as “business-pickers,” not stock-pickers. Public shares and wholly owned subsidiaries are different legal forms of the same underlying activity: committing capital to future business performance. [2]

**Berkshire adds an institutional dimension:** Buffett is not only selecting securities. Berkshire buys operating businesses, chooses their chief executives, redirects their excess cash, manages insurance obligations, and decides among reinvestment, acquisitions, stocks, cash, and repurchases. His 2005 letter explicitly sought qualifying businesses “in this country or abroad,” making the philosophy U.S.-centered but internationally applicable. [2][14]

Three familiar investments illustrate different parts of the approach.

| Business | Dated anchor | Economic lesson | Ownership setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| See’s Candies | 1972 purchase; discussed in 2006 | Intangible franchise value can exceed tangible assets | Controlled business |
| GEICO | 1996 shareholder letter | Low costs and referrals reinforce competitive advantage | Controlled insurer |
| Coca-Cola | 1994–2022 example | Growing distributions reward durable ownership | Publicly traded minority stake |

Buffett explained the See’s lesson at the 2006 meeting, GEICO’s reinforcing cost advantage in the 1996 letter, and Coca-Cola’s long-term cash distributions in the letter for 2022. [15][16][2]

### Intrinsic value and owner earnings

**Intrinsic value supplies the economic yardstick:** In the Owner’s Manual first issued in 1996, Buffett defined intrinsic value as the discounted value of cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life. Discounting converts future cash into an equivalent present value, recognizing that money received later is worth less than money available now at an appropriate return. The investor then compares that estimate with the actual purchase price. [17]

**The estimate is inherently judgmental:** Buffett explicitly says intrinsic value changes when interest rates change or forecasts of future cash flows are revised. Two informed people—including Buffett and Munger—can reach somewhat different answers from the same facts. This is why his philosophy combines valuation with conservatism: the number is a reasoned estimate, not a fact revealed by a spreadsheet. [17]

**Owner earnings focuses on extractable cash:** A useful shorthand for the cash economics Buffett emphasizes is *owner earnings*: the cash economically left for owners after necessary reinvestment. The 2017 summary of Tren Griffin’s account uses that label, while Buffett’s own 1998 meeting explanation directly states the operating principle: a business spending the cash it generates receives credit only for “whatever net cash is left every year.” [18][19]

**Reinvestment separates earnings from spendable wealth:** A company may report substantial profits yet need much of its cash to replace equipment, maintain its competitive position, or sustain unit sales. Buffett therefore rejects treating gross cash generation as though all of it were distributable. For the owner, money required to keep the business functioning is an economic cost even when an accounting presentation makes it less conspicuous. [19]

**This explains his EBITDA criticism:** EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Buffett and Munger objected to using it as a substitute for owner economics because asset wear and replacement spending remain real. The Owner’s Manual distinguishes certain acquisition-accounting charges from depreciation, warning that depreciation is generally an economic cost rather than something investors can safely ignore. [17]

**Growth must earn its capital:** The consequence is that sales growth, profit growth, and value creation are not interchangeable. Growth creates value when the additional investment needed to produce it earns an attractive return. A business that can expand while requiring little additional capital may produce more owner wealth than a faster-growing business that continuously absorbs its own cash. Buffett’s 1986 letter makes capital-light operating economics central to Berkshire’s allocation challenge. [20]

**Price converts economics into investor return:** Buffett’s May 2007 farm illustration makes the logic accessible. If a farm produces US$70 per acre annually and the investor requires 7%, the implied valuation is US$1,000 per acre: US$70 ÷ 0.07. The same productive asset can be attractive or unattractive depending on what the buyer pays; business quality and purchase price must therefore be assessed together. [21]

### Economic moats, competence, and management

**A moat protects future earning power:** Buffett’s economic moat is a durable competitive advantage that makes a company’s profits harder for competitors to erode. It may arise from a cost advantage, customer loyalty, distribution, or another business-specific strength. The important point is the mechanism: an investor must explain why the advantage can persist, rather than merely label an admired company “high quality.” [21]

**GEICO provides a reinforcing mechanism:** In the 1996 letter, Buffett explained that GEICO’s low costs permitted low prices, which attracted and retained policyholders. Customers then referred others, reducing acquisition expenses and strengthening the cost advantage. At that time, more than one million annual referrals supplied over half its new business. The moat was therefore a reinforcing operating system, not simply a well-known brand. [16]

**A moat still requires monitoring:** Competitive advantage is not a promise of uninterrupted profitability. Berkshire’s 2022 annual report recorded a GEICO pre-tax underwriting loss of approximately US$1.88 billion. That does not by itself settle the long-term value of the franchise, but it demonstrates why an owner must keep examining current operating economics rather than rely indefinitely on an old description of the moat. [22]

**Competence means assessing the important future:** At the 1998 meeting, Buffett described understanding a business, its competition, what could go wrong, management, and likely earning power over five, ten, or fifteen years before choosing a price. He stressed that some futures are impossible to assess, others difficult, and a few relatively tractable. Berkshire looks for the tractable cases rather than demanding a forecast for everything. [19]

**Industry labels are secondary to understanding:** Buffett’s later Apple investment does not require abandoning this principle. At the 2018 meeting, he said he bought Apple because of conclusions about its ecosystem, consumer behavior, durability, threats, and capital allocation—not because he wanted a technology stock. Understanding the source of customer loyalty mattered more than being able to dismantle an iPhone. [5]

**Management affects the value of earnings:** Buffett wants managers who are able, honest, and owner-minded. This was already visible in his 1965 partnership discussion of Hochschild, Kohn: he said a cheaper price would not have justified buying the department store with run-of-the-mill management. In the 1986 letter, he praised managers who thought like owners and found their businesses absorbing. [23][20]

**Retained money makes management consequential:** An outside shareholder cannot independently redirect the company’s retained earnings. Buffett’s 2014 report illustrated the issue by contrasting the fate of a dollar entrusted to the leaders of Sears or Montgomery Ward with one entrusted to Sam Walton. Management quality belongs inside valuation because it influences what today’s earnings become tomorrow. [24]

### Capital allocation and long-term ownership

**Capital allocation is a principal job:** In 1986, Buffett identified allocating capital as the second of his and Munger’s two Berkshire responsibilities, alongside their work with managers. It mattered especially because Berkshire earned substantial money, retained its earnings, and owned many businesses requiring relatively little incremental capital. Cash released by those businesses needed a productive destination. [20]

At Berkshire, capital allocation is Buffett and Munger’s second job: they retain cash when each dollar can be reinvested for more than a dollar of value, prefer 100% ownership when qualifying criteria are met, and allocate capital directly in controlled businesses while holding public stocks passively.

**Berkshire separates operations from allocation:** At the 1998 meeting, Buffett described centralizing money while decentralizing nearly everything else. Operating managers retained responsibility for running their businesses in their own successful styles; headquarters could compare uses of excess capital across a much wider opportunity set. This is a distinctive organizational expression of his investment philosophy, not merely a preference for certain stocks. [19]

That arrangement pairs owner-minded managers with centralized allocation: operations remain decentralized, retained earnings are kept when reinvestment should create more value, and qualifying businesses are preferably bought outright rather than held only as passive interests.

**Retained earnings must justify retention:** Buffett’s underlying test is whether keeping a dollar can create more value than distributing it. The 2019 letter explains how retained earnings at public investees can expand operations, improve efficiency, or fund repurchases, creating economic value that does not immediately appear in Berkshire’s reported operating earnings. The essential issue is productive use, not retention for its own sake. [25]

**Buybacks face the same investment test:** At the 2018 meeting, Buffett said Apple should repurchase shares only when they sell below their worth, the company has the cash, and no acquisition offers a better alternative. Repurchases are purchases of a business interest on behalf of continuing owners. Paying too much destroys value just as overpaying for another company does. [5]

**Ownership can grow without another purchase:** In the same Apple discussion, Buffett used a mentally rounded 5% Berkshire stake to explain how company repurchases could increase its interest to 6% or 7% without Berkshire spending more. This was an illustration of the mechanism, not a universal buyback endorsement. The benefit depends on the repurchase price and the alternatives available to the company. [5]

**Long ownership lets operating gains accumulate:** Berkshire completed its seven-year accumulation of the 400 million Coca-Cola shares cited in the 2022 letter in August 1994, at a total cost of US$1.3 billion. Annual dividends received increased from US$75 million in 1994 to US$704 million in 2022. The example illustrates how a durable business can deliver growing cash distributions while its shareholder remains largely inactive. [2]

**Holding is not identical across vehicles:** Buffett’s 2005 statement “We buy to keep” principally concerns Berkshire’s business acquisitions. The updated Owner’s Manual separately clarifies that its reluctance to sell good controlled businesses—even at attractive prices—does not apply identically to marketable securities. This distinction matters when comparing Berkshire’s permanent business relationships with an individual investor’s stock-selling decisions. [14][26]

### Risk, margin of safety, and temperament

**Risk concerns future purchasing power:** In the 2017 letter, Buffett defined investing as forgoing consumption today to enable greater consumption later; risk is the possibility that this objective will not be achieved. Short-term price volatility matters differently depending on when the investor needs the money. A sensible long-term equity investment can still be unsuitable for a near-term obligation. [27]

**Margin of safety protects against error:** Buffett seeks a price below a conservative estimate of value, leaving room for imperfect forecasts. In the 2019 meeting’s repurchase discussion, he described intrinsic value as a range with an approximately 10% band and offered shares selling 25%–30% below value as an example that could justify substantial purchases when no better opportunity existed. These were context-specific judgments, not a mandatory discount for every investment. [28]

**Financial conservatism preserves decision-making freedom:** The Owner’s Manual says Berkshire will reject interesting opportunities rather than overleverage its balance sheet. Buffett and Munger would not trade a good night’s sleep for a few extra return points or risk what their partners needed to pursue what they did not need. This is both a financing policy and a behavioral safeguard: financial resilience makes rational patience feasible. [17]

**Inactivity is permitted, fallibility is admitted:** Buffett’s 2022 letter says most of his allocation decisions over fifty-eight Berkshire years were no better than “so-so,” with some bad decisions rescued by luck. He attributed satisfactory results to about a dozen truly good decisions—roughly one every five years—and the advantages of long ownership. His philosophy does not require constant brilliance; it requires a few important successes without enough severe errors to overwhelm them. [2]

**The practical lesson from Buffett:** Estimate the cash an owner can benefit from, understand what protects it, assess who will allocate it, and demand a purchase price and financial structure that leave room for being wrong.

## Charlie Munger: better judgment, fewer mistakes, and superior alternatives

**Munger’s distinctive contribution is judgment:** Munger combined a preference for high-quality businesses with a systematic effort to improve how decisions are made. Mental models, incentives, inversion, checklists, opportunity cost, and temperament were central to that effort. His mature approach was to reject most possibilities, identify rare superior businesses at attractive prices, and remain an owner long enough for their economics to matter. [29][3]

**His experience involved several vehicles:** Munger’s record includes an investment partnership, Berkshire, and corporate capital allocation at the Daily Journal. Although U.S.-centered, the opportunity set was international: Berkshire’s ISCAR business and the Daily Journal’s reported financial-crisis investment in South Korean steelmaker Posco sit alongside U.S. examples. His 2018 Berkshire remarks also encouraged greater attention to Chinese opportunities. [30][5]

| Example | Period or setting | Decisive feature | Philosophical application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Costco | Earlier valuation recalled in 2018 | Roughly 12–13 times earnings; strong competitive position | Combine price with business quality |
| ISCAR | 2006 Berkshire meeting | Exceptional people; leading business quality | Evaluate organizational capability |
| Daily Journal investments | Financial-crisis account | US$15.5 million across four named companies | Wait, then act decisively |

Munger discussed Costco and ISCAR at shareholder meetings; the Daily Journal allocation is described in a secondary career retrospective naming Bank of America, Wells Fargo, U.S. Bancorp, and Posco. [5][15][30]

### Business quality and valuation

**Quality improves the long-term ownership proposition:** Munger favored businesses with durable competitive strength, attractive economics, and exceptional people. In the 1994 USC lecture, he explained how specialization can make a business unusually capable within a niche, while scale, distribution, brands, and related advantages can protect larger enterprises. Coca-Cola’s global availability illustrated how a business advantage can become difficult to displace. [29]

**The best business need not be biggest:** At Berkshire’s 2006 meeting, Munger praised ISCAR for growing from modest beginnings into what he considered the best company in its field, even though it was not yet the largest. He emphasized the extraordinary average quality of its people. The criterion was a company’s capability and economic position, not size as an end in itself. [15]

**Quality never makes price irrelevant:** In his April 19, 2022 conversation with Todd Combs, Munger said a great company held for a long time could produce exceptionally attractive results, but high purchase prices could make that strategy fail. His preference for quality was therefore a way of finding more durable value, not permission to ignore the amount paid for it. [3]

**Costco demonstrates integrated rather than mechanical valuation:** At the 2018 meeting, Munger recalled finding Costco attractive at about twelve or thirteen times earnings because of its competitive strength, real estate, personnel system, and likelihood of improving. He also recalled a valuation around three times book value, showing that an apparently high asset multiple could coexist with an attractive business valuation. He was combining the evidence rather than applying one ratio in isolation. [5]

**Arithmetic remains essential to judgment:** In Whitney Tilson’s notes from the May 2007 meeting, Munger defined margin of safety as receiving more value than one pays and described the underlying arithmetic as elementary. In the same discussion, he rejected the idea of a universal mechanical method for valuing every business. His position was neither formula worship nor intuition without numbers: basic arithmetic must be informed by business-specific understanding. [21]

### Mental models, incentives, and checklists

**Mental models organize causal understanding:** A mental model is a useful explanation of how something works—for example, how incentives influence behavior, how scale changes costs, or how probabilities affect outcomes. Munger’s “latticework” means connecting such explanations across disciplines rather than accumulating isolated facts. In his 1994 lecture, he offered the rough rule of thumb that eighty or ninety important models would carry about 90% of the practical load, with a handful doing especially heavy work. [29]

Munger’s latticework therefore uses roughly 80–90 important models drawn from multiple disciplines, with a full toolkit applied checklist-style rather than a single disciplinary lens.

**One model can distort the evidence:** Munger’s concern was the tendency to interpret every problem through the tool one already knows. In his October 3, 2003 UCSB lecture, he called this “man-with-a-hammer syndrome” and prescribed a full toolkit used checklist-style. For investors, the implication is that a low earnings multiple, an attractive brand, or a promising technology should not become the sole explanation that crowds out contrary evidence. [31]

**Incentives explain behavior inside organizations:** In the speech generally dated approximately June 1995, Munger used Federal Express’s night-sort operation as an example. He described efforts to improve performance through exhortation, followed by a change from hourly pay to per-shift compensation that made finishing the work more attractive. The investment lesson is to examine the behavior a compensation system rewards, not just management’s stated intentions. [32]

**Qualitative evidence also deserves weight:** The 2003 lecture warns against overweighting what can be measured while neglecting important factors that resist precise quantification. Culture, customer attachment, and management integrity can be economically decisive even when they do not fit neatly into a valuation cell. This is a direct connection between Munger’s broader reasoning framework and the business-quality concerns shared with Buffett and Duan. [31]

**A checklist protects against omission:** The point is not to replace thought with a rigid ritual. It is to make sure an investor deliberately considers several explanations and failure modes instead of hoping the right one happens to come to mind. An impressive growth story can still be undermined by poor incentives, excessive financing needs, or a business model whose customer economics do not work. That application follows from Munger’s argument for using multiple tools deliberately. [31]

### Inversion, competence, and correcting mistakes

**Inversion begins with the failure case:** Instead of asking only how an investment succeeds, inversion asks what would destroy it and how that outcome could arise. Buffett’s 1996 letter records Munger’s dictum about identifying where he would die so he could avoid going there. In the investment setting, that means examining intolerable consequences before optimizing the attractive outcome. [16]

**Backward reasoning challenges an appealing thesis:** Applied to a company, inversion might mean asking whether refinancing could fail, whether customers could leave, or whether management could misuse the cash. Those questions do not automatically imply rejection. They identify the assumptions that deserve the most scrutiny and distinguish a survivable disappointment from a failure that permanently destroys the investment.

**The too-hard pile limits false precision:** Munger’s broad reading did not lead him to claim competence in every business. In the May 2007 meeting notes, he said Berkshire placed almost all businesses in the “too hard” pile and sifted through a few easier cases. In a 2009 Stanford Lawyer interview, he expressed the principle sharply: competence includes knowing its own edge. [21][33]

**Rationality includes examining personal attachment:** At the 2018 meeting, Munger acknowledged that Berkshire’s newspaper decline had been faster than expected and suggested that his and Buffett’s affection for newspapers may have contributed to the miscalculation. He offered that as a possible explanation, not a measured causal finding. The example matters because a sophisticated framework is useful only if its owner applies it to his own preferences. [5]

**A clear mistake calls for correction:** In the 2021 CNBC conversation, Munger said that when an investment is clearly a mistake, it should be fixed quickly because waiting does not improve it. He and Buffett connected this with their difficult Baltimore department-store investment and moving capital elsewhere. This is fully compatible with long ownership: patience toward a sound business and persistence in a recognized error are different behaviors. [34]

### Opportunity cost, concentration, and temperament

**Alternatives determine whether action is worthwhile:** Munger treated opportunity cost as a central decision rule. At the 1997 Berkshire meeting, he explained that an existing opportunity better than 98% of the alternatives could eliminate the need to examine those inferior alternatives further. Buffett connected the same reasoning with comparing a new candidate against buying more Coca-Cola or Gillette, including the relative certainty of the business judgment. [35]

**Concentration follows from unequal opportunities:** If investments differ substantially in quality, price, and confidence, equal allocations need not make economic sense. In a passage reproduced by Value Investing World in 2016, Munger described owning three securities—A, B, and C—and regarding opportunity cost as a reason not to add another. The principle is not that three is a universally correct number; it is that a new holding should improve the portfolio rather than merely lengthen the list. [36]

**Waiting and decisiveness belong together:** The Daily Journal retrospective describes Munger holding excess capital in Treasury securities for years, then deploying US$15.5 million during the financial crisis into the four named financial and industrial companies. The behavioral pattern is prolonged selectivity followed by meaningful action when an opportunity meets the test. Patience without willingness to act would not implement the philosophy. [30]

**Temperament must withstand price declines:** In a reproduced 2017 Daily Journal Q&A, Munger argued that long-term investors should organize their lives so they can withstand a 50% decline with composure. The point is financial and emotional preparation, not a recommendation to dismiss every falling investment. An investor still has to distinguish temporary quotation losses from deterioration in the business itself. [37]

**Tolerance for volatility is not tolerance for ruin:** In the 2009 Stanford Lawyer interview, Munger described Berkshire as cautious and low-leverage, explaining that he and Buffett would not have been comfortable taking more risk with other people’s net worth. Accepting fluctuating quotations while avoiding forced liquidation is a consistent position. It is quite different from maximizing leverage because an investment feels certain. [33]

**Sustained attention matters more than brilliance:** In the 2022 conversation with Combs, Munger said additional intelligence helps, but the right temperament applied over a long period can produce excellent results. He also emphasized that the most successful searchers may find only one or two truly great businesses rather than ten, twenty, or thirty. The demanding task is recognizing those opportunities without persuading oneself that every exciting idea qualifies. [3]

**The practical lesson from Munger:** Improve the quality of the decision before increasing the size of the commitment, and judge every prospective investment against both its failure modes and the alternatives already available.

## Comparing all three on the same investment dimensions

**The differences concern emphasis and application:** All three connect business understanding with valuation, risk control, and behavior. The table below compares their mature philosophies on identical dimensions; it does not imply that any one investor ignores the principles emphasized by another.

| Dimension | Duan Yongping | Warren Buffett | Charlie Munger |
|---|---|---|---|
| What to buy | Understandable model, strong culture, good people | Durable economics, moats, trustworthy managers | Rare superior businesses with durable advantages |
| Valuation versus quality | Quality first; acceptable price and alternatives still matter | Conservative intrinsic value and sensible price | Quality plus an attractive value-price gap |
| Risk and margin of safety | Understanding, no margin borrowing, protect essential capital | Avoid permanent impairment; conservative value and financing | Invert fatal risks; distinguish volatility from ruin |
| Knowledge boundaries | Honest, investor-specific understanding | Assessable long-term earning power | Multiple models; most cases too hard |
| Diversification and concentration | Few deeply understood holdings; variable sizing | Concentrate selectively; consider underlying business diversity | Concentrate when alternatives are clearly inferior |
| Management and allocation | Culture, integrity, business-model discipline | Owner-minded managers; centralized capital allocation | Incentives, superior people, opportunity cost |
| Time horizon | Long intention; revise for economics or alternatives | Buy-to-keep acquisitions; patient stock ownership | Long-pull ownership of exceptional businesses |
| Behavior and temperament | Ordinary mind; stop wrong actions | Discipline, liquidity, resistance to market noise | Rationality, inversion, patience, sustained attention |

These comparisons follow Duan’s dated Q&A, Buffett’s Owner’s Manual and shareholder letters, and Munger’s lectures and meeting remarks. [1][26][31]

### What to buy: quality and price are complementary

**Cheapness means more than a low multiple:** Duan’s Apple and Tencent discussions, Buffett’s intrinsic-value framework, and Munger’s Costco explanation all assess price in relation to future business economics. A low earnings multiple may indicate a bargain, but it may also reflect fragile earnings or large reinvestment requirements. A higher multiple can be defensible when durable advantages allow earnings to grow and remain economically valuable to owners.

**Their emphases target different mistakes:** Duan’s business-model and culture filter helps resist buying a troubled company merely because the stock has fallen. Buffett’s cash-and-capital analysis helps distinguish reported profitability from cash that can benefit owners. Munger’s broader toolkit helps prevent one appealing feature—growth, management charisma, or a cheap ratio—from dominating the entire decision. These are complementary defenses, not competing valuation systems.

**Quality reduces some uncertainty, not all:** Better economics may make cash flows easier to assess, but they do not remove the purchase-price problem. Munger’s 2022 warning about overpaying for great companies and Duan’s April 2012 instruction to wait when a good business is too expensive reach the same practical conclusion. The superior business is not automatically the superior investment at every price. [3][1]

**No single discount captures their practice:** A secondary 2017 account presents a minimum 25% intrinsic-value discount for Buffett and Munger. Their directly attributed discussions are more contextual: Buffett’s 2007 remarks allow paying nearer estimated value for a great business, while his 2019 repurchase discussion uses a valuation range and compares alternative uses of capital. The stronger interpretation is a conservative value-price relationship calibrated to the case, rather than mechanically applying one percentage everywhere. [18][21][28]

**The resulting quality-price judgment:** All three prefer improving the underlying business proposition over relying entirely on a low entry price, while retaining price as an essential condition of an attractive investment.

### Risk and knowledge boundaries work together

**Knowledge comes before the safety calculation:** A margin of safety is meaningful only if the investor has a defensible idea of the business’s value and vulnerabilities. Duan emphasizes this connection most explicitly, sometimes using safety margin to describe understanding itself. Buffett operationalizes it through long-term earning-power analysis, while Munger adds the discipline of placing difficult cases outside the decision set.

**Their refusal rules differ in expression:** Duan says not to touch what he does not understand and not to finance stock ownership through margin borrowing. Buffett looks for tractable business economics and refuses a price or financing structure that undermines the owner’s objective. Munger asks what could cause fatal damage, then rejects the difficult or insufficiently attractive cases. The shared mechanism is reducing exposure to uncertainty that the investor has no useful ability to price.

**Volatility depends on financial circumstances:** Munger’s willingness to endure a large drawdown is compatible with Buffett’s warning that equities can be unsuitable over short periods and Duan’s insistence on surplus capital. If an investor needs to sell during the decline, temporary quotation risk can become a permanent financial problem. The useful question is not which investor sounds toughest, but whether the ownership structure allows the thesis to survive adverse conditions. [27][1]

**Personal margin and institutional financing differ:** Berkshire’s low-leverage philosophy does not mean it uses no financial liabilities. Buffett’s Owner’s Manual discusses insurance float—premiums held before claims are paid—and deferred taxes, while emphasizing their actual liability status. Duan himself distinguished ordinary margin borrowing from long-dated institutional financing in a February 19, 2013 Q&A, describing fifteen-to-twenty-year borrowing with very different collateral and repayment characteristics. [26][1]

**The resulting risk judgment:** Their common aim is resilient ownership, but financing terms, cash needs, and the investor’s actual knowledge determine whether a particular position achieves it.

### Concentration is an outcome, not a starting rule

**All three permit concentrated portfolios:** Duan reported usually owning about three companies, with at most five or six in one reproduced account. Buffett’s partnership allowed exceptionally large commitments under demanding conditions. Munger explicitly argued that a clearly superior opportunity can displace most alternatives. None of those statements establishes an optimal number of holdings for every investor. [8][23][35]

| Investor | Vehicle and period | Concentration evidence | Decision illustrated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duan | Reported Q2 2025 13F portfolio | Apple 62.47%; ten stocks | One dominant business exposure |
| Buffett | Partnership rule, reporting on 1965 | Up to 40% in one security | Size follows unusually strong evidence |
| Munger | Partnership, end of 1974 | Two holdings: approximately 60.8%–84% across source descriptions | A few holdings can dominate results |

Duan’s figures come from the Q2 2025 portfolio report; Buffett’s are an explicit partnership rule; Munger’s source-based range reflects the review’s summary and the position arithmetic discussed below. [38][23][39]

**Duan’s later snapshot remains top-heavy:** The same Q2 2025 report assigns 14.24% to Berkshire and 7.86% to PDD. Together with Apple, the calculated top-three share is 84.57%: 62.47% + 14.24% + 7.86%. Ten reported stocks therefore do not imply approximately equal diversification, just as his earlier statement about usually owning three does not describe every later account or period. [38]

**Buffett made concentration explicitly conditional:** The partnership’s potential 40% commitment required a very high probability that the facts and reasoning were correct and a very low probability of drastic underlying change. He also warned that concentration increases year-to-year variability and can produce a very poor year. Confidence was supposed to come from the evidence, not from the mere willingness to hold a large position. [23]

**One security can contain many businesses:** The updated Owner’s Manual describes Buffett holding more than 98% of his net worth in Berkshire and Munger’s family holding a majority there. Their stated comfort rested partly on Berkshire’s ownership of many different businesses. A portfolio concentrated in a diversified holding company is economically different from the same percentage in one operating company, even though each appears as one ticker symbol. [26]

**Diversification remains a legitimate alternative:** In the excerpts reproduced by Value Investing World, Buffett distinguishes the confident professional’s concentration from broad diversification for people whose game is not individual-stock selection. Duan likewise allows diversification among businesses an investor understands but finds difficult to distinguish. Their concentration should therefore be read as a consequence of informed selection and risk capacity, not an instruction to imitate their largest weights. [36][10]

**The resulting portfolio judgment:** Concentration magnifies the benefit of a correct business judgment and the damage from an overlooked error; the appropriate degree depends on understanding, underlying exposures, and the capacity to remain an owner.

### Culture, management, and capital allocation overlap

**Duan’s emphasis is organizational character:** His culture-first language directs attention to whether the business consistently behaves in ways that sustain customer trust and correct mistakes. Buffett’s emphasis on owner-minded managers asks who will operate the assets and deploy retained profits. Munger’s incentive analysis asks what organizational arrangements encourage those people to act well or badly.

**The contrast is not mutually exclusive:** It would be misleading to say Duan cares about culture while Buffett does not. At the 2018 meeting, Buffett discussed Berkshire’s culture at length, describing owner-oriented shareholders, directors, and managers, while Munger argued that its values could endure beyond the current leadership. Duan’s distinction is the prominence of culture in his investment vocabulary, not sole ownership of the idea. [5]

**Incentives connect character with actual behavior:** Buffett’s 1997 letter explained that Berkshire subsidiary bonuses were tied to each unit’s business performance rather than Berkshire’s stock price, which individual managers could not meaningfully control. This is a concrete institutional application of Munger’s broader concern with incentives. It also shows why evaluating good people and evaluating the system around them should reinforce one another. [40]

**Their ownership powers also differ:** Duan’s public-equity framework usually concerns choosing whether to own a business interest. Berkshire, when it controls a company, can select the CEO and redirect capital; when it owns public shares passively, it has no corresponding operating authority. Buffett’s philosophy consequently includes an unusually developed system for what happens to business cash after it has been earned. [2]

**The resulting management judgment:** Culture, people, incentives, and allocation are different ways of examining whether future earnings will become durable value for owners.

### Long horizons require disciplined updating

**Their common horizon is business-led:** All three expect the investment’s eventual success to come primarily from what the business produces, rather than from predicting the next buyer’s willingness to pay more. Long holding gives durable economics, reinvestment, and distributions time to accumulate. It also reduces the need to repeatedly find and correctly execute replacement investments.

**Their selling commitments are not identical:** Duan explicitly conditions holding on business performance and opportunity cost. Munger combines long-pull ownership with quick correction of clear mistakes. Berkshire’s controlled-business policy goes further: the Owner’s Manual acknowledges a reluctance to sell good businesses even when doing so could improve financial results. That reflects relationships and institutional identity as well as a narrowly financial comparison. [13][34][26]

**Temperament is supported by structure:** Duan’s ordinary mind, Buffett’s good night’s sleep, and Munger’s composure during drawdowns describe a shared aspiration: avoid allowing fear, envy, pride, or excitement to determine allocation. But temperament is easier to maintain when the investor understands the holding, has no forced near-term sale, and has not used dangerous financing. Emotional discipline and portfolio design are therefore connected rather than separate virtues.

**The resulting holding judgment:** Patience is justified by continuing business evidence; it becomes stubbornness when it merely postpones recognition of an error.

### Chinese, U.S., and global contexts change application

**The economic logic crosses borders:** Duan applies ownership reasoning to Chinese and American businesses; Buffett’s acquisition policy includes businesses abroad; Munger’s examples include international industrial and financial exposures. Country, listing venue, and industry do not create separate definitions of value. They change what the investor needs to understand about the business and the ownership claim.

**Local familiarity can change the opportunity set:** Duan’s industry and Chinese-business experience helped him interpret opportunities that were not initially inside Buffett’s circle. Conversely, Buffett’s insurance and financial-business experience covered areas Duan often found less comfortable. Their different investments can therefore follow the same philosophy without requiring a philosophical disagreement. Duan made this distinction explicitly in his January 2025 comparison with Buffett. [4]

**Scale also changes what is practical:** At the 2018 meeting, Buffett explained that Berkshire’s need to deploy billions made foreign accumulation more difficult, including where ownership disclosure thresholds and publicity affected purchases. He also explained BNSF as a good, capital-intensive alternative when Berkshire could not place enough money in capital-light businesses at sensible prices. Munger agreed that satisfactory rail and utility returns could be attractive despite not matching an idealized asset-light business. [5]

**The resulting contextual judgment:** The philosophy travels more easily than the portfolio: an investor’s knowledge, capital size, financing, and ownership powers determine how the shared principles should be applied.

## Shared principles, documented influence, and genuine differences

### Buffett and Munger’s intellectual partnership

**Their influence on each other is explicit:** CNBC’s 2021 retrospective places Buffett and Munger’s first meeting in 1959 and includes Buffett’s statement that he immediately recognized someone from whom he would learn. Munger, in turn, described gradually accepting that Buffett had found a better way to make a living than his earlier professional arrangement. Their relationship was a documented exchange of ideas, not an inference from similar holdings. [34]

**Buffett credited Munger with improving application:** At the 2024 Berkshire meeting, Buffett said Benjamin Graham supplied the fundamental stock-as-business principle and Munger helped him put it to better use. He connected the evolution with ownership of businesses such as American Express, Coca-Cola, and Apple. The shift toward quality was therefore an extension of the value framework, not abandonment of valuation. [41]

**Agreement did not eliminate particular disagreements:** The 2018 meeting contains a specific example. Buffett said he considered American Express’s decision not to bid as low as a competitor to retain Costco’s business smart, and explicitly said Munger disagreed. They could share an ownership philosophy yet reach different judgments about the economics of one commercial relationship. [5]

### Duan’s learning and personal contact

**Duan publicly acknowledged learning from Buffett:** The contemporary report of his US$620,100 winning lunch-auction bid quotes him saying he had learned much from Buffett and his philosophy and wanted to thank him. The auction occurred in 2006. His later dated Q&A makes the intellectual connection more specific: on April 6, 2012, he identified starting with the business model as an important lesson from Buffett. [7][1]

**Munger’s procedural influence is also visible:** Duan’s March 29, 2010 discussion invoked Munger’s checklist principle, and his October 22, 2010 entry used inversion to explain avoiding destructive actions. A January 14, 2011 follow-up applied that reasoning to shorting and margin borrowing. These are concrete connections between Munger’s decision principles and Duan’s own stop-doing discipline. [1]

**The Apple story establishes contact:** At Zhejiang in January 2025, Duan recalled discussing Apple with Buffett in 2018 and a 2022 dinner where Buffett introduced him to Munger and Bill Gates. Duan’s account says Buffett referred to his comparison of Apple’s business model with Coca-Cola’s. Duan also treated the introduction as potentially humorous and did not claim certainty about its effect on Buffett’s decisions. [4]

**Chronology constrains the causal claim:** In Duan’s own account, Berkshire began buying Apple in 2016, before their 2018 discussion. The conversation therefore does not explain the initial entry, although it could in principle have affected later conviction or positioning. A stronger causal claim would require Buffett’s attributable confirmation linking the conversation to a purchase; likewise, a three-way philosophical dispute would require attributable opposing positions on the same question. The documented conclusion is shared learning, personal contact, and differing business knowledge.

### The most important differences

**Duan most prominently foregrounds culture:** His entrepreneur’s lens asks whether the business model and organizational character deserve long-term confidence. Buffett gives especially developed attention to the economic value of cash, the managers controlling it, and its allocation across an ownership system. Munger contributes an unusually explicit account of how to reason, how people misjudge, and how alternatives should govern action.

**Leverage creates a qualified historical tension:** Duan’s strong prohibition on margin-funded stock investing differs from biographical accounts of borrowing in Munger’s earlier partnership. But Munger’s later description of Berkshire as cautious and low-leverage aligns closely with the shared survival principle. Comparing early practice with mature statements therefore reveals development and different vehicles, rather than establishing a permanent Duan-versus-Munger divide. [42][33]

**The resulting relationship judgment:** These are closely related ownership disciplines with distinct emphases and occasional differences in application, not three separate camps whose investment philosophies must oppose one another.

## How their philosophies evolved

**The mature positions developed through experience:** None of the three should be understood as having followed one perfectly fixed formula throughout his career. Their records show continuity in ownership reasoning alongside changes in what they understood, which opportunities were available, and how their capital was organized.

| Investor | Earlier practice or experience | Important transition | Mature emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duan | NetEase in 2001; GE in 2008 | Apple from 2011; explicit definitions in 2012 | Model, culture, competence, conditional holding |
| Buffett | Berkshire purchases from 1962; control in 1965 | Insurance in 1967; See’s in 1972; 1992 strategy explanation | Durable economics and capital allocation |
| Munger | Partnership from 1962; 1973–1974 losses | Decision to leave partnership management in 1974; liquidation in 1975 | Quality, models, superior managers, resilient ownership |

The chronology follows Duan’s dated entries and retrospectives, Buffett’s own accounts, and the reproduced biographical account of Munger’s partnership. [1][34][39]

### Buffett: from cheap assets toward better businesses

**The textile experience exposed cheapness’s cost:** Buffett began buying Berkshire shares in 1962 and took control in 1965. In the retrospective reproduced by CNBC, he described spending twenty years struggling with the textile business and contrasted it with the productive insurance direction begun in 1967. Buying an apparently cheap asset did not ensure that continuing operations would use capital well. [34]

**See’s changed the perceived source of value:** At the 2006 meeting, Buffett contrasted his pre-1972 attraction to tangible assets and book-value bargains with what he and Munger learned from See’s Candies. Properly identified and maintained intangible assets could produce more attractive owner economics than large quantities of cheap physical assets. The lesson broadened what counted as value rather than making purchase price unimportant. [15]

**The 1992 letter states the transition:** Buffett wrote that after earlier acquisition failures he revised his strategy toward buying good businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices. The later emphasis on moats, owner earnings, management, and allocation makes that preference operational. His increasingly large capital base also made tiny liquidation bargains less useful, a constraint he discussed explicitly at the 2018 meeting. [43][5]

### Munger: concentration, partnership pressure, and adaptation

**The early record includes more aggressive financing:** The biographical account reproduced by GuruFocus describes Munger committing heavily, including borrowed funds, to a British Columbia Power arbitrage. Other early investments included asset-discount situations rather than only the exceptional long-term businesses associated with his later reputation. These examples place his mature conservatism in the context of a developing career. [42]

**The 1973–1974 losses exposed structural pressure:** The reproduced biography illustrates the period with US$1,000 shrinking to US$467 between January 1, 1973 and January 1, 1975, compared with US$688 for the Dow illustration. The calculated declines are 53.3% and 31.2%, respectively: one minus ending value divided by US$1,000. Munger’s reported distress centered on partners’ morale and their ability to remain invested, not simply his personal willingness to tolerate quotations. [39]

**The concentration range has explicit arithmetic:** The same GuruFocus review’s summary reports Blue Chip Stamps at 61% and New America Fund at 23%, totaling 84%. Its quoted position data instead imply an estimated 60.8% combined: [(505,060 × US$5.25) + (427,630 × US$3.75)] ÷ US$7 million. On that position-data basis, the shares are approximately 37.9% and 22.9%, respectively. Both descriptions support substantial concentration, while the 60.8%–84% range preserves the conflicting numerical bases rather than treating either as a precise universal measure. [39]

**The partnership decision preceded liquidation:** The detailed account says Munger resolved by the end of 1974 to stop managing outside money in that partnership format and distributed securities in liquidation at the end of 1975. That distinction matters: the experience was not simply a stock-picking lesson. It also concerned finding an ownership structure compatible with long-term judgment and the financial circumstances of the people supplying capital. [39]

**Later adaptation extended beyond cheap compounders:** At the 2016 meeting, Munger described Berkshire’s shift to “Plan B” when extraordinarily attractive, low-reinvestment purchases became harder to find. He pointed to the growing importance of selecting superior managers for businesses such as Precision Castparts. His 2017 Michigan conversation separately described moving away from cheap “cigar butts”—weak businesses offering a final bargain realization—and painful liquidations toward better businesses. [44][45]

### Duan: clearer filters and more explicit holding discipline

**His continuity is business ownership:** Duan’s record connects the industry-based NetEase opportunity, the GE experience, Apple ownership, and later Tencent discussions through an insistence on understanding what is being bought. The opportunity set changed, but he repeatedly resisted describing the change as a rotation from one stock-market “style” to another. In a September 27, 2011 entry, he framed the constant as investing in what he believed he understood. [1]

**Experience sharpened the exclusions:** His dated discussions from March 2010 onward emphasize the consequences of shorting, borrowing, overconfidence, and failing to correct an error. The April 2012 definitions make the lifetime-cash-flow and competence principles explicit. His later GE reassessment shows that he could retain the ownership framework while deciding an earlier application had been wrong. [1][11]

**The later holding language became clearer:** The long Apple experience reinforced the importance of remaining invested in a good business, while the November 2025 interview clarified that long holding is an intention, not an irrevocable commitment. His philosophy consequently contains both a high threshold for abandoning a sound company and an obligation to recognize changed economics or a genuinely better alternative. [13]

**Evidence and measurement affect interpretation:** Buffett’s official letters and dated Owner’s Manual editions provide the strongest direct documentary base; updated editions should not have every figure assigned to 1996. Duan’s evidence often consists of Chinese Q&A compiled later, translations, and retrospective self-reports, with prices and gains retained on each account’s stated basis. Munger’s lectures and meeting remarks directly support his principles, while early partnership figures and borrowing come mainly through biography and include differing numerical descriptions; one reproduced Stanford lecture carries a 1996 filename and a December 29, 1997 text label. Portfolio percentages refer to the named vehicle and period, not total personal wealth or trade rationale. The 6.25% Apple yield, portfolio sums, historical-loss percentages, and position-data concentration are calculations from stated inputs; historical valuation scenarios are not current return forecasts. [26][1][46]

**The most useful combined lesson:** Their philosophies are best learned as disciplines of judgment, not copied as portfolios: understand the business, assess the people and incentives, compare price with conservative value and alternatives, and preserve the ability to remain an owner. Duan makes that discipline especially concrete through business model and culture, Buffett through owner economics and allocation, and Munger through the reasoning needed to avoid fooling oneself.
