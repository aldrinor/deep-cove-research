# Parkinson's Disease: Warning Signs by Stage, When Families Should Act, and Living Well After Deep Brain Stimulation

The warning signs that matter most change with each stage of Parkinson's. In the early years they are subtle non-motor clues and one-sided motor symptoms. In the middle years the concern shifts to balance loss, falls, "wearing off" and medication side effects. In advanced disease, swallowing trouble, confusion, hallucinations and dementia take over. The signs that should send a family to emergency care are a fall with head injury, choking or pneumonia signs, sudden confusion, fever with severe stiffness after medication stops, suicidal statements, and a DBS device that suddenly stops working. After DBS surgery, the most useful things families can do are protect the device, keep medicines on schedule, keep up targeted exercise and speech therapy, and watch for mood, speech and weight changes.

## TL;DR

- **Stage-specific signs:** Before diagnosis and early on, look for acting out dreams (REM sleep behavior disorder), loss of smell, constipation and a one-sided tremor or slowness. Mid-stage brings balance loss, falls, freezing, wearing-off and dyskinesia. Advanced disease brings swallowing problems, dizziness or fainting on standing, hallucinations and dementia. Aspiration pneumonia is a leading cause of death.
- **Act now, not later:** Seek emergency care for a head injury after a fall, choking or signs of pneumonia, sudden confusion (often caused by an infection such as a UTI), high fever with rigidity after medicines were stopped or missed, suicidal talk, or a DBS system that stops. Call the neurologist promptly for new hallucinations, compulsive gambling, shopping or sexual behavior, worsening "off" periods, or trouble swallowing pills. In hospital, insist that Parkinson's medicines are given within 15 minutes of the home schedule.
- **After DBS:** Expect 3–6 months of programming and a 50.0% average reduction in levodopa-equivalent daily dose after STN-DBS, per a 2021 *npj Parkinson's Disease* meta-analysis (Lachenmayer et al.) (people still take some medication). DBS does not stop progression and often helps speech, balance and freezing less than tremor and dyskinesia. Protect the device: no diathermy, MRI only under the manufacturer's conditions, carry the ID card, and check and charge the battery daily or on the schedule you were given. Exercise (tai chi, physiotherapy, LSVT BIG), LSVT LOUD speech therapy, weight monitoring and mood screening all help.

## Part 1 — Warning Signs Across the Stages of Parkinson's

### How stages are described

**Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) scale.** This is the most widely used staging system. It was published in 1967 and later modified to add half-stages.\[1\]

| Stage | What it means | Typical signs families notice |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No signs of disease | — (prodromal symptoms may already exist) |
| 1 | Symptoms on one side only | Tremor, stiffness or slowness in one arm or leg. Reduced arm swing on one side. One side of the face less expressive. Often so mild that a diagnosis isn't made yet |
| 1.5 | One side plus trunk/midline | Slight stoop, reduced facial expression |
| 2 | Both sides affected, balance normal | Masked face on both sides, decreased blinking, softer voice, smaller handwriting, slower dressing |
| 2.5 | Mild both-sided disease, recovers on the "pull test" | Early balance hesitancy |
| 3 | Balance impaired, still physically independent | Falls begin, slower turns, shuffling. Daily tasks take much longer |
| 4 | Severe disability, can still stand and walk without help | Needs help with many daily activities. Walker often needed. Freezing and falls are frequent |
| 5 | Needs a wheelchair or is bedridden unless helped | Full-time care. High risk of swallowing problems, pneumonia, pressure sores, dementia and hallucinations |

**Limitations of H&Y.** The scale measures only motor disability and largely ignores non-motor symptoms. Those non-motor symptoms (mood, cognition, autonomic problems) often drive quality of life and the need for care. Progression also varies widely between people.\[2\] Think of the stage as rough orientation, not a prediction.

**Clinical "early / mid / advanced" framing.** Clinicians often talk about early disease (roughly H&Y 1–2, good response to medicine), mid-stage (H&Y 2.5–3, with motor fluctuations and early balance loss) and advanced disease (H&Y 4–5, with symptoms that don't respond to levodopa). In advanced disease the main problems become swallowing and speech difficulty, gait and balance failure, dementia and psychosis. Medication helps these much less.

**The prodromal (pre-motor) phase.** Parkinson's starts years before the tremor. The strongest single early clue is **isolated REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD)**: shouting, punching or kicking while dreaming. In a 1,280-patient international study, people with isolated RBD converted to Parkinson's or a related disorder (dementia with Lewy bodies, multiple system atrophy) at about **6.3% per year, with 73.5% converting by 12 years**.\[3\] Other prodromal clues include loss of smell, chronic constipation, depression or anxiety, and lightheadedness on standing. Orthostatic hypotension (a drop in blood pressure on standing) is formally recognized as a marker of prodromal PD in the Movement Disorder Society research criteria.\[4\]

### Early indicators vs. markers of progression

| Symptom | Typically early (prodromal–stage 2) | Typically signals progression (stage 3–5) | Why it matters to family |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tremor** (resting, often one hand) | ✔ Classic early sign | Usually stays manageable | Most visible, but often *not* the most disabling |
| **Bradykinesia** (slowness, smaller movements, small handwriting) | ✔ | Worsens; affects dressing, eating | Underlies many falls and daily-care needs |
| **Rigidity** (stiffness, aching shoulder) | ✔ Often mistaken for arthritis | — | May show up as pain |
| **Postural instability / falls** | Rare early (early falls suggest an atypical parkinsonism) | ✔ Defines stage 3 | The main driver of injury and loss of independence |
| **Freezing of gait** (feet "glued" at doorways, turns) | — | ✔ Mid-to-late | Major fall trigger; often responds poorly to medication |
| **Motor fluctuations ("wearing off")** | — | ✔ Mid-stage, after years of levodopa | Symptoms return before the next dose is due |
| **Dyskinesia** (writhing, involuntary movements) | — | ✔ Mid-stage; risk just under 40% after 4–6 years of levodopa (Ahlskog & Muenter, *Movement Disorders* 2001) | A sign of peak-dose medication effect; a common reason for DBS |
| **RBD (dream enactment)** | ✔ Often years before diagnosis | Persists | Injury risk to patient and bed partner |
| **Loss of smell** | ✔ Prodromal | — | Also a safety risk (gas, spoiled food) |
| **Constipation** | ✔ Prodromal | Worsens | Can block absorption of medication and cause abdominal emergencies |
| **Depression / anxiety / apathy** | ✔ Can precede motor signs | Often worsens | Treatable; often missed |
| **Orthostatic hypotension** | Can be prodromal | ✔ Common; pooled prevalence 30.1% across 25 studies (Velseboer et al. meta-analysis) | Dizziness, fainting, falls |
| **Urinary urgency/frequency, nighttime urination** | Possible | ✔ | Night-time falls; UTIs can trigger delirium |
| **Speech changes** (soft, monotone voice) | ✔ Subtle early | ✔ Worsens | Social withdrawal; LSVT LOUD can help |
| **Drooling** | — | ✔ | Often signals reduced swallowing frequency |
| **Dysphagia** (swallowing difficulty) | Subtle | ✔ Hallmark of advanced disease | Aspiration pneumonia risk |
| **Cognitive change / dementia** | Mild slowing or executive problems possible | ✔ Common late | Major predictor of care needs |
| **Hallucinations / psychosis** | Rare early (early prominent hallucinations suggest Lewy body dementia) | ✔ 20–40% over the disease course | Often medication-related; needs prompt review |
| **Pain, fatigue** | ✔ Common at any stage | ✔ | Often undertreated |
| **Weight loss** | — | ✔ Advanced | Suggests swallowing problems, high energy use from dyskinesia, or poor intake |

### What the long-term data show

- **Non-motor and levodopa-resistant problems take over over time.** In the Sydney Multicentre Study, which followed newly diagnosed patients for 20 years, the main problems by the later years were the *non-levodopa-responsive* features: falls, choking, autonomic disturbance, neuropsychiatric symptoms and dementia. Dementia was present in **83% of 20-year survivors** (48% at 15 years), and **27% of participants died from pneumonia**.\[5\]\[6\]
- **Dementia is common but not inevitable.** A later multicentre study in *npj Parkinson's Disease* found that only **19% of 36 people with disease lasting 20 years or more** met dementia criteria. That group was less disabled on average (mean H&Y 3.19 vs. 4.2 in Sydney).\[7\] The Sydney figure is a sobering upper estimate, not a certainty for any individual.
- **Psychosis.** Hallucinations and delusions affect **20–40%** of people with Parkinson's. When "minor" phenomena are counted (a sense of a presence, things passing at the edge of vision, illusions), estimates rise to 25–70%. In one study tracking people with minor hallucinations, 10% resolved, 52% stayed the same and 38% worsened over a few years.\[8\]
- **Swallowing and pneumonia.** A 2024 meta-analysis in the *European Journal of Neurology* (Chua et al.) found that people with Parkinson's have **more than three times the risk of aspiration pneumonia**, with **10.0% hospital mortality** when it occurs. A postmortem study cited by Chua et al. found aspiration pneumonia was the primary cause of death in 30% of patients, and a newer late-stage cohort found it was the most common main cause of death (34%).

## Part 2 — Red Flags for Family Members: When to Act

The guiding rule is that **sudden change is not "just Parkinson's."** Parkinson's usually progresses over months to years. A noticeable worsening over hours to days almost always has another cause: infection (especially urinary or chest), a missed or changed medication, a new drug, dehydration, constipation, or a device problem after DBS. These causes are often reversible, which is why they deserve prompt attention.

### Tier 1 — EMERGENCY: call emergency services or go to the ER

| Sign | Why it's an emergency |
|---|---|
| **Fall with head strike**, especially on blood thinners, or any fall followed by confusion, vomiting, severe headache or new weakness | Possible brain bleed or fracture |
| **Choking episode, or sudden cough, fever and breathing difficulty after eating** | Possible aspiration pneumonia, a leading cause of death in PD\[9\]\[10\] |
| **High fever with severe rigidity, confusion, sweating, fast heart rate or unstable blood pressure**, especially after medicines were stopped, missed for a day or more, or cut back (for example around surgery or illness) | **Parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome (PHS)**, a rare, potentially fatal reaction resembling neuroleptic malignant syndrome. The most common trigger is abrupt withdrawal of levodopa. Treatment centers on restarting effective dopaminergic therapy quickly\[11\]\[12\] |
| **DBS system suddenly stops (or the battery is depleted) and the person becomes severely rigid, immobile, feverish or confused** | **DBS withdrawal syndrome** can mimic PHS. Boston Scientific's patient manual warns that "suddenly stopping brain stimulation can cause serious reactions to develop"\[13\]\[14\] |
| **Suicidal statements, a plan, or a suicide attempt** | Risk is elevated in PD and in the years after STN-DBS (see Part 3) |
| **Sudden severe confusion, agitation or drowsiness (delirium)**, especially with fever, burning urination or new incontinence | Often caused by a urinary or chest infection, dehydration or a new drug, and needs urgent work-up |
| **Fainting with injury, chest pain, or failure to come round quickly** | Orthostatic hypotension is common in PD, but fainting can also signal heart problems |
| **Unable to swallow any medication or fluids at all** | Missed doses risk severe "off" states and PHS; dehydration follows quickly |
| **Signs of stroke** (face droop, arm weakness, speech loss), particularly in the weeks after DBS surgery | Bleeding is a recognized surgical complication |

### Tier 2 — CALL THE CARE TEAM PROMPTLY (within days)

- **New hallucinations, paranoia or delusions**, such as believing a spouse is unfaithful or that strangers live in the house. These are often medication-related. Don't stop drugs on your own, but report them quickly. Seek urgent help if the hallucinations are frightening, the person loses insight, or the person acts on them.\[15\]
- **Impulse control disorders.** Watch for compulsive gambling, shopping, hypersexuality, binge eating, or compulsive hobbies and "punding" (repetitive aimless tasks). In the 3,090-patient DOMINION study, **13.6% of people with PD had an impulse control disorder**. The rate was **17.1% in those taking dopamine agonists vs. 6.9% in those not taking them** (pramipexole 17.7%, ropinirole 15.5%).\[16\] Patients often hide these behaviors, so family observation is crucial. Check bank statements and online activity if you suspect a problem. Also watch for **dopamine dysregulation**: taking extra doses, or demanding more medication despite dyskinesia. Doses should be reduced *under supervision*, because abrupt agonist withdrawal can cause a withdrawal syndrome.\[17\]
- **Worsening wearing-off or longer "off" periods**, unpredictable on/off switching, or **troublesome dyskinesia**. Medication timing or formulation may need adjusting. These are also the classic reasons to discuss advanced therapies, including DBS.
- **Freezing episodes becoming more frequent**, or **a first fall**, or **near-falls**. Ask for a physiotherapy referral.
- **Trouble swallowing pills, coughing or throat-clearing during meals, food sticking, wet or gurgly voice after drinking, drooling, or meals taking much longer.** Ask for a speech-language pathology swallow assessment. Silent aspiration is common.\[18\]
- **Dizziness or lightheadedness when standing**, especially after meals or in the morning. Ask about lying-to-standing blood pressure checks. A meta-analysis of 25 studies (Velseboer et al.) put the pooled prevalence of orthostatic hypotension in PD at 30.1%, and it raises fall risk.
- **Persistent low mood, tearfulness, withdrawal, loss of interest (apathy), or anxiety.** These are treatable.
- **Unintended weight loss** (for example, more than 5% over a few months), poor appetite, or severe constipation (no bowel movement for several days, or abdominal swelling).
- **New memory, attention or planning problems** affecting finances, medicines or driving.
- **Any new medication from another doctor**, especially antipsychotics, anti-nausea drugs or sedatives. Many common ones (for example haloperidol, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine and droperidol) block dopamine and can sharply worsen Parkinson's.\[19\]

### Tier 3 — RAISE AT THE NEXT ROUTINE VISIT

- Gradual changes in tremor, slowness, handwriting or voice volume.
- Acting out dreams, daytime sleepiness, or insomnia.
- Mild constipation, urinary urgency, or nighttime urination.
- Fatigue, aches, or stiffness-related pain.
- Mild forgetfulness without daily-life impact.
- Questions about exercise, driving, work or advance care planning.

### The hospital: a predictable danger zone

Hospital stays are risky for people with Parkinson's. The Parkinson's Foundation reports that more than 300,000 people with Parkinson's are hospitalized each year and that **three out of four people with Parkinson's in hospital don't get their medication on time**, and that about **12% received at least one dose of a contraindicated drug** during a stay. Its standard is that **all Parkinson's medications be given within 15 minutes of the at-home schedule, 100% of the time**. Its Aware in Care / Hospital Safety kit raised the share of patients confident they got medications on time from about 25% to 50%. That is better, but still far from enough.

**Family checklist for any hospital visit:**
- Bring an up-to-date medication list with **exact clock times**, plus the medications themselves in original containers.
- Carry Parkinson's ID and the DBS device ID card and patient controller.
- Ask staff to enter a custom medication schedule rather than default "four times daily" timing.
- Ask that any "nil by mouth" order include a plan for Parkinson's medication (for example a dispersible form, a patch, or a feeding tube).
- Flag contraindicated drugs, ask for a swallow screen and early mobilization, and watch for delirium.
- Use the Parkinson's Foundation's free Hospital Safety Guide and "go bag" checklist.\[20\]

### How to track and document symptoms

- **On/off diary.** For 3–7 days before appointments, record each half-hour or hour as "off," "on," "on with non-troublesome dyskinesia" or "on with troublesome dyskinesia." Note medication times and meals. This is the format neurologists use to fine-tune dosing and to judge DBS candidacy.
- **Event log.** Record falls and near-falls (time, place, activity, and on/off state), choking or coughing episodes, hallucinations, dizziness, and bowel and bladder issues.
- **Weekly checks.** Weigh once a week. If dizziness occurs, check blood pressure lying and then standing (a home monitor works).
- **Phone videos.** Short videos of freezing, dyskinesia or night-time behaviors are extremely useful because symptoms often aren't visible in clinic.
- **Wearables and apps.** Wrist-worn sensors and smartphone apps can objectively track tremor, dyskinesia and step patterns. Some are approved for clinical use. They supplement but do not replace a diary, and the evidence that they improve outcomes is still emerging.

### When to bring in allied professionals

| Professional | Bring in when… |
|---|---|
| **Physiotherapist** (ideally Parkinson's-trained) | At diagnosis for a baseline exercise plan, and again at first falls, freezing or balance loss |
| **Occupational therapist** | Daily tasks get harder; for home safety assessment, bathroom equipment, dressing and eating aids |
| **Speech-language pathologist** | Voice getting softer (LSVT LOUD), any swallowing concern, drooling, or communication difficulty |
| **Dietitian** | Weight loss or gain, constipation, protein–levodopa timing issues, texture-modified diets |
| **Mental-health professional** | Depression, anxiety, apathy, impulse control disorders, adjustment to diagnosis, caregiver distress |
| **Social worker** | Benefits, respite, home care, legal and financial planning, long-term care options |
| **Palliative care** | Not only at end of life. Useful for symptom burden, advance care planning and family support from mid-stage onward, and especially once dementia, recurrent pneumonia or weight loss appear |

## Part 3 — Daily Life After Deep Brain Stimulation

### What DBS can and cannot do

DBS is an established therapy for levodopa-responsive Parkinson's with motor fluctuations or dyskinesia. A 2021 meta-analysis in *npj Parkinson's Disease* found that subthalamic (STN) DBS improved:
- **dyskinesia by 64%**
- **daily "off" time by 69.1%**
- **quality of life (PDQ-39) by 22.2%**

It also **reduced the levodopa-equivalent daily dose by 50.0%**.\[21\] Most people *reduce but do not stop* medication.\[22\]

DBS has firm limits:
- It **does not slow disease progression**.
- It **does not improve symptoms that don't respond to levodopa**. Before surgery, the best predictor of benefit is how well symptoms respond to levodopa.
- Speech, balance, freezing that persists even when medication is working, cognition and swallowing typically improve least and may worsen over time.

Families who expect DBS to "fix" these axial and cognitive problems are often disappointed. Expectation-setting is one of the most important supports you can give.

### Phase 1: The first weeks after surgery

**What recovery usually looks like.** Most people stay in hospital **1–3 days** after lead placement. Before discharge they must show they can sit, stand, walk and use the bathroom.\[23\]\[24\] The battery (pulse generator) is often implanted in a separate, short procedure, typically about a week later in some centers.\[25\] Post-operative pain is usually mild. Swelling around the eyes and tenderness at the incisions can last a week or two.\[26\] Most people return to most activities within about a month, apart from strenuous exercise.\[23\]

**Activity restrictions.** These vary by center, so always follow your own written instructions. Common advice includes:
- Avoid strenuous activity for about 2 weeks.\[23\]
- Don't drive or fly for about 2 weeks after the last surgery.\[23\]
- Avoid lifting heavy objects for 4–6 weeks. Some centers set the limit at more than 10 lb for the first month; others at more than 20 lb.\[25\]\[26\]
- Arrange for someone to **stay with the patient for the first few days** to watch for confusion or complications.\[23\]

**Keep taking Parkinson's medication.** The stimulator is usually *not* switched on until several weeks after surgery.\[25\] Some people feel much better right after surgery anyway. This **"honeymoon" or microlesion effect** comes from the small disruption caused by inserting the lead, and it fades. Don't change medications during this period unless the team says to.\[23\]

**Watch the wounds.** Call the surgical team the same day for:
- redness spreading from an incision
- swelling, warmth or drainage
- an incision that opens, or hardware visible or pressing through the skin
- fever
- worsening headache, new confusion, weakness, seizures, or speech or vision changes

How common are complications? In one study of 1,757 DBS patients, **7.5% had a complication within 90 days**: wound infection 3.6%, pneumonia 2.3%, bleeding 1.4% and pulmonary embolism 0.6%. Reported surgical mortality is **below 0.5%**. Older age, pre-existing cognitive problems, obesity and cardiovascular disease raise the risk.\[23\]

### Phase 2: Programming and optimization (months 1–6+)

- **Why benefits take time.** Programming usually starts **2–4 weeks after surgery**, once the microlesion effect fades. Optimization typically takes **3 to 6 months**. It is rare to reach good settings in fewer than three sessions, and **six or more** are common. Early visits are often scheduled after skipping morning medication so the team can see the effect of stimulation alone.\[23\] Plan for transport and a companion, because the patient may arrive "off."
- **Stimulation and medication are adjusted together.** As stimulation rises, the neurologist lowers medication step by step. Expect temporary swings: dyskinesia right after programming (often a sign of good lead placement), slurred speech, tingling, or facial pulling. These usually resolve with setting changes.\[23\]
- **Keep a notebook.** Record symptoms, side effects and questions between visits. If the patient may adjust stimulation within preset limits, get written instructions.\[23\]
- **Remote and adaptive programming.** Remote programming is now approved for at least one system. In 2025 the US FDA approved **adaptive DBS** (stimulation that adjusts to brain signals) for Medtronic's Percept system.\[27\]\[28\] These are promising, but long-term comparative evidence is still accumulating.

### The device: batteries, controllers and daily checks

**Battery types.**

| | Non-rechargeable | Rechargeable |
|---|---|---|
| Lifespan | About 3–5 years (depends on settings) | About 15 years or more (manufacturers market up to 15–25 years)\[29\] |
| Daily work | None, but needs planned replacement surgery | Regular charging needed |
| Main risk | Unplanned depletion if replacement isn't scheduled in time | Missed charging leads to therapy stopping |
| Best for | People who can't manage charging (for example with cognitive impairment or no reliable helper) | People willing to charge and wanting fewer replacement surgeries |

**Recharging routines (manufacturer guidance).**
- **Medtronic Percept RC:** check the battery **daily**. Charge either about **15 minutes every day** or about **4 hours every 10–14 days**.\[30\]
- **Boston Scientific Vercise:** **15–30 minutes daily** or **3–4 hours every 1–2 weeks**. The manual warns: **"You should not charge while sleeping. This may result in a burn."**\[14\]
- **Abbott Liberta RC:** markets up to 37 days per full charge.\[28\]
- If the battery is over-discharged, therapy stops. Medtronic warns that over-discharge can permanently reduce battery capacity or, in some cases, require surgical replacement.\[31\]

Caregivers of patients with memory problems should take over charging and pair it with a fixed daily routine (for example after breakfast).

**Checking stimulation.** Learn to use the patient programmer or controller to check whether therapy is on and to check battery level. With non-rechargeable systems, an "approaching end of service" message means **call the clinic to schedule replacement now**.\[32\] Don't wait for depletion.\[33\] Medtronic's patient guidance says to call your doctor if:
- symptoms aren't relieved even though the stimulator is on
- you can't turn it on or off
- symptoms change unexpectedly\[34\]

**If stimulation stops suddenly:**
1. Check the controller: is therapy off, or is the battery depleted?
2. If the device is rechargeable, charge it fully.
3. If it still doesn't work, **contact the DBS team immediately**. Boston Scientific's manual says to turn stimulation off and contact the physician immediately "so that the system can be evaluated."\[14\]
4. Make sure all Parkinson's medication is being taken on schedule. The team may temporarily increase it.
5. **Go to emergency care** for severe rigidity, inability to move or swallow, fever, or confusion.\[35\]

DBS withdrawal is rare but can kill. In a 2026 multicentre analysis of 210 people with DBS, 28 (13.3%) had battery shutdowns. Most experienced only temporary motor worsening, but **3 (1.4%) developed severe withdrawal needing intensive care**.\[36\] A 2025 review of 38 PHS-like cases after DBS found **battery depletion caused 68.4%** and **76.3% recovered after timely device replacement**.\[37\] Restoring stimulation quickly is the cornerstone of treatment. Proactive battery management prevents most cases.\[36\]\[38\]

### Safety precautions: MRI, scanners, procedures

- **Diathermy is prohibited.** This includes shortwave, microwave and therapeutic-ultrasound "deep heat" treatment, given **anywhere on the body**, whether the stimulator is on or off. Medtronic warns it "can cause neurostimulation system or tissue damage and can result in severe injury or death."\[39\]\[40\] Tell every physiotherapist, chiropractor and dentist.
- **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** is contraindicated. **Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** is listed by manufacturers as unestablished or contraindicated.\[14\]\[39\] Some published cases used strict precautions, but treat it as off-limits unless the DBS team and psychiatrist plan it together.\[41\]
- **MRI.** Only under the manufacturer's specific conditions. Newer systems (for example Medtronic Percept, Boston Scientific Vercise Genus, Abbott Infinity) have "MRI mode" and MR-conditional labeling; Medtronic Percept is labeled for full-body 1.5T and 3T scans under conditions. Older systems may be restricted to head-only scans or need an eligibility review.\[42\]\[43\]\[44\] Always contact the DBS team *before* any MRI is booked. Controllers and chargers are **MR Unsafe** and must stay outside the scanner room.\[45\]
- **Security scanners and shop theft detectors.** These won't harm the device, but may switch stimulation on or off or cause a brief surge in sensation on some systems. Show the **device ID card** and request a hand pat-down instead of a wand held over the generator. Pack the controller in carry-on luggage.\[23\]\[42\]
- **Electromagnetic interference.** Normal household appliances, microwaves and phones are fine. Avoid arc welding, high-voltage equipment and strong magnets. Avoid prolonged temperatures above about 100°F (saunas, hot tubs, tanning beds), scuba diving deeper than 33 feet, and rides or activities that jolt the neck or risk blows to the generator.\[23\]
- **Swimming.** Case reports describe people losing the ability to swim after DBS. Re-test swimming in shallow, supervised water first.\[23\]\[46\]
- **Medical and dental procedures.** Per the UC Davis DBS medical safety handout:
  - For surgery with electrocautery, Medtronic and Boston Scientific devices should be turned **off**, and Abbott devices put in "Surgery Mode." Bipolar cautery is preferred, and settings should be documented beforehand in case the device resets.\[42\]
  - For cardioversion, ECG or EEG, turn the device off.\[42\]
  - For dental work, ultrasonic cleaning is allowed with Abbott and Medtronic systems only if no grounding pad is placed on the patient. Boston Scientific users should have manual cleaning only.\[42\]
  - Electric toothbrushes and drills are fine.\[42\]
  - Lithotripsy should not be focused near the device.\[42\]
  
  Always tell the dentist or proceduralist about DBS in advance and bring the controller.
- **Always carry** the device ID card, the controller and the DBS clinic's phone number. Register the device in your medical records and with emergency contacts.

### Common changes after DBS and how to manage them

**Speech and voice.** Speech is the most common functional trade-off with STN-DBS.\[47\] In a controlled study, speech intelligibility **fell on average about 14–17% one year after STN-DBS**, compared with roughly 4% in medically treated patients.\[48\] A 2026 *Movement Disorders* meta-analysis confirmed a modest but consistent decline compared with best medical therapy.\[49\]\[50\] Some people improve, and stimulation adjustments can sometimes help.\[48\]\[51\] What to do:
- Report slurring or softer speech to the programming team, since settings can sometimes be changed.
- Get an early speech-language pathology assessment.
- Consider LSVT LOUD or similar intensive therapy, but set realistic goals. One UCL thesis found traditional PD dysarthria therapy was less effective after STN-DBS, where speech changes are often of a different type.\[52\]

**Gait, balance and freezing.** DBS helps freezing that occurs in the "off" state, but freezing and imbalance during "on" periods often persist. In one analysis, about 45% of people who froze before surgery still did at 6–12 months, though often less severely. Keep up fall prevention and physiotherapy after surgery.

**Cognition.** Verbal fluency (finding words quickly) commonly declines slightly.\[53\] Larger cognitive drops are uncommon in well-screened candidates. Report any new confusion, which may reflect settings, medication reductions, infection or disease progression.

**Mood and behavior.** Several patterns can appear:
- Apathy or depression, especially as dopaminergic drugs are reduced.
- Impulsivity, hypomania or mania, especially with certain stimulation settings.
- Persistence or resolution of impulse control disorders.

Suicide risk is real. In an international survey of 5,311 STN-DBS patients, **0.45% died by suicide and 0.90% attempted suicide**. Rates were highest in the first year after surgery.\[54\]\[55\] A Grenoble cohort (534 patients) found **0.75% completed and 4.11% attempted suicide**, mostly in the first 3 years.\[56\] What families should do:
- Ask directly about mood.
- Watch for hopelessness, withdrawal, risky spending or sudden personality change.
- Report changes the same week.
- Treat any suicidal talk as an emergency.

**Weight gain.** This is very common after STN-DBS. A systematic review found a pooled average gain of **5.7 kg (about 12.6 lb)**, mostly in the first year.\[57\] Likely causes are less energy burned once dyskinesia and tremor settle, lower daily activity, and possibly changes in appetite circuits.\[58\]\[59\] Weigh the person weekly, adjust portions with a dietitian, and build in daily activity from the start.

**Disease progression continues.** Over the years, non-motor and axial symptoms will still advance. Stimulation and medications will need re-tuning, and the Part 2 red flags still apply.

### Practical daily-living support strategies

**Fall prevention and home modifications**
- Remove loose rugs and clutter. Install grab bars by the toilet and in the shower, and use a shower chair and raised toilet seat.
- Light the route from bed to bathroom at night. Keep a urinal or commode by the bed if nighttime urination is frequent.
- Mark doorways and turns with floor tape. Teach cueing strategies for freezing: stepping over a line, counting, a metronome or music beat, or a laser-cane.
- Rise slowly: sit on the edge of the bed, pump the ankles, then stand. Drink fluids early in the day if blood pressure drops on standing.
- Ask for an occupational therapy home-safety visit.

**Exercise.** Exercise is the single best-supported self-management strategy.
- **Tai chi:** In a *New England Journal of Medicine* randomized trial, twice-weekly tai chi for 24 weeks improved balance and walking more than resistance training or stretching. Falls were **0.22 per person-month vs. 0.51 (resistance) and 0.62 (stretching)**, which works out to **67% fewer falls than stretching**. Benefits persisted 3 months after the program ended.\[60\]\[61\]
- **Physiotherapy-led programs:** progressive resistance training, balance training, and LSVT BIG (amplitude-focused movement training, supported by meta-analysis).
- **Cycling and aerobic exercise:** reasonable, though trials are smaller.
- **Non-contact boxing:** popular, and reasonable to try. The evidence is mostly small or observational.
- Start supervised after DBS clearance, typically after the early 4–6-week restriction period. Avoid blows to the generator site.

**Speech and swallowing**
- LSVT LOUD has the strongest evidence among speech treatments for Parkinson's. In a 2018 randomized trial (64 participants), it improved vocal loudness more than an articulation-focused therapy or no treatment. Communication gains were maintained at 7 months.\[62\]\[63\]
- For swallowing:
  - Sit fully upright for meals and for 30 minutes afterward.
  - Take small bites and sips, and avoid talking while chewing.
  - Time meals to "on" periods.
  - Follow the speech therapist's advice on textures and thickened liquids.
  - Keep up good mouth care, which reduces pneumonia risk.

**Nutrition and constipation**
- Some people find that high-protein meals blunt levodopa. A dietitian can help with timing; don't cut protein without advice.
- Aim for fiber, 6–8 glasses of fluid daily unless restricted,\[64\] and regular movement. Discuss stool softeners or osmotic laxatives early.

**Medications**
- Use pill organizers, alarms and phone apps.
- Never stop Parkinson's drugs abruptly.
- Keep a current list with exact times.
- Check every new prescription against the Parkinson's Foundation contraindicated-medication list.

**Sleep**
- Keep a regular schedule and get morning light.
- Limit late caffeine and long daytime naps.
- If dream enactment is present, make the bedroom safe: pad sharp corners, move the bed away from windows, and consider separate beds.
- Report daytime sleepiness, especially in drivers.

**Mental health and social life**
- Screen for depression and anxiety. Counseling, medication and support groups work.
- Encourage social activity: group exercise classes, choirs (which also help voice), volunteering and Parkinson's support groups.
- Prolonged isolation worsens mood and cognition.

**Caregiver self-care**
- Caregiver strain is a recognized risk for both partners.
- Use respite care, adult day programs, a second "backup" care partner, and the Parkinson's Foundation helpline (1-800-4PD-INFO).\[65\]
- Davis Phinney Foundation and APDA care-partner meetups, and a social worker's help with benefits, can also help.
- Schedule your own health checkups.

### What to discuss with the DBS team (and when to call)

**Before and early after surgery, ask:**
- Which of my symptoms should improve most and least?
- Which target (STN or GPi) and why, and what does that mean for medication reduction, speech and mood?
- Which device and battery type, who manages charging, and what is the replacement plan?
- What is the written activity, wound-care and driving plan?
- What is the programming schedule, and can some visits be done remotely?
- Who do we call after hours, and what counts as an emergency?
- Is it MRI-compatible, and under what conditions?

**Call the DBS or programming team promptly for:**
- loss of benefit, or a controller showing therapy off or a low or depleted battery
- new speech slurring, muscle pulling, double vision or tingling after a setting change
- mood changes, impulsivity or mania
- new falls or confusion
- skin changes over any hardware
- any planned surgery, dental work or MRI

**Go to emergency care for:**
- fever with severe rigidity
- sudden inability to move or swallow
- signs of stroke
- seizures
- suicidal thoughts

---

## Caveats

- Figures quoted come from specific studies and cohorts. Individual courses vary widely, especially for dementia, where estimates range from about 19% to 83% after 20 years depending on the population.\[7\]
- Device-specific rules differ by manufacturer and model and change with new labeling. Always defer to the patient's own device manual and DBS clinic.
- Evidence for tai chi, LSVT LOUD and physiotherapy is stronger than for boxing, cycling programs or wearable monitoring, which are reasonable but less well proven.
- DBS withdrawal and PHS figures come from small case series and retrospective cohorts. They show the problem is rare and serious, but the exact frequency is uncertain.

*This report is general information, not medical advice. Decisions about medications, DBS settings, procedures and emergencies should always be made with the patient's own neurologist, movement disorder specialist and DBS team.*
