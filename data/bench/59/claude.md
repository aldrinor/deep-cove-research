# How Migratory Birds Navigate: Compasses, Maps, Cues and Disturbances

Migratory birds find their way by combining at least two systems. A **compass** tells them which way to fly. It draws on the geomagnetic field, the sun, the stars and polarized skylight. A **map** sense tells experienced birds where they are relative to a goal. The best-supported map cues are magnetic inclination, intensity and declination, plus smell in pigeons and seabirds. All of this rests on an inherited, clock-driven program that steers young birds on their first trip. The magnetic compass is the best understood of these senses. It is light-dependent, works through the eye, and probably relies on a quantum "radical-pair" reaction in the retinal protein cryptochrome 4. The map sense is still contested. The main natural disruptors are geomagnetic storms and overcast skies. The main human-made ones are artificial light at night and radio-frequency electromagnetic noise.

## TL;DR
- **Juveniles mostly fly on a compass, adults also use a map.** First-time migrants follow an inherited "clock-and-compass" program (fly this direction for this long). Experienced adults can correct for displacements of up to 3,700 km, which counts as true navigation. Starlings, white-crowned sparrows and reed warblers have all shown this.
- **The cues overlap and back each other up.** Compass cues are magnetic (an inclination compass based on cryptochrome in the eye), the sun (corrected for time of day), the stars (learned from how the sky rotates) and twilight polarized light, which may calibrate the others. Map cues are probably magnetic (inclination, intensity, declination) and, at least in pigeons and shearwaters, smell. Landmarks such as coastlines take over in familiar areas.
- **Disruptions are real and measurable.** Geomagnetic storms cut nocturnal migration intensity by 9–17% (Gulson-Castillo et al. 2023, *PNAS*) and, in autumn, reduced birds' effort flying against the wind by 25% under cloudy skies (University of Michigan). AM-band electromagnetic noise switched off the robin magnetic compass in double-blind tests. A single bright light installation affected about 1.1 million migrants over seven nights. Light reduction and electromagnetic shielding are clear priorities for managers.

## Key Findings

### 1. The conceptual framework: compass vs. map

**Compass vs. map.** A compass (orientation) gives a bird a reference direction. A map (position-finding) tells it where it is relative to the goal. Under the classic **map-and-compass model**, an experienced bird first works out its position, then chooses a heading toward the goal, then holds that heading with a compass. **True navigation** means reaching a goal from an unfamiliar place with no route-based information. The diagnostic test is to displace birds and see whether they correct.

**Juveniles: clock-and-compass (vector) navigation.** Young migrants appear to inherit a program: one or more compass headings, each held for a set amount of time, timed by an internal circannual clock. A displaced juvenile therefore keeps flying in its inherited direction and ends up in the wrong place.

**The key displacement experiments:**
- **Perdeck (1958, *Ardea*).** From 1948 to 1957, "more than 11000 marked Starlings (one-third adults, two-thirds juveniles), caught during autumn migration near The Hague, were (by airplane) displaced to Switzerland." In Perdeck's words, "the juvenile Starlings after their displacement followed a course about the same as the preferred direction held in the region from where they were taken."\[1\] Juveniles ended up wintering in Spain and France, while adults turned back toward their normal wintering area in England.\[2\] A 2024 reanalysis in *Biology Letters* found that "none of the obtained recoveries suggested that juveniles followed adults."\[3\] This rules out a social explanation. Total counts differ between sources (that reanalysis used about 14,800 birds from the full dataset), so "more than 11,000" is the safest figure.\[4\]
- **White-crowned sparrows (Thorup et al. 2007, *PNAS*).** Researchers radio-tracked birds from small aircraft. They found that "only adult, and not juvenile, long-distance migrating white-crowned sparrows rapidly recognize and correct for a continent-wide displacement of 3,700 km from the west coast of North America to previously unvisited areas on the east coast." Juveniles "continue in the species-specific migratory direction." The adults' map therefore covers at least the continental US.\[5\] In the team's summary, "juveniles use their compass, but the adults also use their map."\[6\]
- **Eurasian reed warblers (Chernetsov, Kishkinev, Mouritsen and colleagues).** Adults caught in spring at Rybachy on the Baltic coast and moved 1,000 km east to Zvenigorod near Moscow shifted their heading in Emlen funnels from NE to NW, back toward their breeding grounds.\[7\]\[8\] This became the standard test system for map experiments.
- **Caveat.** The adult/juvenile split is not absolute. Thorup & Rabøl's (2007) meta-analysis of orientation-cage experiments found that "even juvenile birds were able to detect and react to displacements, including those in which displacement was simulated in a planetarium," especially under starry skies. It is unclear whether this reflects true navigation or homing to a familiar point on the route.

### 2. Compass cues

#### 2a. The magnetic compass

**It is an inclination compass.** Wiltschko & Wiltschko (1972, *Science*) showed that European robins "do not use the polarity of the magnetic field." Instead they read the axis and dip of the field lines, treating as "poleward" the direction where field lines and gravity form the smaller angle.\[9\] In practice the compass says "poleward vs. equatorward," not "north vs. south." It also fails near the magnetic equator, where field lines are horizontal.

**It depends on light and sits in the eye.**
- The compass is disrupted under red light and works under blue-green light.
- Robins have a magnetic compass in both eyes.\[10\]
- Zapka et al. (2009, *Nature*) showed that robins with lesions of **Cluster N**, a night-active forebrain area fed by the visual thalamofugal pathway, could not orient magnetically but could still use their sun and star compasses. Cutting the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve did *not* affect magnetic compass orientation. The title sums it up: "visual but not trigeminal mediation of magnetic compass information."\[11\]\[12\]

**The radical-pair / cryptochrome hypothesis.** Light excites a flavin in a cryptochrome protein and creates a pair of radicals (molecules with unpaired electrons). The radicals interconvert between quantum spin states at a rate that depends on how the molecule sits relative to the Earth's field, and this could produce a direction-dependent signal in the retina.
- **Xu et al. (2021, *Nature*)** showed that "the photochemistry of cryptochrome 4 (CRY4) from the night-migratory European robin… is magnetically sensitive in vitro, and more so than CRY4 from two non-migratory bird species, chicken… and pigeon." Site-directed mutants pointed to "four successive flavin–tryptophan radical pairs."\[13\] ErCRY4 is expressed in double cones and long-wavelength single cones. Co-author Peter Hore cautioned "if we can prove that cryptochrome 4 is the magnetic sensor…", so in-vivo proof is still missing.\[14\]\[15\]
- **The radio-frequency "fingerprint."** Radical pairs should be disrupted by weak oscillating fields up to a cut-off frequency set by the molecule's chemistry. Leberecht et al. (2022, *J Comp Physiol A*) found that broadband 75–85 MHz fields disoriented Eurasian blackcaps, "consistent with a flavin-based radical pair magnetoreceptor."\[16\]\[17\] A follow-up in *PNAS* (2023) found no disruption at 140–150 or 235–245 MHz, which puts the cut-off between roughly 80 and 145 MHz, as theory predicts.\[18\] Hore & Mouritsen (2016, *Annual Review of Biophysics*) and Mouritsen (2018, *Nature*) review this framework.

**The magnetite hypothesis (a candidate map sensor, not a compass).** Iron-oxide particles linked to nerves could sense field *intensity*, which would suit a map rather than a compass.
- The once-celebrated iron-rich structures in the pigeon upper beak turned out to be immune cells. Treiber et al. (2012, *Nature*) showed "clusters of iron-rich cells in the rostro-medial upper beak of the pigeon… are macrophages, not magnetosensitive neurons," calling for "a renewed search for the true magnetite-dependent magnetoreceptor."\[19\]
- Even so, the trigeminal nerve matters for the *map*. Kishkinev et al. (2013, *PLoS ONE*) displaced 57 Eurasian reed warblers with or without sectioned V1 (the ophthalmic branch); birds with V1 cut still oriented in their seasonal direction but did not correct for the 1,000 km eastward displacement. Pakhomov et al. (2018, *Scientific Reports*) found the same result after a purely *virtual magnetic* displacement: sham-operated birds re-oriented, while "birds with bilaterally ablated ophthalmic branches of the trigeminal nerves were not able to re-orient."
- Candidate receptor sites (the inner-ear lagena, other iron-containing cells) are still unconfirmed. Whatever V1 carries, its receptor has not been found.

#### 2b. Sun compass and polarized light

A sun compass needs an internal circadian clock, because the sun's compass bearing (azimuth) moves about 15° per hour on average. The classic proof is clock-shifting: birds whose internal clock has been reset deflect their orientation in a predictable way. Day-migrants and homing pigeons rely on it heavily. Nocturnal migrants may use the sunset position and skylight cues at dusk to choose a departure heading.

The band of maximum skylight **polarization** crosses the horizon 90° from the sun at sunrise and sunset. Averaging the sunrise and sunset intersections gives geographic north–south, regardless of latitude or season.\[20\] Muheim, Phillips & Åkesson (2006, *Science*) showed that migratory Savannah sparrows "use polarized light cues from the region of sky near the horizon to recalibrate the magnetic compass at both sunrise and sunset."\[21\] They reported the same in white-throated sparrows (2009) "during both autumn and spring migration."\[22\]

#### 2c. Star compass

Emlen's planetarium experiments with indigo buntings in the 1960s–70s showed that the star compass is **learned, not innate**. Young birds learn "north" as the centre of celestial rotation.\[23\] Buntings raised under a sky rotating around Betelgeuse treated Betelgeuse as north, and they "failed to re-orient to the natural sky rotating around the 'real' celestial north even after being housed in an outdoor aviary with full view of all celestial cues for six months."\[24\] Once learned, the star pattern can be used without watching the sky rotate.\[24\] Emlen also introduced the **Emlen funnel**, an inkpad-floored cone in which a restless bird's footprints record its preferred direction.\[25\] It is still the workhorse of cue-manipulation experiments.

#### 2d. Calibration and hierarchy of cues

Because each compass has a different reference (magnetic vs. geographic north), birds must calibrate them against each other.
- **Evidence for polarized light as the reference.** Muheim and colleagues argue that twilight polarized light near the horizon is the primary reference that calibrates the magnetic compass. In the field, Cochran, Mouritsen & Wikelski (2004, *Science*) found that free-flying thrushes appeared to recalibrate their magnetic compass daily from twilight cues.\[26\]\[27\]
- **Contrary evidence.** Wiltschko and colleagues (2008, "Contradictory results on the role of polarized light in compass calibration") found the magnetic compass dominant in Australian silvereyes. Chernetsov et al. (2011) reported that "not all songbirds calibrate their magnetic compass from twilight cues," and some shorebirds failed to recalibrate.\[28\]\[29\]\[30\]
- **A reasonable synthesis.** During development, celestial rotation calibrates the magnetic compass. In adults, the hierarchy varies with species, season and experimental details, particularly how much of the horizon a bird can see.

### 3. Map / position-finding cues

#### 3a. The geomagnetic map

Total intensity and inclination vary mostly north–south. Declination (the angle between magnetic and geographic north) varies more east–west in parts of Europe, so combining them could in principle give grid-like coordinates.
- **Virtual magnetic displacement.** Reed warblers at Rybachy exposed to the magnetic field of Zvenigorod (all three parameters changed, no physical move) re-oriented as if they had been moved (Kishkinev et al. 2015).\[31\]
- **Declination as a longitude cue.** Chernetsov et al. (2017, *Current Biology*) found that an 8.5° declination shift alone (intensity and inclination unchanged) re-oriented adult reed warblers as if moved to Scotland. Juveniles became disoriented.\[32\]
- **Kishkinev et al. (2021, *Current Biology*).** Birds in Austria were exposed to field values found far *beyond* their range. They "demonstrated re-orientation toward their migratory corridor as if they were translocated to the corresponding location but only when all naturally occurring magnetic cues were presented, not when declination was changed alone."\[33\] This suggests birds can **extrapolate** magnetic gradients into unfamiliar territory. The declination-only combination did not exist in nature, and the authors suggest the birds may have discounted it as unreliable.\[34\]
- **Packmor et al. (2024, *Proc R Soc B*)** found that reed warblers "can extract positional information from magnetic inclination and magnetic declination alone."\[35\]
- **Magnetic "stop signs" (Wynn et al. 2022, *Science*).** Wynn and colleagues analysed 17,799 consecutive-year ringing recoveries of reed warblers (1940–2018). Year-to-year drift in the field moves a site's magnetic signature, and birds returned where their natal *inclination* had moved. "The only hypothesis that significantly out-performed the null model was inclination acting as a stop-sign." The suggested mechanism is that inclination "is learned before departure," and birds stop "at the first place where the right inclination is encountered on an inherited return vector."\[36\] Manx shearwaters show a similar pattern: small natal-to-return changes in inclination predicted shifts in recruitment latitude (Wynn et al. 2020, *Current Biology*).\[37\]

#### 3b. Olfactory navigation

- **Pigeons.** Floriano Papi's olfactory map hypothesis, now more than 40 years old, holds that pigeons learn how wind-borne odours vary around the loft. Anosmic pigeons repeatedly fail to home from unfamiliar sites (reviewed by Gagliardo 2013, "Forty years of olfactory navigation in birds," *J Exp Biol*).\[38\]
- **Shearwaters.**
  - Cory's shearwaters displaced about 800 km from the Azores were tracked by GPS or satellite. Anosmic birds were impaired, but magnetically disturbed birds were not. The authors concluded geomagnetic information is "neither sufficient nor necessary for shearwater navigation over the ocean" (Gagliardo et al. 2013).\[39\]\[40\]
  - Scopoli's shearwaters displaced 400 km in the Mediterranean (Pollonara et al. 2015, *Scientific Reports*) behaved the same way. "Anosmic shearwaters oriented in a direction significantly different from the home direction when in open sea" but "are able to home eventually by following coastal features." Control and magnet-carrying birds homed quickly.\[41\]\[42\]
- **Songbirds and gulls.**
  - Holland et al. (2009, *J Exp Biol*) radio-tracked gray catbirds. Anosmic adults "reverted to a direction similar to that shown by juveniles," while magnetic treatment "had no effect on the orientation of either adults or juveniles." The sample was small: 24 adults and 24 juveniles across three treatments.\[43\]\[44\]
  - Lesser black-backed gulls with severed olfactory nerves kept a southerly heading but could not compensate for a westward displacement (Wikelski et al. 2015).\[45\]
  - Results in reed warblers point the other way. Anosmic warblers still showed a compensatory response after long-distance translocation (*J Ornithol* 2019), whereas V1 section abolished it.\[46\] The olfactory map in songbirds is not established.

#### 3c. Landmarks, topography and infrasound

In familiar areas birds pilot by visual features such as coastlines, rivers and mountain ranges. Anosmic shearwaters falling back on coastlines is a direct demonstration of this. Social learning in cranes probably works partly by passing on landmark knowledge.\[47\] Jonathan Hagstrum (USGS) proposed that pigeons use **infrasound** "maps."\[48\] His acoustic ray-tracing (2013, *J Exp Biol*) suggested that Cornell pigeons, which consistently vanished at random from Jersey Hill, lay in an acoustic "shadow" of their loft.\[49\] On 13 August 1969, the one day they homed well, infrasound would have reached the site.\[50\]\[51\] This is a correlational re-analysis of historical data and has not been tested experimentally.

#### 3d. Multimodal integration

The overall picture is redundancy. Different cues dominate at different scales and in different groups: magnetic and celestial cues for continental orientation, magnetic or olfactory gradients for position, landmarks for the final approach. Birds also seem to downweight cues that conflict.

### 4. Other factors

- **Circannual rhythms and Zugunruhe.** Caged migrants show nocturnal restlessness (Zugunruhe) whose timing and amount track the length and timing of the natural journey. This is the behavioural basis for funnel testing.
- **Genetics of direction.**
  - Berthold & Helbig (1992, *Ibis*) found that cross-breeding blackcaps with different directions "produced offspring with phenotypically intermediate directional preferences."\[52\]
  - Berthold et al. (1992, *Nature*) showed that blackcaps wintering in Britain, a route that barely existed before the 1950s, produce offspring that orient west-northwest. This is "genetically distinct" and "must have evolved within the past 30 years"\[53\] — a textbook case of rapid microevolution, probably helped by garden feeding and milder winters.\[54\]
- **Wind and weather.** Migrants choose departure nights with favourable tailwinds. They partly compensate for crosswind drift, and adults do so better than juveniles.
- **Social learning.** Mueller et al. (2013, *Science*) used 8 years of data on reintroduced whooping cranes. "Social learning from older birds reduced deviations from a straight-line path, with 7 years of experience yielding a 38% improvement in migratory accuracy."\[55\] Genetic relatedness did not predict performance.\[56\] Abrahms et al. (2021, *Nature Communications*), using 16 years of satellite tracking of 105 reintroduced whooping cranes, found that "subadult birds relied on social information, while mature birds primarily relied on experiential information."

### 5. Disturbances

#### Natural
- **Geomagnetic storms.**
  - Gulson-Castillo et al. (2023, *PNAS*) analysed data from 37 US NEXRAD weather radars (1995–2018) and found "a 9 to 17% decrease in migration intensity in both spring and fall during severe space weather events," with predicted declines starting at about 500 nT.
  - In autumn, birds spent less effort flying against crosswinds, most strongly "under both overcast conditions and high geomagnetic disturbance"; according to the University of Michigan, this effort "was reduced by 25% under cloudy skies during strong solar storms in the fall." This suggests "a depression of active navigation such that birds drift more with the wind."
  - Tonelli, Youngflesh & Tingley (2023, *Scientific Reports*) analysed 2.2 million ringing records of 152 species (1960–2019) and found that "vagrancy across North American landbirds is associated with increased geomagnetic disturbance in the fall migration season." Both studies are correlational.
- **Magnetic anomalies.** Alerstam (1987, *J Exp Biol*) tracked migrants by radar over a strong magnetic anomaly in Sweden. Birds changed altitude, usually descending, most often where intensity was abnormally high and inclination steepest. They were sometimes "briefly disturbed (clear weather) or disoriented for longer periods… (poor visibility)."\[57\]
- **Overcast skies and fog.** Cloud removes star, sun and polarization cues, leaving the magnetic compass and wind. This is why storms plus overcast skies are a common recipe for vagrancy and for mass attraction to lights.
- **Storms and crosswinds** push birds off course, especially inexperienced juveniles. This produces vagrants and the reverse-direction "mirror-image" vagrancy that many rarity records show.
- **Moonlight** brightens the night sky and may interact with how attractive artificial lights are. Evidence is limited.

#### Anthropogenic
- **Artificial light at night (ALAN).**
  - Van Doren et al. (2017, *PNAS*) studied the 9/11 "Tribute in Light" beams in New York using radar, acoustic sensors and simulations. The beams "influenced ≈1.1 million birds (95% CI: 0.6–1.6 million)" over seven nights in seven years, at altitudes up to about 4 km. Birds circled, slowed and called more, and the effect occurred "even when it isn't foggy or stormy."\[58\]\[59\]
  - Brief shutdowns, triggered when more than 1,000 birds were circling, dispersed the birds within minutes. This is strong evidence that switching lights off works.\[60\]
  - The 1.1 million figure counts birds whose behaviour changed, not deaths.\[60\] Building collisions are the lethal end of the same attraction process.
- **Radio-frequency noise.** Engels et al. (2014, *Nature*) found that robins in unscreened huts on the Oldenburg campus could not use their magnetic compass. Grounded aluminium screening, which cut noise between 50 kHz and 5 MHz "by approximately two orders of magnitude," restored orientation. Removing the grounding or adding broadband noise abolished it again, and birds at a rural site needed no screening. These were "fully double-blinded tests." The disrupting fields were far below WHO limits, about a thousandth of the guideline value according to the university press release.\[61\]\[62\] Power-line (50/60 Hz) fields fall outside the radical-pair-sensitive band, and their effect on migrants in the wild is not established.
- **Offshore wind farms, habitat loss and climate change.** These act mainly through collision, barrier effects, the loss of stopover "refuelling" sites and phenological mismatch rather than through the sensory compass itself. Lit turbines and platforms add to the light-attraction problem. Climate change is also *reshaping* routes, as with the British-wintering blackcaps.
- **Secular variation (slow drift of the geomagnetic field).**
  - In reed warblers and Manx shearwaters, birds seem to *track* drifting inclination rather than correct for it. They return slightly displaced, in the predicted direction and by about the predicted amount.
  - A modelling study (Wynn et al. 2022, *J Comp Physiol A*) found that the points where inclination and intensity lines cross shifted "a median of 95 km in the UK but an average of 28 km in Poland." It concluded that a single magnetic coordinate plus local cues near the destination could keep philopatry (returning to the birth site) robust.\[63\]\[64\]

### 6. Where the science is uncertain or contested

- **Radical pair vs. magnetite.** The radical-pair compass is now strongly supported: light-dependence, Cluster N, RF disruption with a flavin-consistent cut-off, and in-vitro CRY4 sensitivity. It is still not proven in a living animal; knocking out CRY4 in a migrant has not been reported. The magnetite map receptor has never been located, and the beak story collapsed. Whether the trigeminal map signal comes from magnetite, from another mechanism, or partly from a radical pair is open.
- **The olfactory map in migratory songbirds.** It is strong in pigeons and shearwaters. In songbirds the evidence is thin and conflicting: catbirds suggest yes, reed warblers suggest no.
- **How declination is sensed.** Declination requires comparing magnetic north with geographic north, for example from a star or polarization compass. How birds integrate these, and why declination-only shifts sometimes work (2017) and sometimes don't (2021), is unresolved.
- **Gradient map vs. signposts.** Extrapolation beyond the range (2021) suggests a gradient map. Stop-sign results (2022) suggest simpler single-coordinate rules. Birds may use both at different stages.
- **Sensitivity.** The ringing-recovery studies imply birds detect inclination differences of only about 0.01–0.05°. A 2024 exchange in *Nature Communications* (Schneider, Wynn, Packmor, Lindecke et al.) noted that these studies "used tiny amounts of year-on-year secular variation (c.0.02–0.05 degrees)." It argued that such sensitivity is hard to reconcile with sensor physics, and proposed models in which low-resolution sensors produce the same patterns.\[65\]\[66\]
- **Cue hierarchy.** Whether polarized light or magnetic cues are the primary calibration reference remains disputed between research groups.

## Caveats
- Many mechanistic results come from caged birds in Emlen funnels. These measure *intended* direction, not real-world success, and small samples with circular statistics can mislead.
- The headline field results on storms, vagrancy and stop signs are correlational, from radar or ringing data.
- The CRY4 evidence is in vitro.
- Some figures differ between sources, such as how many starlings Perdeck displaced. I have used the most conservative figure.
- Very recent work (2025–26), including reports that free-ranging Manx shearwaters may not rely on magnetic cues, could not be verified and is not relied on here.\[71\]\[72\]

## Recommendations
- **Conservation and policy.** Reduce and switch off high-intensity upward and decorative lighting on peak migration nights. Radar-based migration forecasts make this feasible, and the Tribute in Light shutdowns show it works within minutes.
- **Infrastructure.** Treat broadband electromagnetic noise in the roughly 0.1–100 MHz range as a possible threat near major stopover sites and research stations. The evidence is experimental and strong, but its field scale is unknown.
- **Research priorities:**
  - Test CRY4 in vivo, for example with genetic knockdown in a migrant.
  - Identify the trigeminal map receptor.
  - Run more free-flight GPS displacement studies combining olfactory and magnetic manipulation in songbirds.
  - Test whether storms plus overcast skies predict light-attraction events.
