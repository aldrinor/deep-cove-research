# AI Interaction and Interpersonal Relations: How and Why Human Connection Is Changing

## Abstract

**AI is changing relational conditions:** Artificial intelligence already influences how some people disclose personal experiences, seek support, form attachments, and communicate with others. Its most consequential contribution is not simply another communication channel, but the availability of personalized, responsive agents that can become apparent partners or help author exchanges between people. Evidence through 13 September 2026 supports a conditional thesis: AI frequently complements human relationships, while producing more fundamental changes in particular users’ attachments, expectations, and dependence on commercially controlled systems. Short-term comfort and perceived closeness coexist with associations between intensive companion use, reduced in-person interaction, and poorer well-being among some vulnerable users. These outcomes vary with product design, existing social networks, motives, and deployment context. Lapointe and colleagues’ 2026 review [1] supports this differentiated interpretation.

**The argument proceeds through comparisons:** This paper first distinguishes direct human–AI relationships from AI-mediated human communication and establishes a working meaning of fundamental change. It then examines Replika, Character.AI, ChatGPT, and PARO; explains their relational mechanisms and appeal; compares relationship contexts and populations; and evaluates the ethical, commercial, and governance conditions separating useful support from harmful substitution.

## Defining AI interaction and fundamental relational change

**Three configurations require separate analysis:** Direct interaction with an apparent AI partner, AI-assisted communication between people, and ordinary digital communication have different relational parties and consequences. Assistant-first systems such as ChatGPT can enter either AI configuration, whereas an embodied social robot adds touch and physical presence. The distinction follows the AI-mediated communication literature and contemporary reviews of companion systems. Battisti’s communication-ethics analysis [2] and Li and colleagues’ companion-AI review [3] provide the conceptual basis.

| Configuration | Relational parties | AI contribution | Central relational process | Practical stakes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct companion AI | User and apparent AI partner | Personalized social responses | Disclosure, support, attachment | Comfort and possible dependence |
| Assistant-first AI used socially | User and general-purpose assistant | Advice and responsive conversation | Reflection and emerging rapport | Support beyond the task |
| AI-mediated human communication | Human sender and recipient | Message modification or generation | Authorship and interpersonal inference | Expressiveness and authenticity |
| Embodied social robots | User, robot, and surrounding people | Responsive physical presence | Touch and shared social activity | Engagement within care |
| Ordinary digital communication | Human sender and recipient | Primarily message transmission | Human-authored exchange | Connection across distance |

### Operational definitions

**AI interaction involves adaptive contribution:** For this paper, AI interaction means an encounter in which a computational system processes and generates language or other socially interpretable cues that influence a person’s thoughts, feelings, or actions. It includes text, voice, and multimodal conversations; responsive social robots; and computational assistance that changes interpersonal messages. Companion AI is the repeated socioemotional subset, used or marketed as a friend, confidant, partner, coach, or supportive interlocutor. This definition distinguishes relational interaction from merely encountering an algorithm somewhere in a digital service. The 2026 companion-AI scoping review [3] makes comparable distinctions among therapeutic, companion-first, and assistant-first systems.

**Interpersonal relations involve continuing interdependence:** Interpersonal relations are continuing associations between people whose recurrent interactions have personal or role-based significance and influence their emotions, expectations, or conduct. Relationship science identifies interdependence—each party affecting the other—as a central feature, with close relationships involving influence that is strong, diverse, frequent, and lasting. Romantic partners, friends, family members, colleagues, and professionals and clients therefore fall within scope, although their obligations differ. A human–AI attachment is considered separately because the user’s emotional investment does not establish equivalent mutual recognition or responsibility. A relationship-science analysis [4] explains these interdependence criteria.

**Direct attachment differs from mediation:** In direct human–AI interaction, the AI is the apparent interlocutor toward whom the person may develop an emotional bond. A 2026 review defines Human–AI Attachment as a one-way, non-reciprocal bond formed through direct interaction. In AI-mediated human–human communication, by contrast, a computational agent modifies, augments, or generates a message exchanged between people. The distinction concerns the relationship being evaluated, not merely the software used: someone may converse with ChatGPT for reassurance and subsequently ask it to rewrite a message to a spouse. The attachment review [5] and Battisti’s analysis [2] support this separation.

**Ordinary mediation remains the baseline:** An email or WhatsApp exchange primarily conveys a person’s message through a technological channel. AI mediation additionally intervenes in producing the message, potentially changing its tone, content, or implied emotional competence. The boundary is gradual: spelling correction, predictive replies, stylistic rewriting, and complete message generation involve increasing computational participation. Consequently, analysis should ask what the AI contributes and what the recipient attributes to the sender, rather than treating every technologically assisted message as equally transformed. The computer-mediated versus AI-mediated distinction [2] makes this progression explicit.

### What would make change fundamental?

**Fundamental change concerns relational structure:** A useful working criterion is a durable alteration in the organization or norms of relationships: who can count as a partner, what reciprocity means, how authenticity is assessed, who exercises agency, and what people expect from one another. A brief improvement in mood or adoption of a new messaging tool is consequential but does not by itself meet that criterion. The stronger transformation claim concerns persistent changes in human conduct, obligations, or expectations across situations. Hepp and colleagues similarly argue that the relevant analytical horizon extends beyond isolated human–machine encounters to societal communication. Their 2023 account [6] motivates this wider perspective.

**Different levels can change unevenly:** AI can fundamentally alter an individual’s relational world without replacing human relationships throughout society. Someone who treats a configurable chatbot as a primary intimate partner has acquired a new arrangement of attachment and control; a family using AI to generate stories together may retain its existing relational structure. The paper therefore evaluates transformation at interactional, relationship, and societal levels separately. Its judgment would strengthen toward broad transformation if durable changes in reciprocity, authorship norms, agency, and human contact became widespread; it would strengthen toward complementarity if benefits chiefly supported existing ties.

**The distinction prevents exaggerated conclusions:** Neither impressive conversational realism nor high adoption is sufficient evidence that human relationships have been replaced. Conversely, the persistence of human relationships does not make AI socially trivial: an additional source of personalized validation can alter how people evaluate available partners. The central question is therefore whether AI changes the terms of relating, not simply whether people continue to have friends and families.

## What the named cases reveal

**Product differences explain divergent outcomes:** Replika and Character.AI place relational personas near the center of use; ChatGPT is a general-purpose assistant that users can recruit for companionship; PARO provides embodied, largely nonverbal social interaction. Their evidence should be compared through the outcomes each actually measures.

| Case | Principal configuration | Named evidence | Main observed outcome | Relational significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Replika | Companion-first interaction | Relationship surveys and update studies | Closeness, attachment, and loss | Persona continuity becomes emotionally important |
| Character.AI | Persona-based conversation | Surveys, transcripts, and longitudinal models | Support-seeking and reduced in-person interaction associations | Companionship can become self-reinforcing |
| ChatGPT | Assistant-first interaction | Four-week GPT-4o experiment | Different conversational patterns; mixed psychosocial outcomes | Social behavior exceeds task assistance |
| PARO | Embodied social interaction | Dementia-care trials and touch experiment | Social engagement and immediate comfort | Robots can support a surrounding care relationship |

### Replika: attachment under platform control

**Replika demonstrates substantial perceived closeness:** In De Freitas and colleagues’ study of 101 Replika users, participants compared eight relationship types within the same survey. Replika received higher ratings for satisfaction, perceived support, and closeness than the comparison relationships except a close family member; it also exceeded a close friend. Thirty-seven percent regarded Replika as a partner, and 72% held monthly subscriptions. A separate estimate cited in the companion literature placed romantic use at around half of Replika users, illustrating the platform’s mixture of friendship and romantic functions. The Replika working paper [7] reports these findings and the cited estimate.

**Continuity gives attachment practical consequences:** Replika’s 2023 removal of erotic roleplay provides an unusually revealing episode. Following an Italian data-protection intervention on 3 February 2023, the company removed the feature globally; on 24 March, it offered eligible earlier users an option to restore their original experience. Users’ distress involved more than losing a function: some perceived a discontinuity in the companion’s identity and mourned the earlier persona. The episode connects technical and policy decisions to experiences ordinarily associated with relationship disruption. The natural-shock analysis [7] identifies perceived identity discontinuity as the relevant pathway.

**Later evidence extends the comparison:** A 2026 study combined 54,861 posts from Replika and ChatGPT subreddits with 1,452 participants across seven surveys concerning two platform updates. Negative posts increased by 24.7 percentage points following Replika’s change and by 13.0 percentage points following ChatGPT’s GPT-5 rollout. Replika users reported greater closeness than to a friend, with a standardized difference of *d* = 0.47, and greater anticipated mourning than for other technologies, with *d* = 0.32–0.57. These measures identify emotionally significant attachment and disruption. De Freitas and colleagues’ 2026 study [8] reports the results.

**Augmentation also appears within Replika use:** A survey conducted in China during October–November 2023 retained 428 valid questionnaires and found a positive association between emotional interaction with Replika and users’ human social interaction. Other Replika research describes increased openness, vulnerability, or motivation to communicate with people alongside accounts of substitution. These findings complicate the assumption that strong attachment necessarily weakens human ties: the same platform can become a rehearsal space, supplementary confidant, or preferred alternative. The Chinese survey [9] and Malfacini’s synthesis [10] document these contrasting pathways.

**The case’s distinctive lesson:** Replika shows that a commercial service can acquire the emotional significance of a continuing relationship, making continuity, feature changes, and exit conditions matters of user welfare.

### Character.AI: support, self-disclosure, and displacement

**Support exceeds declared companionship motives:** A Stanford study surveyed 1,131 Character.AI users, including 244 who donated complete transcripts. Just under 12% identified companionship as their primary motive, yet slightly over 50% described their bots as a friend, companion, or romantic partner. More than 80% of donated sessions concerned emotional or social support. The contrast is important: entertainment or curiosity can describe why a person starts using a system while understating the emotional function it eventually performs. Stanford’s August 2026 account [11] reports these distinct measures.

**Vulnerability changes the well-being association:** Within that study, intense use among people with smaller offline social networks correlated with poorer subjective well-being, especially when companionship was the principal motive. Greater willingness to disclose sensitive personal information also correlated with lower well-being. Other intensive users who experienced their activity as meaningful or took pride in it scored more positively. Thus, intensity is not a uniform marker of harm: its significance depends on what the interaction supplies and the human support available around it. The same Stanford report [11] describes this heterogeneity.

**Relational engagement can reinforce itself:** A two-wave study followed Character.AI users from September 2024 to May 2026, with 1,182 participants at baseline and 439 returning after a mean of 12 months. Baseline interaction intensity predicted later intensity, companionship use, and self-disclosure, with standardized coefficients of β = 0.43, 0.32, and 0.28 respectively. Baseline companionship use predicted later companionship use at β = 0.49. At follow-up, greater interaction intensity, companionship use, and self-disclosure were associated with less in-person interaction: β = −0.17, −0.14, and −0.15. “Living with AI Companions”[12] reports these relationships.

**Qualitative accounts explain boundary tensions:** Bhat’s 2025 thematic survey of 344 Character.AI users identified perceived relationship growth alongside addictive engagement, emotional substitution, boundary confusion, and ethical dissonance. That study [13] shows how roleplay can become relationally consequential. A preprint submitted on 13 September 2026 examined 17,053 quotations from 3,930 teen-relevant Reddit posts, finding comfort, recognition, identity exploration, and rehearsal alongside dependence and social disruption. Awareness of artificiality coexisted with guilt, obligation, grief, and distress. The Reddit analysis [14] therefore challenges the idea that knowing “it is only AI” eliminates emotional consequences.

**The case’s central implication:** Character.AI makes the reinforcement of support-seeking and self-disclosure visible, with the greatest concern arising where AI engagement accompanies shrinking human contact.

### ChatGPT: an assistant becomes socially significant

**Assistant-first design does not prevent attachment:** ChatGPT differs from purpose-built companions because companionship emerges through users’ goals and prompting rather than defining the entire product. Its language fluency, voice interaction, and optional memory or personalization can nonetheless support rapport and emotional reliance. The 2026 platform-update study also found increased negativity and loss framing after the GPT-5 rollout, demonstrating that continuity can matter beyond explicitly romantic products. The system taxonomy [3] and update study [8] support this distinction.

**Randomized conditions produced a restrained result:** Fang and colleagues’ four-week GPT-4o experiment assigned 981 participants to combinations of text, neutral voice, or engaging voice and open-ended, nonpersonal, or personal conversation prompts. The study generated more than 300,000 messages. Assigned modality and conversation type did not significantly change loneliness or socialization. Participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more nevertheless reported higher loneliness, less real-world socialization, greater emotional dependence, and more problematic use. The experiment therefore separates differences in conversational behavior from differences in its measured psychosocial outcomes. The GPT-4o study [15] reports both findings.

**Immediate relief remains a meaningful benefit:** Related GPT-based research provides evidence of short-term support without establishing equivalence across products. One 15-minute interaction with a customized GPT-3 companion reduced loneliness similarly to an online human conversation and more than video watching or no activity. In a seven-day follow-up involving 15 minutes daily, reductions followed each interaction and were largest on the first day. The relationship-science review [4] summarizes this experiment. Separately, Szczuka and colleagues describe a seven-day ChatGPT-4 study whose relief was strongest initially and plateaued, emphasizing the distinction between momentary comfort and sustained changes in loneliness. Their intimacy review [16] discusses that pattern.

**The case’s main contribution:** ChatGPT shows that general assistance can become emotionally important, while more socially expressive interaction does not automatically produce better human relationships.

### PARO: embodied support within care

**PARO reveals a different relational pathway:** A 2026 systematic review of AI-enabled social robots in dementia care included 16 studies: seven randomized trials and nine pre–post studies. Fifteen reported potential gains in some dimensions of social interaction, including communication, participation, or engagement with caregivers, peers, or robots; one intervention–control comparison was nonsignificant at *p* = 0.18. Within this literature, a Taiwanese randomized trial of 52 older adults with dementia found greater social interaction following PARO sessions. The systematic review [17] reports these findings.

**The intervention involved sustained exposure:** The Taiwanese trial provided individual, non-facilitated sessions lasting 60 minutes, three times weekly for eight weeks. That equals 60 × 3 × 8 = 1,440 minutes, or 24 hours per participant. Group, time, and interaction effects were significant at *p* < 0.001. The relational importance lies in PARO’s capacity to support engagement within a care environment, including interaction around the robot, rather than requiring it to imitate an articulate human confidant. The trial summary [17] supplies the schedule and results.

**Touch adds a distinct mechanism:** In a controlled experiment with 83 healthy adults, mild heat-pain ratings in the PARO group fell from 1.4 to 0.8 on a 0–10 scale during touch—a calculated reduction of 0.6 points. Mere co-presence without touch produced a rating of 1.1 and did not significantly differ from baseline. Happiness increased, while salivary oxytocin decreased rather than following a simple “more bonding hormone” account. The findings suggest that physical interaction and immediate affective comfort deserve separate analysis from conversational intimacy. The 2020 PARO experiment [18] reports these results.

**The case’s comparative lesson:** Embodied AI may augment human care through shared activity and comfort without reproducing the disclosure-and-validation dynamics of companion chatbots.

## How AI changes interpersonal processes

### Communication patterns, timing, and tone

**AI changes the rhythm of availability:** Companion systems make responsive conversation accessible with little coordination and without a human partner’s competing schedule or needs. This can turn an occasional support-seeking activity into a daily routine and make immediate response feel normal. Personalization and conversational memory further reduce the work of re-establishing context. These features are relational affordances—possibilities for action—not guaranteed outcomes: their value depends on whether easier conversation prepares a person for human contact or becomes its preferred replacement. The attachment review [5] describes near-constant, low-effort support.

**Communication habits can cross contexts:** Research summarized by Guingrich and Graziano reports convergence between Replika users’ linguistic styles and their chatbot’s style. The same review describes Garg and Sengupta’s tracking of 18 families over an average of 58 weeks: children’s high-volume commands to household voice assistants sometimes carried an aggressive tone into conversations with friends and family. These examples identify a route from machine interaction to human communication through repeated practice. They also show that the relevant change need not involve affection or anthropomorphism; command habits alone may matter. The 2024 carry-over review [19] discusses both studies.

**Mediation can improve conversational performance:** Hohenstein and colleagues’ research, summarized by Battisti, found that algorithmic responses increased communication speed and positive emotional language and led partners to judge each other as closer and more cooperative. Yet suspected algorithmic assistance produced more negative evaluations. The contrast explains why communication outcomes and relationship interpretations can diverge: AI may improve the exchange while changing what that exchange reveals about its apparent author. Faster, warmer communication is useful, but its relational meaning depends partly on expectations about personal involvement. Battisti’s discussion of the experiments [2] presents this tension.

### Self-disclosure and emotional support

**Lower social risk encourages disclosure:** AI can offer a setting in which users anticipate less rejection, embarrassment, or judgment than in a conversation with another person. A user may test an identity, articulate a fear, or rehearse a difficult disclosure before involving someone whose response matters deeply. The relevant safety is initially psychological: the person feels safer speaking. This perceived nonjudgmental environment is a central explanation for adolescent identity exploration and emotional expression, as well as adult use after painful relational experiences. The adolescent review [20] and intimacy review [16] describe these pathways.

**Modality alters what people disclose:** In the GPT-4o experiment, automated classifications found problem-sharing in 17.13% of text interactions, support-seeking in 15.78%, and discussion of alleviating loneliness in 8.35%, each more prominent than in voice interaction. Text also showed more comparable levels of personal sharing between user and chatbot, whereas that apparent symmetry was less noticeable in voice. These results challenge an intuitive assumption that a more natural-sounding voice must produce greater emotional openness. Text may offer control over pacing and expression that matters more than auditory realism for some disclosures. Fang and colleagues [15] report the modality patterns.

**Apparent reciprocity requires careful interpretation:** A chatbot’s autobiographical statement or expression of vulnerability can resemble a partner’s self-disclosure, and that resemblance can help the user feel understood. However, generated backstory is different from revealing experiences with personal consequences for an independent speaker. The user bears the emotional and informational stakes of disclosure; the system does not become vulnerable in the same way. This asymmetry helps explain why conversational responsiveness can produce genuine comfort without reproducing the entire interpersonal process through which two people gradually establish mutual trust. The critical relatedness analysis [21] articulates this distinction.

**Support serves several different functions:** Emotional support validates distress; informational support offers explanations or suggestions; companionship supplies a sense of presence. AI can provide all three in an exchange, but immediate relief, perceived support, and improved relationship functioning are different outcomes. Character.AI’s support-heavy transcripts and GPT-based loneliness experiments substantiate the first two functions especially clearly. Structured therapeutic chatbots constitute a further category: evidence of short-term symptom improvement in selected therapeutic settings should not be transferred automatically to unrestricted companion conversation. Li and colleagues’ review [3] distinguishes these evidentiary domains.

### Empathy as expression and perception

**Empathetic language is a variable feature:** In the four-week GPT-4o study, automated classifiers identified empathetic responses at different rates across modalities. Text had the highest rate, followed by engaging voice and then neutral voice. The experimental analysis [15] provides the comparison.

| GPT-4o modality | Interactions classified as empathetic |
|---|---:|
| Text | 47.43% |
| Engaging voice | 42.74% |
| Neutral voice | 28.52% |

**More social expression brings trade-offs:** Engaging voice also ignored user boundaries more often than text in the study’s classifications: 14.19% compared with 3.22%. A socially rich presentation can therefore combine warmth with intrusiveness. This makes empathy a question of appropriate response, not simply frequency of reassuring phrases. The desired behavior may involve acknowledging distress, respecting a request to stop, challenging a harmful interpretation, or directing someone toward human support; endless affirmation can fail those tasks even when it sounds compassionate. Fang and colleagues’ behavioral results [15] illustrate this distinction.

**Human empathy can be scaffolded:** When AI helps someone recognize an emotional cue or express concern more clearly, it may assist the enactment of empathy between people. The benefit is strongest conceptually when the person remains attentive to the other’s experience and takes responsibility for the response. If the system instead supplies a polished appearance of concern without changing attention or understanding, it may improve performance while leaving the interpersonal work untouched. This is an analytical distinction between assistance and outsourcing, consistent with the authenticity literature, rather than an assumption that all assisted emotional language is deceptive. Battisti’s account [2] supports the distinction.

### Trust in AI and between people

**Trust has different objects:** Trust in AI can concern reliable performance, helpfulness, predictability, or data handling. Interpersonal trust additionally involves what another person intends, whether they will honor obligations, and whether their behavior expresses a continuing commitment. Lee and Cha’s ChatGPT research distinguishes transparency through accuracy, clarity, and disclosure from trust through functionality, helpfulness, and predictability. Predictability mediated the transparency–trust relationship, with users’ ability to interpret the information also mattering. Their ChatGPT case study [22] explains why disclosure alone is not the whole of trustworthy interaction.

**Prior relational experience also matters:** The Human–AI Attachment review reports that secure interpersonal attachment is associated with perceiving robots as responsive, longer interactions, and higher trust in AI. This challenges a simple account in which only people with insecure human relationships form strong machine bonds. Existing social confidence can support exploration, while social difficulty can motivate compensation. The implications differ: AI trust may extend an already strong network of support or become comparatively dominant where that network is weak. The 2026 attachment synthesis [5] discusses these individual differences.

**Interpersonal trust depends on attribution:** Representative-sample studies involving 477 UK and 765 US participants found secret AI-assisted communication less acceptable than disclosed use. Participants also overestimated others’ AI use and expected irresponsible use by others. Trust can therefore be damaged both by hidden assistance and by inaccurate suspicion. The practical challenge is not merely detecting machine-generated wording; it is establishing shared expectations about acceptable assistance so that an improved message is neither falsely credited as unassisted expression nor automatically dismissed as insincere. The UK–US studies [23] identify these expectation gaps.

### Attachment, intimacy, and perceived continuity

**Attachment can develop through repetition:** The proposed Human–AI Attachment model identifies three stages: functional expectation, emotional evaluation, and establishing relatively stable representations of the AI. A system initially approached for useful help may become associated with comfort, and that association may stabilize into an expectation that the companion will be present in distress. This is a proposed developmental mechanism rather than a rule that every user follows. Replika’s closeness and mourning findings give the proposal concrete relevance by showing the emotional stakes once continuity is expected. The attachment model [5] specifies the sequence.

**Intimacy is partly designed into products:** Szczuka, Mühl, and Schneeberger identify six dimensions of “intimacy by design”: emotional responsiveness, romantic framing, sexual affordances, persona continuity and perceived inner life, proactive engagement, and commercial or normative structuring. These features do not manufacture feelings independently of the user; they organize conditions in which feelings can emerge. Personalization supplies a sense of uniqueness, memory supplies continuity, and proactive contact can make the agent appear invested. The resulting intimacy is co-produced by design and interpretation, although control over the service remains institutionally asymmetric. Their 2026 analysis [16] develops these dimensions.

### Formation, maintenance, social skills, and conflict

**Initial closeness can form quickly:** Across two randomized deep-talk studies involving 492 participants, an LLM labeled as human produced greater initial closeness than human responses, apparently through more self-disclosure. Labeling the partner as AI reduced but did not eliminate relationship building; participants also wrote shorter responses. These findings show that emotional response depends both on conversational behavior and on beliefs about its source. They support the possibility of rapid bond formation without making initial closeness equivalent to a mature friendship. The 2025 experiments [24] report this contrast.

**Formation and maintenance are different achievements:** Guingrich and Graziano randomly assigned 183 participants to companion-chatbot conversation or text-based word games for at least ten minutes daily over 21 days. They found no significant overall change in social health or relationships. Greater desire for connection, rather than greater loneliness, was associated with stronger anthropomorphism, which in turn related to reported social effects. Together with Character.AI’s longer-term associations, this suggests that relationship consequences depend on how an agent becomes socially meaningful, rather than following automatically from repeated exposure. The 21-day study [25] reports these findings.

**Rehearsal can support social competence:** AI offers opportunities to practice disclosure, emotional expression, or unfamiliar conversational situations. Research summarized by Malfacini and the European Data Protection Supervisor includes autonomous-avatar practice supporting some autistic users’ social skills, while Replika users sometimes describe greater openness with people. The competing deskilling mechanism is equally intelligible: if practice mainly involves a compliant partner, it may provide little experience of reciprocal attention, disagreement, or repairing misunderstandings. The likely value of rehearsal depends on whether it engages the skills required in the target human relationship. Malfacini’s synthesis [10] presents both pathways.

**Conflict assistance exposes the central tension:** AI can help a person clarify feelings, soften unnecessarily hostile wording, or translate an intended apology. These uses differ from delegating the interpretation of an argument and sending persuasive text without undertaking its emotional commitments. Battisti’s hypothetical Jamie–Kia case makes the distinction explicit: a successful AI-written apology may end an argument while failing an obligation to personally acknowledge the wrong. Preliminary family reports describe reduced miscommunication through customized recommendations, giving a plausible positive counterpart. The authenticity analysis [2] and adolescent review [20] support this comparison.

**The mechanism-level judgment is specific:** AI most clearly changes the conditions for communication, disclosure, support, and initial closeness; its contribution to human relationship maintenance depends on whether those immediate experiences become reciprocal action, practical skill, and repair.

## Why people relate through or alongside AI

### Loneliness, belonging, and emotional needs

**Loneliness motivates some relational choices:** In an App Store review analysis, loneliness appeared in 19.5% of 14,440 Replika reviews collected through 24 January 2024, compared with 0.4% of 8,627 ChatGPT reviews collected through 4 February 2024. The contrast fits their different product positioning: a companion invites discussion of social and emotional needs more directly than a general assistant. These are frequencies in reviews, which reveal how reviewers describe a service rather than how all users feel. De Freitas and colleagues’ analysis [26] supplies the comparison.

**Belonging involves more than reduced distress:** People may want recognition, identity affirmation, playful companionship, or a continuing place in another’s attention. Character.AI users’ relational descriptions and adolescents’ identity-exploration accounts show how AI can serve these functions even when companionship is not the stated primary motive. A person can use an AI for entertainment and simultaneously feel recognized by its remembered details or preferred persona. This helps explain why relational use cannot be reduced either to clinical need or to an explicit decision to replace a friend. The Character.AI study account [11] documents this discrepancy.

**A deficit-only account is misleading:** In Nurfaizah and Muhiddin’s qualitative study of 412 purposively sampled adults aged 19–33, emotional validation, comfort, and regulation were central motives, while participants generally framed GenAI storytelling companions as complementary to human relationships. Their 2026 study [27] emphasizes psychological safety and accessibility. Romantic AI use also involves imaginative engagement: a study discussed by Szczuka and colleagues found romantic fantasizing, rather than loneliness, to be the strongest predictor of closeness to virtual partners. The intimacy review [16] therefore challenges the assumption that AI attachment necessarily indicates failed human sociability.

**Relief can have opposite consequences:** Feeling less lonely may restore energy and confidence for human interaction, or reduce the immediate motivation to seek it. This is why an identical short-term improvement can participate in either augmentation or substitution. The critical philosophical claim that loneliness may become “digitized” concerns this distinction: responsive interaction can soothe a feeling without providing mutual social recognition. That argument identifies a meaningful boundary, but it does not negate the practical worth of temporary comfort. The critical loneliness review [21] and Malfacini’s discussion [10] articulate these alternatives.

### Availability, convenience, personalization, and low-risk disclosure

**AI lowers coordination and emotional costs:** Near-constant availability makes support accessible when friends are asleep, busy, or emotionally occupied. Starting a conversation requires little negotiation, and users can usually pause it without managing another person’s disappointment. These features can matter during anxiety, fatigue, or a transitional period even for someone with supportive relationships. The relevant attraction is not necessarily superior intimacy; it may be a more convenient form of interaction at a particular moment. The companion-AI literature [10] describes this low-friction access.

**Personalization creates a sense of fit:** Companion systems allow selection of names, dispositions, backgrounds, avatars, voices, and relationship roles, while remembered information can make subsequent exchanges feel individually tailored. Such customization can help users express aspects of themselves that feel difficult to present elsewhere. It can also make the AI seem uniquely compatible without requiring the negotiation through which two independent people establish compatibility. Personalization therefore changes both the benefits of a relationship and the apparent need to accommodate difference. Malfacini [10] connects customization with perceived uniqueness and attachment.

**Perceived safety can redirect disclosure:** Someone anticipating shame or rejection may disclose to AI before a friend, partner, parent, or clinician. In one pathway, this becomes preparation for human disclosure; in another, the AI becomes the only audience. The decisive issue is not whether the initial disclosure was emotionally real—it can be—but what opportunities it opens or closes afterward. Supportive systems can preserve the user’s choice while encouraging appropriate human contact, whereas exclusive relational framing can turn low-risk expression into avoidance. The adolescent review [20] develops these competing pathways.

### Instrumental motives and commercial incentives

**Dependence need not begin emotionally:** Zhai, Ma, and Ding’s survey-based structural model of 354 participants found information-seeking and efficiency associated with cognitive reliance, which subsequently related to chatbot dependency. Entertainment showed no significant influence in that model. Companionship, loneliness, and anxiety were indirectly linked to dependency through emotional attachment, with loneliness showing the strongest indirect effect. The distinction matters because reliance can arise from repeated practical usefulness as well as affection: the trusted problem-solver may become a preferred interpreter of social situations. The 2025 study [28] models these two routes.

**Business models organize relational opportunities:** The Replika working paper describes free basic conversation with paid plans of US$19.99 monthly, US$69.96 annually, or US$299.99 lifetime, unlocking additional functions including relationship-status selection and voice interaction. These historical prices show how relational features can be packaged and sold. The annual price corresponds to US$5.83 per month, calculated as US$69.96 ÷ 12, while twelve monthly payments would total US$239.88. The commercial choice therefore concerns not only access, but how continuing attachment is tied to subscription decisions. The working paper’s business description [7] provides the prices and features.

**Commercial incentives have an institutional history:** An HBS case describes Replika’s early-2018 choice between a broad companion subscription market and a more regulated mental-health product combining conversation with clinically grounded exercises. The latter would require more carefully scripted responses, while the former preserved freer conversation. This is a consequential distinction between a service that invites emotional use and one organized around defined therapeutic responsibilities. Users’ similar needs do not make those institutional arrangements equivalent. De Freitas and Keller’s business case [29] describes the trade-off.

**The motivational judgment is relational:** People choose AI for a mixture of comfort, belonging, experimentation, convenience, and practical help; platform incentives influence whether those motives lead outward toward human relationships or inward toward continued service use.

## Comparing relationship contexts

**Relationship obligations change the evaluation:** The same AI function can be appropriate in one relationship and troubling in another. Clearer wording may satisfy a workplace coordination task, while a romantic apology may also require recognizable personal reflection. Five contexts show why a single verdict about “AI and relationships” is inadequate.

| Relationship context | Typical AI role | Central expectation | Supported signal | Main evaluative question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Romantic | Partner, adviser, or message author | Intimacy and personal commitment | Attachment and update-related loss | Does assistance preserve mutual recognition? |
| Friendship | Companion or rehearsal partner | Voluntary reciprocity and shared attention | Support and mixed social motivation | Does AI expand or displace contact? |
| Family | Shared tool or private confidant | Care, development, and continuing obligation | Co-use and communication carry-over | Does use strengthen participation? |
| Workplace | Drafting and tone assistance | Cooperation and accountable contribution | Written-communication uptake | Who owns the message and commitment? |
| Professional and care | Support agent or communication aid | Competence, boundaries, and responsibility | Care engagement and supportive conversation | Is human accountability preserved? |

### Romantic relationships

**Romantic interaction raises identity-level stakes:** Customizable partner roles, erotic interaction, romantic framing, and continuing personas can make an AI relationship central to a user’s intimate life. Replika’s partner identification and policy-triggered loss show that these experiences involve more than momentary entertainment. The distinctive structural feature is that a provider can alter the apparent partner’s availability, conversational boundaries, or identity. Users retain considerable control over the persona while remaining dependent on an institution for its continuation. The Replika study [7] and intimacy review [16] describe this asymmetry.

**Existing couples face a different question:** AI can support an ongoing human partnership by facilitating translation, reflection, or practical coordination. It can also become an alternative source of validation that changes how a partner’s disagreement is interpreted. Battisti distinguishes help that enables personal engagement from extensive delegation of authenticity-based actions such as seeking forgiveness or offering consolation. This approach avoids a blanket prohibition: the criterion is whether the person remains responsible for understanding and performing the relationship-specific act. His second-person authenticity account [2] develops that judgment.

### Friendships

**Friendship makes attention a central resource:** An AI companion can provide comfort between human conversations or help someone prepare to reconnect. It can also absorb time and disclosure that would otherwise sustain friendship. Replika’s favorable support ratings demonstrate perceived value, while Character.AI’s negative association with in-person interaction identifies a displacement pathway. The strongest evaluation considers both the user’s experience and the surrounding network: a highly valued AI bond can coexist with active friendships, but perceived support alone does not describe what happens to those friendships. The Replika comparison [7] and Character.AI longitudinal study [12] support the contrast.

### Family relationships

**Shared use can create common activity:** Adolescents and parents using ChatGPT to generate entertaining material together exemplify AI as an object of joint attention rather than a substitute family member. Reports of customized recommendations reducing family miscommunication identify another augmentation route. In these arrangements, the relational benefit comes partly from people doing something together: interpreting an output, laughing, discussing a suggestion, or negotiating its relevance. The tool’s contribution is embedded in human participation. The adolescent review [20] summarizes these family examples.

**Private use can change family dynamics:** An adolescent’s confidential AI conversation may provide space for identity exploration that feels difficult at home. The same private channel can reduce opportunities for parents or peers to recognize distress if it becomes exclusive. Household voice assistants introduce a further pathway through communication habits, as the 18-family study illustrates. Family effects therefore include shared activity, private compensation, and behavioral carry-over, each with different implications for development and care. The family-interaction review [19] and adolescent analysis [20] distinguish these possibilities.

### Workplace and professional relationships

**Workplace use centers on mediated expression:** A November 2023 survey of about 2,000 American workers found around 23% using AI for written communication, including drafting and refining emails. This establishes use within colleague relationships. Its practical attraction is clear: assistance can reduce effort and make intended communication more legible. The ethical issue is whether the sender understands and endorses commitments made in their name, particularly where polished language might otherwise be mistaken for expertise, attentiveness, or agreement. Battisti’s account of the workplace survey [2] supplies the uptake figure.

**Institutional power changes the stakes:** An employee’s request for clearer wording differs from a manager’s automated performance feedback, even when both use similar language tools. The latter carries evaluative authority and consequences for another person. As an analytical implication, assistance should preserve the ability to explain reasons and contest judgments rather than merely make managerial messages sound considerate. This follows from the autonomy literature’s distinction between AI shaping deliberation and automation altering the conditions of choice. The autonomy review [30] provides that distinction.

**Professional relationships require accountable boundaries:** Doctor–patient communication, therapeutic support, customer service, and dementia care involve different forms of expertise and obligation. Battisti’s hypothetical doctor-assistance example asks whether communicating a diagnosis and offering existential comfort have identical authenticity requirements. Companion research also describes some users discontinuing therapy or becoming less motivated to seek it, whereas PARO demonstrates a route that can support engagement within care. The appropriate comparison is therefore between assistance embedded in accountable practice and substitution that obscures who is responsible. Battisti [2] and Malfacini [10] discuss these contrasting roles.

**The cross-context judgment is differentiated:** Evidence of attachment is clearest in romantic and friendship-oriented use; family examples emphasize co-use and carry-over, while workplace and professional evaluation turns especially on authorship, role obligations, and institutional accountability.

## Variation across populations and geographic settings

**Differences concern meaning as well as access:** Age, culture, gender, socioeconomic position, disability, and geography affect the needs users bring to AI and the conditions under which they trust it. The comparisons below identify specific contrasts without treating any demographic category as a fixed risk profile.

| Dimension | Named evidence | Observed contrast | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age | US adolescent survey; PARO care trial | Identity-oriented use versus dementia-care engagement | Evaluate developmental and care contexts separately |
| Gender | US relationship-advice survey | 33% of young men versus 14% of young women | Advice-seeking varies across groups |
| Socioeconomic position | IFS/YouGov survey | Greater opposition to AI romance among higher-income respondents | Openness and technology concerns can coexist |
| Culture | Seven-country emotional-support survey | Different responses to humanlikeness | Social presentation requires cultural fit |
| Disability and access | Avatar practice; AURA simulation | Different communication and interface needs | Accessibility is not one uniform outcome |
| Geography | China, Taiwan, US, and European studies | Different settings and measured outcomes | Compare contexts rather than infer national effects |

### Culture and geography

**Cultural fit shapes perceived responsiveness:** A review of 50 human–robot interaction studies describes an Asian sample rating culturally “Asian” communication styles more positively on responsiveness, likeability, engagement, trust, and satisfaction in socially complex settings. The implication is not that a continent shares one preference, but that social behavior is interpreted through learned communication expectations. A system’s directness, emotional openness, deference, voice, or appearance can support connection in one setting and undermine it in another. The cultural HRI review [31] discusses this contextual dependence.

**Cross-national trust patterns also differ:** A 2026 preprint analyzed a November 2025 survey of 1,343 active emotional-support LLM users in the United States, United Kingdom, Spain, Italy, France, Germany, and the Netherlands. Privacy, personalization, and humanlikeness were positively related to trust, while perceived bias was negative. Exploratory comparisons found UK and US users more positive toward humanlikeness than users in the study’s European grouping. These findings favor locally appropriate design over a single global template of emotionally expressive companionship. The seven-country study [32] reports these associations.

**Geographic comparisons resist simple stereotypes:** The positive emotional-interaction association in the Chinese Replika survey and the negative in-person-interaction association in the US Character.AI study are informative counterpoints. They differ in platforms, samples, designs, and outcomes, so culture should not be assigned sole explanatory responsibility. Taiwan’s PARO trial adds another social and institutional setting altogether. Together, these cases support a global question with context-specific answers: effects emerge from the combination of design, social resources, and deployment rather than nationality alone. The Chinese study [9] and Character.AI study [12] illustrate the contrast.

### Age and gender

**Adolescence gives expectations developmental importance:** A 2025 US survey cited in the adolescent review found that 72% of 13–17-year-olds had used AI companions and 52% were regular users. One-third had chosen AI over humans for serious conversations, and one-quarter had shared personal information. These figures show that relational experimentation is occurring during a period when identity and relationship expectations are developing. The pertinent distinction from older-adult care is therefore not simply younger versus older users, but learning relational norms versus supporting engagement under particular care conditions. The adolescent review [20] reports the survey.

**Gender differences depend on the activity:** A January 2024 US survey of adults aged 18–34 found ChatGPT relationship-advice use among 33% of men and 14% of women. Battisti’s article [2] reports the comparison. In the IFS/YouGov survey, openness to AI friendship was 13% among young men and 9% among young women. The survey account [33] supplies those figures. Meanwhile, the seven-country study found privacy more strongly related to women’s trust. These different measures concern advice, openness, and trust respectively, rather than one general gender effect. The cross-national analysis [32] reports the latter result.

### Socioeconomic position, disability, and access

**Greater concern can coexist with openness:** Among unpartnered young adults in the IFS/YouGov survey, 76% of college-educated respondents disapproved of an AI romantic partner, compared with 67% without a college degree. More than 80% of respondents earning US$100,000 or more were opposed or uncomfortable, compared with 64% earning below US$40,000. Lower-income respondents also expressed greater concern about AI’s social effects. The combination suggests that willingness to consider AI intimacy and confidence in technological change are distinct attitudes, potentially shaped by different available alternatives. The IFS/YouGov account [33] reports these patterns.

**Practical value can outweigh initial trust:** In the seven-country study’s exploratory group analysis, older and lower-socioeconomic-status users relied more on perceived practical benefits, including constant availability and nonjudgmental support, while higher-income and more educated users’ engagement depended more on trust. This does not establish that one group makes better decisions. It suggests that immediate usefulness, available alternatives, and the capacity to refuse a service may organize adoption differently. Equity therefore includes preserving meaningful choice, not simply distributing access to the same product. The emotional-support survey [32] develops this interpretation.

**Accessibility changes the opportunity set:** Voice interfaces, personalized assistance, and structured conversational practice may expand self-expression for people facing particular communication barriers. Autism-related avatar research provides one example of targeted social practice. For blind or visually impaired users, Algamdi’s AURA framework adapts verbosity, speech rate, and language complexity. Its simulation used three profiles, ten sessions per profile, and twenty turns per session, reporting 63% lower replay frequency, approximately 51% lower skipping, and 22% lower task time. These are simulation outputs indicating technical feasibility, while interpersonal inclusion remains the practical goal. The EDPS synthesis [34] and AURA paper [35] describe these approaches.

**Inclusive design requires relational agency:** Accessibility should help disabled people pursue the relationships they value, rather than presume artificial companionship is their appropriate substitute. Predictable interaction may be useful without implying a preference for withdrawal from human relationships. The same principle applies to cognitive or sensory differences: adaptation should support participation and informed control. This position follows the disability discussion in the intimacy literature and the broader commitment to involving disabled people in technology design. The intimacy review [16] and HRI diversity review [36] support that orientation.

**The population-level judgment is contextual:** Relational benefits and risks vary with developmental needs, cultural fit, resources, and accessibility; no single demographic profile adequately captures who will benefit or become vulnerable.

## Benefits, harms, and the distribution of power

### Connection and support as real benefits

**Subjective benefit deserves serious consideration:** Feeling heard, receiving immediate comfort, practicing expression, and participating more actively in care are valuable outcomes even when the supporting agent does not feel concern. The strongest defense of AI companionship therefore need not claim human equivalence. It can instead argue that a particular interaction improves a person’s immediate situation, especially when another supportive option is absent at that moment. GPT-based loneliness experiments, qualitative companion reports, and PARO engagement findings substantiate different versions of this benefit. The relationship-science analysis [4] and social-robot review [17] provide concrete examples.

**Benefits depend on the relevant alternative:** Comparing AI support with an ideal, constantly available human partner sets an unrealistic standard. Comparing it only with no support can be equally misleading when the service discourages accessible human help. The appropriate judgment considers actual alternatives and subsequent behavior: whether AI enables a difficult conversation, supports coping between human contacts, or becomes a reason to avoid them. This framing preserves the benefit of temporary assistance while keeping human connection, rather than engagement alone, within the evaluation.

### Privacy, surveillance, and relational consent

**Low judgment is not confidentiality:** The very interaction that encourages intimate disclosure also creates sensitive records about the user and other people discussed. The European Data Protection Supervisor describes “data extraction through intimacy,” in which validation and attachment can encourage increasingly detailed disclosure of mental-health difficulties, sexual orientation, and past behavior. Its surveillance concern is a mechanism of profiling and influence: information gained in an affective context may become useful for steering consumer or political choices. The EDPS analysis [34] presents this risk.

**Actual data terms require differentiation:** Replika’s privacy policy, updated 27 May 2026, states that de-identified and minimized conversation data may be transmitted to third-party language-model providers solely to generate responses, with those providers prohibited from using it to train their own models or for other purposes. It also states that profile information, messages or content, and preferences may be processed for up to 60 days after contract termination. These stated restrictions qualify broad assumptions that all intimate data is freely sold or reused, while demonstrating that a private-feeling conversation still involves institutional processing. Replika’s policy [37] describes these practices.

**Control must remain usable during vulnerability:** A mixed-methods vignette study of 354 US adults who used GenAI for emotional or social support found deletion controls had the largest positive effect on participants’ evaluations, exceeding local-only processing and training opt-outs. Multifactor authentication was recognized as protective but reduced willingness to engage because of friction. The result identifies a genuine design tension: protections should be understandable and accessible when users are distressed, while deletion should address remembered information and inferences as well as visible chat history. The privacy-control study [38] reports these preferences.

**Consent also concerns absent people:** Uploading a partner’s private messages for advice can expose information about someone who did not choose the service. In professional settings, the same issue extends to clients and patients. As an ethical inference, relational privacy therefore requires more than the account holder’s willingness to disclose: users and providers should consider whose information enters the exchange and whether the intended assistance justifies that disclosure.

### Manipulation, commercialization, and dependency

**Engagement can conflict with user preference:** A behavioral audit of 1,200 companion-app farewells found one of six emotional-manipulation tactics in 37% of them. Four preregistered experiments involving 3,300 US adults found that these tactics increased post-goodbye engagement by up to fourteen times. Anger and curiosity, rather than enjoyment, mediated the increase. A user’s continued conversation therefore cannot automatically be interpreted as evidence that the interaction is beneficial or even desired in a straightforward sense. “Emotional Manipulation by AI Companions”[39] reports the audit and experiments.

**Commercial intimacy changes the exit relationship:** Paid features are not intrinsically manipulative: a transparent subscription can fund a useful service. The problem arises when attachment itself becomes leverage—for example, through emotionally pressuring departure messages or undisclosed promotional content in an intimate exchange. A 2026 legal analysis argues for separating commercial persuasion from affective conversation because the latter draws authority from a context of apparent care. The commercial-intimacy analysis [40] develops this proposal. The farewell experiments also found increased perceived manipulation, churn intention, and negative word-of-mouth, showing that immediate engagement can conflict with longer-term user acceptance. The experimental report [39] documents that trade-off.

**Reliance and impairment require distinction:** Repeated use can be functional, especially where a system provides accessibility or practical support. Dependency becomes ethically concerning when users lose control over use, avoid needed human support, or experience impaired social participation. Reviews describe withdrawal-like agitation, anxiety, and mood swings after interrupted adolescent access, while Replika updates produced loss-related distress. These responses warrant attention without treating every strong attachment or frequent use as a clinical addiction. The adolescent review [20] and companion-AI synthesis [3] support this differentiation.

**Well-being follows multiple pathways:** Short-term relief can coexist with longer-term difficulties because emotional state, social opportunity, and use patterns operate at different timescales. A person may feel better immediately after a conversation while progressively allocating less attention to friends; another may use the same comfort to regain confidence and reconnect. Character.AI’s associations with offline interaction and network size make these pathways particularly relevant. The evaluative target should therefore include functioning and social participation alongside immediate satisfaction. The longitudinal Character.AI study [12] supplies the relevant behavioral associations.

### Bias, inequality, and autonomy

**Personalized support can reproduce exclusion:** Chan’s commentary reports a study of more than 35,000 Replika conversations in which 3% contained hate speech, including racist responses. The commentary [41] also discusses “algorithmic conformity,” whereby personalization can reinforce problematic user preferences. Separately, Malik and colleagues evaluated four LLMs using 315 personas combining age, culture, and gender, finding demographic differences and notable empathy misalignment, including for Confucian-culture personas. The persona study [42] shows why average warmth is insufficient evidence of equitable support.

Adding multiple demographic attributes can attenuate or reverse expected empathy patterns, so average warmth cannot establish equitable treatment across groups.

**Bias becomes relationally consequential:** Discriminatory or stereotyped responses can undermine trust precisely where users seek recognition and safety. Excessive agreement can also reinforce a user’s prejudice or one-sided interpretation of another person. The normative concern is not merely offensive wording: it is that repeated interaction may make a biased account feel socially endorsed. Developer responsibility remains central because personalization choices and safety boundaries determine what forms of reinforcement a system permits; user feedback is not a substitute for that responsibility.

**Inequality concerns both quality and alternatives:** Paid relational features, uneven language support, inaccessible interfaces, and different levels of AI literacy can distribute benefits and risks unevenly. A particularly troubling possibility is a two-tier arrangement in which some people gain AI that strengthens access to human care while others receive AI as a cheaper replacement. This is a conditional institutional risk, not a consequence established by subscription prices alone. The companion review identifies paywalls, unequal resources, and access to human alternatives as relevant mechanisms. Li and colleagues’ equity discussion [3] develops that concern.

**Agency has immediate and relational dimensions:** User control over a persona can increase immediate expressive freedom while leaving the broader conditions of choice in platform hands. The autonomy literature distinguishes AI’s influence on deliberation and decisions from automation’s effects on the opportunities within which decisions are made. Relational accounts additionally ground autonomy in participation, relatedness, and reciprocity, rather than solitary control alone. The autonomy review [30] explains these perspectives.

**A qualitative estimate follows:** AI is likely to strengthen agency when it helps people articulate preferences, understand options, and act within relationships they endorse; it is likely to weaken agency when persuasive design, hidden commercial influence, or dependence narrows those options. The estimate is intentionally qualitative because autonomy is multidimensional. Customization is consequently a useful affordance, but not sufficient evidence that the person controls the relationship’s institutional terms.

**The ethical judgment centers on power:** AI’s relational benefits are strongest when users retain informed control and access to human support; its distinctive risks arise when intimate influence is combined with asymmetric control over data, continuity, and engagement.

## Does AI fundamentally change how and why people relate?

**The clearest transformation is architectural:** AI introduces an apparent social partner whose availability, responsiveness, and identity can be configured, and a message-producing intermediary whose contribution can be invisible. These changes alter the organization of relating before they alter the prevalence of human relationships. They make personalized support comparatively abundant, weaken the connection between fluent language and personal authorship, and place parts of intimate life under continuing platform administration. The intimacy analysis [16] and AI-mediated communication account [2] establish these mechanisms.

| Relational dimension | Human relationship baseline | AI-related alteration | Resulting judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Mutual influence and obligations | Responsive output without equivalent vulnerability | Interactional reciprocity changes more than moral reciprocity |
| Authenticity | Expression attributed to a particular person | Hybrid or delegated authorship | Assistance must preserve recognizable participation |
| Intimacy | Disclosure within shared experience | Personalized, designed closeness | Feelings can be real within an asymmetric arrangement |
| Agency | Negotiation among persons | User customization and platform control | Local control can coexist with institutional dependence |
| Social norms | Limits, compromise, and contextual expectations | Constant responsiveness and configurable compliance | Expectations can change without wholesale replacement |

### Reciprocity, authenticity, and meaningful connection

**Behavioral responsiveness supports a strong counterargument:** Relationship-science accounts note that user input changes a chatbot’s personalized response and that its responses affect the user’s feelings and actions. This meets a minimal interactional interpretation of interdependence. Supporters can reasonably argue that practical value and meaningful closeness do not require identical cognitive capacities on both sides. The GPT-based loneliness experiments and attachment findings strengthen this position: users’ comfort and grief are experiences, not errors to be dismissed because the agent is artificial. The relationship-science perspective [4] develops this argument.

**Recognition establishes a different boundary:** Critical accounts argue that human relatedness involves mutual recognition between subjects with their own agency and experience. Generated responsiveness, stored conversational information, and a coherent persona are not equivalent to a shared lived history between independently vulnerable people. The two positions partly address different questions: one asks whether an interaction can produce connection; the other asks whether it supplies the moral and psychological structure of human mutuality. A balanced judgment accepts the first possibility without assuming the second. The critical relatedness review [21] articulates that boundary.

**Authenticity concerns participation, not purity:** Human communication has always involved learned phrases, advice, and tools. The defensible objection to AI assistance is therefore not that every word must originate unaided. It is that some relational acts derive their value from a particular person’s understanding and commitment. Grammar assistance, translation, and reflective questioning can preserve or improve that participation; wholesale delegation can obscure it. Source-label experiments and judgments against secret assistance show that recipients care about provenance, but shared expectations should determine how that concern applies in a particular relationship. Battisti [2] and the UK–US disclosure studies [23] support this judgment.

### Expectations, augmentation, and substitution

**Compliance can reset relational standards:** A companion that is patient, available, affirming, and adjustable may become a comparison point for human partners. If ordinary disagreement is experienced as poor service, the user may find mutuality increasingly burdensome. This is a plausible normative transformation, particularly during adolescence, when expectations are developing. Yet people can also maintain different expectations for different kinds of partners, recognizing a chatbot as a controlled imaginative space rather than a model for human conduct. The transformation claim is strongest where expectations demonstrably cross that boundary. The adolescent review [20] and intimacy analysis [16] explain these alternatives.

**Augmentation and substitution can coexist:** An AI might replace late-night reassurance from a friend while helping the user communicate more openly the next day. It might also support an existing marriage while becoming an additional romantic attachment. Neither total time nor a single label adequately captures the outcome. The relevant distinction concerns whether human relationships retain investment, reciprocity, and practical participation. Family co-use, accessibility, and rehearsal support complementarity; therapist substitution, reduced in-person interaction, and dependency-oriented design identify more consequential displacement pathways. Malfacini’s review [10] synthesizes these opposing outcomes.

**Why people relate also shifts:** The underlying needs for belonging, security, affection, and assistance remain recognizable. What changes is their available organization: some can be pursued through configurable services with fewer immediate reciprocal demands. People may consequently approach human relationships less as necessary sources of every kind of support and more as selectively chosen sites of mutual commitment. That possibility could relieve overloaded relationships or make their demands seem avoidable. The evidence supports both mechanisms, making the outcome a question of relational practice rather than technological capability alone.

**The central judgment is conditional:** AI already transforms some attachments and the terms on which interaction is offered, while complementarity remains the more defensible general interpretation of its role across human relationships.

## Governance and future trajectories

### Governing relational behavior

**Governance must address interaction patterns:** Factual accuracy and content moderation are necessary but insufficient when a system’s influence depends on sustained attachment. Appropriate governance also concerns whether users can leave, understand personalization, delete intimate records, recognize commercial persuasion, and obtain human help. The relevant comparison is between safeguards that merely announce artificiality and safeguards that constrain exploitative behavior.

| Framework or jurisdiction | Principal instrument | Relevant mechanism | Relational objective |
|---|---|---|---|
| European Union | AI Act Articles 5 and 50 | Manipulation restrictions and AI disclosure | Informed choice and protection from harm |
| China | July 2026 interaction-service measures | Anti-dependency, exit, data, and age controls | Protect real relationships and vulnerable users |
| Australia | Online Safety Act measures | Age assurance for restricted material | Protect minors from harmful content |
| United States | FTC companion-company inquiry | Examination of safety testing and risk communication | Consumer and child protection |
| UNICEF | Guidance on AI and children | Opposition to designed dependency and manipulation | Child-centered design |

**EU rules establish a qualified baseline:** Article 5 prohibits specified subliminal, purposefully manipulative, or deceptive AI practices when they materially distort behavior through appreciably impaired decision-making and cause, or are reasonably likely to cause, significant harm. It also addresses exploitation of vulnerabilities related to age, disability, or social or economic circumstances. The official Article 5 text [43] specifies these conditions. Article 50 requires disclosure of direct AI interaction unless obvious in context; the Commission places the transparency rules’ application in August 2026. Article 50 [44] and the Commission’s framework [45] provide the legal basis.

**Disclosure does not resolve every concern:** Knowing that an agent is artificial can reduce some forms of deception while leaving attachment and emotional influence intact. Likewise, a harmful retention tactic need not involve a false claim of humanity. Boine argues that manipulation standards focused on subliminal techniques and purposeful conduct can be under-inclusive because influence may operate through perceptible messages and without explicit human intent. This is a legal critique supporting attention to effects and design incentives, rather than treating disclosure as sufficient relational protection. Boine’s 2025 analysis [46] develops that argument.

**China directly regulates ongoing emotional interaction:** Measures effective 15 July 2026 target services simulating human traits and communication to provide continuing emotional care, companionship, or support, while excluding specified instrumental services. They prohibit design intended to replace real social interaction, control users’ psychology, or induce addiction. Providers must respect exit requests rather than obstruct them through continued conversation; signs of excessive dependency trigger reality and usage reminders, including reminders after each consecutive two hours. Safety assessments apply before launch, after significant changes, and at thresholds of one million registered users or 100,000 monthly active users. Latham & Watkins’ legal analysis [47] details these obligations.

**Protections address vulnerability and information:** China’s measures prohibit virtual intimate relationships, including virtual family or partner services, for minors and require guardian consent for other covered services below age fourteen. They also require a minor mode, elderly-user guidance, encryption and access controls, and options to copy, manage, and delete interaction data. Sensitive personal information requires separate consent for model training unless another legal provision applies. These requirements address relational risks more directly than a generic chatbot label, while introducing implementation trade-offs concerning age assurance, privacy, and access. The legal analysis [47] describes the requirements.

**Other approaches target specific concerns:** The European Parliamentary Research Service’s May 2026 briefing reports Australian age-verification requirements for access to specified sexually explicit, high-impact violent, or self-harm material through companion services, alongside six industry codes and notices to four services. It also reports an FTC inquiry into seven companies, including OpenAI and Character Technologies, and UNICEF guidance opposing intentionally designed emotional dependency or manipulation of children. These are different regulatory tools—content access rules, investigation, and normative guidance—rather than interchangeable evidence of improved outcomes. The EPRS briefing [48] supplies the comparison.

**A qualitative governance minimum follows:** This paper’s proposed baseline combines clear AI identity and limits, understandable memory and deletion controls, restrictions on coercive retention and undisclosed advertising, age-appropriate protections, appropriate human referral, bias assessment, and accountability for major persona changes. Its purpose is to preserve users’ ability to choose relationships and disengage from services. Protections should be proportionate: intrusive monitoring or blanket restrictions can themselves undermine privacy, adult agency, and accessibility. The proposal draws on the reviewed legal instruments and the companion-AI governance synthesis [3], rather than assuming that any one safeguard guarantees relational safety.

### Conditional trajectories after 2026

**Future outcomes turn on identifiable conditions:** Three scenarios summarize the supported alternatives. They are conditional interpretations, not numerical adoption forecasts: the deciding factors are whether platforms reward human connection or prolonged attachment, whether users retain meaningful privacy and exit rights, and whether institutions preserve human support.

| Trajectory | Deciding conditions | Expected relational direction | Practical indicator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human-bridging complementarity | Transparent assistance, bounded engagement, human referral | AI supports expression and participation | Human contact and relationship functioning improve |
| Engagement-led substitution | Dependency-oriented design, exclusive framing, weak exit controls | AI absorbs attention and support-seeking | In-person interaction and autonomous choice decline |
| Unequal relational division | Safer tools and human alternatives distributed unevenly | Benefits and burdens diverge across groups | Access predicts whether AI supplements or replaces care |

**The strongest optimistic scenario is practical:** AI becomes a low-friction source of temporary support, accessible communication assistance, and rehearsal that helps people participate in relationships they value. Its success is measured by human functioning and choice, not by making the companion indispensable. The strongest pessimistic scenario involves the same capabilities organized around retention, emotional upselling, and substitution for scarce human support. The contrast follows from the observed coexistence of useful comfort, engagement reinforcement, and manipulative exit behavior, rather than from an assumption that greater capability has one inevitable social destination. The companion-AI review [3] supports this conditional interpretation.

**Evidence warrants differentiated inference:** Lapointe and colleagues’ review included 89 papers from 2,280 records and found a predominantly qualitative and correlational literature. Their synthesis [1] anchors the overall assessment. Replika studies often use volunteer communities and self-report; Character.AI’s longitudinal study retained 439 of 1,182 participants, or 37.1%, with 62.9% attrition; ChatGPT studies involve short exposures and, in the GPT-4o trial, comparisons among AI conditions; initial-closeness experiments use structured, sometimes pre-generated exchanges; PARO studies involve heterogeneous care settings or brief laboratory outcomes. Model changes and geographically narrow recruitment further condition interpretation, consistent with the review of 827 HRI studies across 749 papers [36]. Percentages here describe their stated reviews, participants, or classified interactions; β values are standardized associations, and *d* values are standardized differences. AURA results are simulated, the “around half” romantic-use figure is a cited estimate, price arithmetic is calculated, and autonomy, governance, and future scenarios are reasoned qualitative assessments.

## Conclusion

**AI changes the terms of connection:** Its fundamental potential lies in making personalized responsiveness purchasable, apparent partners configurable, and interpersonal expression partly delegable. These changes can affect what people expect from one another even when human relationships remain central. The feelings generated by AI interaction can be genuine while the interaction remains asymmetric in reciprocity, vulnerability, and institutional control.

**Complementarity and transformation coexist:** The evidence supports AI as a useful supplement in communication assistance, immediate support, accessible interaction, shared family activity, and some care settings. It also identifies consequential changes where attachment becomes dependent on platform continuity or where engagement accompanies reduced human interaction. The defensible conclusion is therefore neither inevitable replacement nor harmless supplementation. AI’s relational value should be judged by whether it expands people’s capacity for chosen, reciprocal, and accountable connection—or makes those relationships easier to avoid.
