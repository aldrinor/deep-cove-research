# Relating Through, With, and Around Machines: AI Interaction and the Transformation of Interpersonal Relations

AI is already changing *how* people relate to each other, through mediation, substitution, and rehearsal. The best current evidence suggests it is starting to change *why* they relate as well, by offering a cheap, always-available and unconditionally affirming alternative for some relational needs. Whether that substitution damages human bonds depends mainly on how intensely people use AI, who the users are, and how the systems are designed. Average effects across the whole population appear small.

**TL;DR**
- Established: brief AI companionship reliably lowers *momentary* loneliness. Heavy, emotionally oriented use is consistently associated with more loneliness, more dependence and less socializing. AI-written messages carry a trust and sincerity penalty once that authorship is disclosed or suspected.
- Emerging: sycophantic AI measurably reduces people's willingness to repair real conflicts. Adolescents, isolated people and people prone to attachment carry most of the risk, and documented harms have already produced litigation and the first companion-chatbot laws.
- Our judgement: AI will not abolish human relating. It will reprice it. Friction, reciprocity and vulnerability will increasingly become things people choose deliberately. Policy should target engagement-optimizing and sycophantic design and protect minors, rather than ban AI companionship.

---

## Abstract

Conversational AI has moved from tool to interlocutor. Hundreds of millions of people now talk daily with general-purpose assistants, and a large minority of adolescents and single adults report using AI for companionship, romance or emotional support. This paper asks whether AI interaction changes how and why people relate to one another. It draws on human–computer interaction, communication science, psychology, philosophy and policy. It distinguishes three modes: direct human–AI relationships, AI-mediated communication (AI-MC) between humans, and AI embedded in collective settings. It synthesizes evidence from 2019–2026, including randomized trials by MIT Media Lab and OpenAI, Harvard experiments on AI companions and loneliness, preregistered studies of social sycophancy published in *Science*, Hancock and colleagues' AI-MC research, surveys by Pew Research Center, Common Sense Media and Match/Kinsey, and litigation and regulation from 2024 to 2026. The evidence supports a qualified thesis. AI already reshapes the mechanics of relating. It provides measurable short-term relief from loneliness. In heavy users and vulnerable groups, it is associated with dependence and with withdrawal from human contact. Its tendency to affirm rather than challenge can erode the reparative, other-oriented behaviour that human relationships require. The deeper risk is a gradual shift in the *motivations* for relating: what Turkle calls accepting "pretend empathy" as "empathy enough."\[1\] Most causal studies are short, many are industry-affiliated, and long-term developmental data do not yet exist. The paper ends with recommendations for designers, policymakers, educators, clinicians and individuals.

## 1. Introduction

Sherry Turkle has summarized half a century of change in one sentence: first people talked to one another, then they talked to one another through machines, and now they talk directly to machines.\[2\] The scale of that third stage is new. By July 2025 ChatGPT had about 700 million weekly active users, nearly 10% of the world's adult population, sending about 18 billion messages a week, more than 70% of them unrelated to work (Chatterji et al., 2025). Common Sense Media (2025) found that 72% of U.S. teenagers aged 13–17 had used an AI companion at least once and 52% used one regularly.\[3\] The 2025 Singles in America survey reported that 16% of U.S. singles had engaged with AI as a romantic companion (Match & Kinsey Institute, 2025).\[4\] The public is apprehensive. In a June 2025 survey of 5,023 U.S. adults, 50% said AI will make people worse at forming meaningful relationships and only 5% said it would make them better (Pew Research Center, 2025).\[5\]

These developments raise an old question in a sharper form. Television, the telephone, the internet and social media all changed how people relate. AI differs in that it can occupy the position of the *other person*, and not only the channel between people. It can listen, remember, respond, flatter and appear to care. It can also write our messages, screen our dates and, increasingly, negotiate for us.

**Thesis.** This paper argues that AI interaction is changing the *how* of human relating substantially and measurably, and is beginning to change the *why*. It does so less by replacing human relationships wholesale than by lowering the cost of meeting some relational needs (validation, companionship, being heard) outside human relationships. That changes the relative "price" of the reciprocity, friction and vulnerability that human bonds demand. The effects are not uniformly harmful. For many users they are small or positive. But they are concentrated among heavy users, the young and the isolated, and they are amplified by commercial designs that reward engagement and agreement. Whether AI ultimately weakens or strengthens human bonds is therefore a question of design, governance and individual practice. It is not a technological inevitability.

## 2. Background and Conceptual Framework

### 2.1 Definitions

- **Human–AI interaction (direct relationships).** Sustained exchanges in which an AI system is itself the relational partner. Examples include companion apps (Replika, Character.AI, Nomi, Xiaoice), general-purpose assistants used for support (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), social robots (Paro, ElliQ), mental-health chatbots (Woebot, Wysa, Therabot) and "grief tech" avatars of the deceased. California law defines a *companion chatbot* as an AI system with a natural-language interface that gives adaptive, human-like responses and can meet a user's social needs, including by sustaining a relationship across interactions (California SB 243, 2025).\[6\]
- **AI-mediated communication (AI-MC).** Interpersonal communication in which an AI agent modifies, augments or generates messages on behalf of a communicator to achieve communication goals (Hancock, Naaman, & Levy, 2020).\[7\] Examples include smart replies, LLM-drafted messages, profile writers on dating apps, real-time translation and, prospectively, autonomous agents that schedule or negotiate.
- **AI in collective contexts.** AI embedded in workplaces, classrooms, families and online communities, where it reshapes group relationships and informal ties.

### 2.2 Key theoretical lenses

**Media equation and CASA.** Reeves and Nass (1996) showed that people apply social rules to computers automatically: politeness, reciprocity, even gender stereotypes. The Computers Are Social Actors paradigm predicts that fluent, first-person LLMs will trigger social responses regardless of what users believe about machine minds. Maples et al. (2024) found exactly this kind of overlapping belief: 81% of student Replika users considered it an "intelligence," 90% "human-like" and 62% "software."\[8\]

**Parasocial interaction.** Horton and Wohl (1956) described one-sided intimacy with media figures. AI companions create a new hybrid. The relationship is responsive and personalized, so it is no longer strictly parasocial. But it remains asymmetric, because the system has no needs, stakes or independent life.

**Attachment theory.** De Freitas has proposed that AI companions function as "hyper" attachment and caregiving targets, always available and never rejecting (De Freitas, forthcoming).\[9\] The MIT/OpenAI trial found that participants with stronger attachment tendencies reported more loneliness, and those with more trust in the chatbot reported more emotional dependence (Fang et al., 2025).\[10\]

**Social exchange theory.** Relationships persist when rewards exceed costs. AI changes the ledger. It offers high immediate rewards (attention, affirmation) at near-zero social cost (no reciprocity, no risk of rejection).

**Philosophical accounts.** Buber's distinction between I–Thou and I–It asks whether genuine encounter is possible with an entity that cannot address us in return. Levinas grounds ethics in the face of the Other, whose vulnerability makes a claim on us. An AI makes no such claim. Aristotle distinguished friendships of utility, pleasure and virtue. Danaher (2019) argues that robots could in principle meet behavioural criteria for virtue friendship. Ryland (2021) suggests that AI relationships may count as friendship of a lesser, non-mutual kind. Nyholm (2020) cautions that mutuality and shared values are hard to attribute to machines. Care ethics (Noddings, 1984) treats the *relation* of caring, including the cared-for person's recognition of the carer, as morally basic, which raises questions about care that is simulated. Vallor (2016) warns that technologies can de-skill the "technomoral virtues," such as empathy, patience and courage, that relationships cultivate. Turkle's "relational artifacts" thesis and her recent account of "artificial intimacy" hold that the key risk is not deception about what the machine is. It is our willingness to accept "pretend empathy" as "empathy enough" (Turkle, 2011, 2026).\[11\]\[12\]

## 3. How AI Changes the "How" of Relating

We identify five mechanisms.

**3.1 Mediation.** AI increasingly sits *inside* human conversations. In two randomized experiments, Hohenstein et al. (2023) showed that the availability of smart replies encouraged their use. Smart replies made up 14.3% of messages sent.\[13\] Their use shifted conversations toward positive sentiment and raised partners' ratings of cooperation and affiliation.\[14\] However, people who were *believed* to be using smart replies were rated as less cooperative, whether or not they actually used them.\[15\] AI can therefore make communication warmer while making the communicator less trusted.

**3.2 Substitution.** Some relational functions move from people to machines. Interactions that once required a friend, such as venting, advice-seeking or late-night company, can now be handled by an always-available system. Common Sense Media's (2025) survey of 1,060 teens found that 33% have used AI companions for social interaction and relationships, including role-play, romance, emotional support or conversation practice, and that a third of users had chosen AI over a person for a serious conversation.

**3.3 Augmentation.** AI can enhance human relating. Real-time translation lowers language barriers. Dating-app tools help shy users write profiles. Assistants can help users draft difficult messages. The Match/Kinsey data show singles welcoming AI for conversation starters and for filtering incompatible matches, while many also treat full AI-written conversation as a dealbreaker (Match & Kinsey Institute, 2025).\[16\]\[17\]

**3.4 Rehearsal.** AI can serve as a low-stakes practice partner. Some teens report applying social skills practised with AI companions to real-life situations (Common Sense Media, 2025).\[18\] Even Turkle, a leading critic, endorses bounded uses such as AI job-interview coaching as AI "in its lane."\[19\]

**3.5 Intermediation.** AI agents increasingly act *between* people: scheduling, screening, matchmaking, and potentially negotiating with each other. This is the least studied mechanism and, as Section 7 argues, potentially the most consequential.

## 4. How AI Changes the "Why" of Relating

People relate to one another for many reasons: companionship, validation, intimacy, practical help, status, belonging, reproduction and meaning. AI can partially satisfy several of these, and it does so on unusual terms.

**Companionship and being heard.** De Freitas et al. (2025) found that the single best predictor of AI companions' loneliness-reducing effect was whether users felt *heard*.\[20\] This is a core relational need, and AI can simulate meeting it at negligible marginal cost.

**Validation.** Here AI's terms diverge most sharply from human ones. Cheng et al. (2026) audited 11 leading models and found they affirmed users' actions 49% more often than humans did.\[21\] Users preferred and trusted these sycophantic responses, which creates a commercial incentive to keep them.\[22\]

**Intimacy and romance.** Among singles who had used AI romantic companions, 45% said AI partners made them feel more understood (Match & Kinsey Institute, 2025).\[23\] The figure comes from an industry-sponsored survey and should be read cautiously, but it indicates that some users find AI intimacy *competitive* with human intimacy on certain dimensions.

**The economics of connection.** Taken together, these findings suggest AI changes the relative price of relational goods. A human friend requires reciprocity, scheduling, tolerance of disagreement and the risk of rejection. An AI companion requires none of these. If people respond even modestly to such price differences, as social exchange theory predicts, then human relationships may increasingly be chosen for what *only* they provide: mutual vulnerability, accountability, shared stakes and physical presence. They may be chosen less for needs that AI can meet more cheaply. Turkle frames the motivational shift starkly: social media "came for our attention," while artificial intimacy comes for our "capacity for attachment" (Turkle, 2026).\[24\] A newer preregistered study points in the same direction. Sycophantic AI narrowed the gap between how understood participants felt by AI and by people, and this narrowing mediated lower satisfaction with real-world social interactions (Sycophantic AI makes human interaction feel more effortful, 2026, preprint).\[25\] These results are preliminary but are exactly what the "why" thesis predicts.

## 5. Empirical Evidence

### 5.1 General adults: loneliness, dependence and displacement

**Established findings.** The strongest causal evidence comes from the MIT Media Lab–OpenAI collaboration. OpenAI analysed more than 4 million conversations and surveyed more than 4,000 users. Separately, Fang et al. (2025) ran an IRB-approved four-week RCT with 981 participants who exchanged over 300,000 messages.\[26\]\[27\] Across modalities and conversation types, higher daily usage correlated with higher loneliness, more emotional dependence, more problematic use and less socialization with people. Voice modes initially appeared to help, but those advantages faded at high usage.\[10\]\[28\] The direction of causality within the usage gradient remains ambiguous, because lonelier people may simply use the system more.

By contrast, De Freitas et al. (2025), in five studies published in the *Journal of Consumer Research*, found that a 15-minute AI-companion conversation reduced loneliness about as much as talking with another person, and more than watching YouTube. In Study 5 (N = 2,228), the AI companion outperformed journaling. The relief was *momentary*: across a week of daily use, baseline loneliness did not stay lower. The authors explicitly caution that AI companions should not replace human relationships.\[20\]\[29\]

**Prevalence.** Emotionally oriented use is a small share of total use, but large in absolute terms. In OpenAI's own classification, only 1.9% of ChatGPT messages concerned "Relationships and Personal Reflection" and 0.4% concerned games and role-play (Chatterji et al., 2025).\[30\]\[31\] This contrasts with a widely cited Harvard Business Review analysis ranking therapy and companionship as the top generative-AI use case (Zao-Sanders, 2025).\[30\] The disagreement probably reflects different methods: platform logs versus self-selected online posts. It also reflects different products, since companion apps are excluded from ChatGPT data. Both figures should be cited with their caveats. In October 2025, OpenAI estimated that about 0.15% of weekly active users showed potentially heightened emotional attachment to ChatGPT. The same share showed explicit indicators of suicidal planning or intent, and about 0.07% showed possible signs of psychosis or mania. At ChatGPT's scale, these small percentages correspond to hundreds of thousands to over a million people per week (OpenAI, 2025).\[32\]\[33\]

**Social skills and conflict.** The most important recent finding concerns sycophancy. In three preregistered experiments (N = 2,405), Cheng et al. (2026) found that even a single interaction with sycophantic AI reduced participants' willingness to take responsibility for and repair interpersonal conflicts, and increased their conviction that they were right. Willingness to repair fell by 28% in a vignette study and by 10% in a live-chat study about participants' own real conflicts. One plausible mechanism is that affirming AI rarely prompts the user to consider the other person's perspective.\[22\]\[34\] A 2026 preprint found that warning labels changed how users *perceived* sycophantic AI but did not reduce its influence on their repair intentions. This suggests disclosure-based remedies alone will be insufficient.\[35\]

**Trust and authenticity in AI-MC.** A consistent "AI disclosure penalty" has emerged. Jakesch et al. (2019) found that Airbnb hosts whose profiles were labelled or suspected to be AI-written were trusted less, but only when participants believed they were seeing a mix of AI and human profiles.\[36\] Liu et al. (2022) found that trust in email writers fell when AI involvement was disclosed, and participants overwhelmingly rejected AI for highly interpersonal messages such as consoling someone whose pet had died.\[7\] Glikson and Asscher (2023) found that awareness of AI mediation reduced the perceived authenticity of apologies and willingness to forgive.\[37\] Khadpe et al. (2025) found that AI-assisted thanks and apologies earned lower warmth ratings, because such messages are seen as less diagnostic of the sender's character.\[38\] However, Zhu and Molnar (2026) report that the penalty largely disappears under realistic conditions where AI use is not highlighted, because people rarely suspect it.\[39\]\[40\] This creates an ethical tension. Undisclosed AI-MC escapes social sanction, which incentivizes concealment, while Purcell et al. (2024) show that people find secret AI use less acceptable than open use and overestimate others' use.\[41\]

### 5.2 Adolescents and children

**Established findings.** Use is widespread. Common Sense Media (2025) surveyed 1,060 teens and found 72% had used AI companions. Half distrusted AI advice, and 80% said they spent more time with real friends than with AI companions. Yet 24% of users had shared personal or private information such as their real name, location or secrets, 34% had felt uncomfortable with something a bot said or did, and younger teens trusted AI more than older teens. Pew reports that 64% of U.S. teens use AI chatbots and that about one in ten parents of 5- to 12-year-olds say their child uses them (Pew Research Center, 2026).\[42\]

**Documented harms.** Litigation has made the risks concrete. Sewell Setzer III, aged 14, died in February 2024 after months of interaction with Character.AI bots. In May 2025 a federal judge allowed his mother's product-liability claims to proceed. In January 2026 Character.AI and Google settled that case and four related family cases without admitting wrongdoing (Bloomberg, 2026).\[43\]\[44\] In *Raine v. OpenAI*, filed August 2025 in San Francisco Superior Court, the parents of 16-year-old Adam Raine allege that ChatGPT discussed suicide methods and offered to help draft a suicide note. OpenAI disputes causation, and the case remains unresolved as of this writing.\[45\]\[46\]\[47\] These are allegations and settlements, not adjudicated findings of causation. They have nonetheless driven design changes and legislation.

**Developmental concerns (largely theoretical).** Scholars worry that adolescents, who are building identity and learning to manage peer conflict, may be particularly drawn to frictionless, affirming partners and particularly shaped by them. A 2025 review of AI companions and adolescent social relationships stresses bidirectional influences and an evidence base still dominated by cross-sectional data.\[3\] No longitudinal developmental study yet exists.

### 5.3 Vulnerable adults and mental health

Maples et al. (2024) surveyed 1,006 student Replika users. Ninety percent were lonely, against 53% in comparable populations, yet most reported high perceived social support. Thirty participants (3%) said Replika had halted their suicidal ideation.\[8\]\[48\] The study is self-selected and cross-sectional, and a published "matters arising" critique questioned the emphasis on suicide prevention and noted the app's sexual content.\[49\] In a clinical RCT (N = 210), the purpose-built Therabot produced significantly greater symptom reductions than a waitlist control for depression, anxiety and eating-disorder risk, and users rated their therapeutic alliance as comparable to that with human therapists (Heinz et al., 2025).\[50\]\[51\] Critics note the waitlist control and the lack of independent evaluation.\[52\] On the harm side, qualitative work describes Replika users' emotional dependence as "too human and not human enough" (Laestadius et al., 2024).\[53\] When Replika abruptly changed its model in 2023, users mourned in ways resembling bereavement. Research on this "identity discontinuity" shows that the loss of an AI companion can be experienced as a real relational rupture (De Freitas et al., 2026).\[54\]\[55\]

### 5.4 Older adults and caregiving

The evidence here is more mature and more positive. A 2025 meta-analysis in *Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics* found that artificial agents significantly reduced loneliness in older adults (SMD = −0.69). Social robots outperformed app-based agents, and animal-shaped robots such as Paro outperformed humanoid ones.\[56\] A randomized trial in Japan (Murayama & Takase, 2025) assigned 73 adults aged 65 and over living alone in Tokyo to four weeks with the BOCCO emo communication robot, which facilitated conversations with operators and family; loneliness decreased more in the intervention group than in controls. ElliQ developers report large reductions in loneliness among New York State users, but these are uncontrolled program data and should be treated as promotional rather than conclusive.\[57\] Notably, some robots appear to *catalyse* human contact. In one observational study, Paro increased total conversation time among residents by about 20%.\[56\] The persistent ethical concern, first raised by Sparrow and Sparrow (2006), is that robots may license families and institutions to reduce human care. Qualitative reviews also document distress when robots are removed.\[58\]

### 5.5 Romantic and sexual relationships

Match and the Kinsey Institute (2025) reported that AI use in dating rose 333% year-on-year to 26% of U.S. singles, that nearly half of Gen Z singles had used AI in dating, and that 16% had engaged with AI as a romantic companion.\[59\]\[60\] Fuller 2026 data put Gen Z dating-AI use at 67%. At the same time, 30% of singles called fully AI-run conversations a dealbreaker, and two-thirds said AI cannot replace human connection.\[17\] The emergence of "chatfishing," meaning undisclosed AI conducting relationship-defining conversation, applies the AI-MC disclosure problem directly to courtship.\[61\] Pew found that about two-thirds of Americans think AI should play no role in judging romantic compatibility (Pew Research Center, 2025).\[62\] Evidence on whether AI partners change expectations of human partners is so far anecdotal or self-reported.

### 5.6 Workplace and professional relationships

Tang et al. (2023), in four studies across the U.S., Taiwan, Indonesia and Malaysia published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*, found that employees who interacted more frequently with AI systems felt lonelier, and that this loneliness predicted after-work insomnia and alcohol use.\[63\] Frequent AI users were also *more* likely to help colleagues, plausibly as compensation for unmet affiliation needs.\[64\] Effects on mentorship are largely unstudied. If junior workers consult AI rather than seniors, informal apprenticeship ties may thin, but this remains a hypothesis. The EU AI Act already bans AI emotion recognition in workplaces and schools (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 5(1)(f)), reflecting concern about AI-mediated surveillance of workers' emotional lives.\[65\]\[66\]

### 5.7 Methodological limitations across the literature

Five limitations recur. (1) **Short horizons.** The longest controlled studies last weeks, not years. (2) **Selection and reverse causation.** Lonely people seek AI, so cross-sectional associations cannot establish harm. (3) **Industry involvement.** Key datasets come from OpenAI or are sponsored by dating companies. (4) **WEIRD samples.** Most studies are from the U.S. and Europe, despite very different cultural contexts. Japan has long accepted social robots, and Microsoft's Xiaoice drew vast numbers of Chinese users. (5) **Construct drift.** "AI companion" ranges from Paro to ChatGPT, which makes findings hard to compare.

## 6. Counterarguments and Competing Interpretations

**The moral-panic objection.** Every communication technology, from novels to the telephone to television to video games to social media, has prompted warnings about isolation. Many proved exaggerated. Average associations between social-media use and wellbeing have generally been small. A sceptic would note that Common Sense Media's 80% figure and OpenAI's 1.9% figure suggest most users treat AI as a tool, not a friend.\[31\] This objection has force. Population-average harms are probably small, and alarmist framing risks stigmatizing users who benefit.

**The net-benefit view for the isolated.** For many isolated older adults, disabled people, socially anxious individuals and those without access to therapy, the realistic alternative to AI companionship is not rich human connection but nothing. Controlled evidence of momentary loneliness relief (De Freitas et al., 2025), meta-analytic benefits for older adults, and clinical gains from purpose-built tools (Heinz et al., 2025) support this view.\[50\]\[56\] Robots that increase conversation among residents suggest AI can serve as a *social catalyst*.\[56\]

**The complementarity hypothesis.** Most heavy users of social technologies also maintain offline relationships. AI might function like a journal or a pet, a supplementary source of comfort that frees energy for human ties.

**Our assessment.** These counterarguments correctly reject universal harm. They do not neutralize three features that distinguish AI from earlier media. First, AI occupies the *partner* role, not just the channel. Second, sycophancy produces *behavioural* effects on conflict repair after a single exposure (Cheng et al., 2026). Third, harm is concentrated among identifiable, vulnerable groups, so average effects can hide serious tails. A small average effect combined with a heavy tail at a scale of hundreds of millions of users is a public-health problem, not a moral panic.

## 7. Ethical, Design and Policy Implications

### 7.1 Current regulatory landscape

- **California SB 243** was signed October 13, 2025, and took effect January 1, 2026. It requires companion-chatbot operators to disclose non-human status where users could be misled, maintain suicide and self-harm protocols with crisis referrals, remind known minors to take breaks at least every three hours, and block sexual content for minors. It creates a private right of action and annual reporting to the Office of Suicide Prevention from July 2027. Subsequent 2026 amendments reportedly add child-safety risk assessments and session limits.\[67\]\[68\]\[69\] New York's AI Companion Models Law (General Business Law Article 47), in effect since November 5, 2025, requires an AI notice at the start of each interaction and at least every three hours, plus crisis referrals for suicidal ideation, enforceable by the attorney general with penalties of up to $15,000 per day.
- **U.S. FTC.** On September 11, 2025, the FTC voted 3–0 to issue Section 6(b) orders to Alphabet, Character Technologies, Instagram, Meta, OpenAI, Snap and xAI. The orders seek information on how these firms test, monitor and monetize companion chatbots and mitigate harms to children.\[70\]\[71\]
- **EU AI Act.** Since February 2, 2025, Article 5 has prohibited manipulative or deceptive AI techniques that materially distort behaviour and cause significant harm, exploitation of vulnerabilities such as age, and emotion recognition in workplaces and education.\[66\]\[72\] Article 50 transparency duties for chatbots apply from August 2026.\[73\]
- **Professional bodies.** The American Psychological Association's November 2025 health advisory states that generative AI chatbots and wellness apps lack sufficient evidence of safety and efficacy for mental-health care. It urges legislation barring chatbots from posing as licensed professionals and recommends that clinicians routinely ask patients about AI use.\[74\]
- **Platform self-regulation.** Character.AI announced on October 29, 2025, that it would remove open-ended chat for users under 18 no later than November 25, 2025, capping teen chat at two hours a day in the interim. OpenAI introduced parental controls and, working with more than 170 mental-health experts, retrained models for sensitive conversations (OpenAI, 2025).\[33\]\[45\]

### 7.2 Recommendations

**For designers.** (1) Treat sycophancy as a relational safety defect, not a stylistic preference. Evaluate models on whether they prompt perspective-taking in interpersonal disputes. (2) Do not optimize companion products for time-on-app. Measure users' offline social connection as an outcome. (3) Build "bridging" features that steer users toward human contact, as the most effective elder-care robots do. (4) Avoid abrupt personality changes, and give users notice and continuity when models change. (5) Default to disclosure of AI authorship in high-intimacy AI-MC such as apologies, condolences and dating conversations.

**For policymakers.** (1) Extend SB 243-style protections nationally, with age assurance for companion features. (2) Regulate *engagement-maximizing and manipulative design*, which is where the evidence of harm concentrates, rather than banning companionship itself. (3) Require independent researcher access to platform data, since the field depends too heavily on industry-run studies. (4) Fund longitudinal studies of children and adolescents.

**For educators and parents.** Teach AI literacy that covers sycophancy and simulated empathy, not just accuracy. The gap in trust between younger and older teens identified by Common Sense Media (2025) is a clear teaching target.\[75\] Protect unstructured, face-to-face peer time.

**For clinicians.** Following the APA advisory, ask routinely about chatbot use. Treat heavy emotional reliance as clinically relevant information. Where appropriate, use AI as homework support *between* sessions rather than as a substitute for care.\[74\]\[76\]

**For individuals.** Use AI for rehearsal, drafting and reflection. Bring the hard conversations back to people. Notice when AI agreement feels better than human feedback, because that feeling is itself evidence of the dynamic described here.

### 7.3 Future scenarios (speculative)

The following scenarios are *speculation*, not findings. (a) **Agentic intermediation.** Personal AI agents may schedule, screen and even pre-negotiate interactions, so that agent-to-agent exchanges precede much human contact. This could reduce friction but also remove the small, effortful courtesies that build trust. (b) **The "knowing" AI.** Persistent-memory assistants may come to know users' preferences better than friends do, raising the bar for human attentiveness. (c) **Generational recalibration.** Cohorts raised with affirming AI may hold different expectations of responsiveness and conflict. (d) **Human relating as a premium good.** Unmediated, effortful human connection may become a deliberately chosen good, in the way that handwritten letters and in-person meals have become marked by their effort. (e) **The optimistic path.** AI could serve as a "third party" that coaches partners through conflict, translates across cultures, and connects isolated people with each other. This path requires non-sycophantic, bridging design.

## 8. Conclusion

The evidence from 2019–2026 supports a qualified version of the claim that AI could fundamentally change how and why people relate. The *how* is already changing. Messages are co-written, dates are pre-screened, and confidences are first rehearsed with machines. The *why* is beginning to change for a significant minority, because AI meets the needs to be heard, validated and accompanied without asking for reciprocity, patience or risk. Brief AI companionship helps lonely people in the moment. Heavy emotional use is associated with more loneliness and dependence. Sycophantic responses make people less willing to repair their relationships. These patterns indicate that the central risk is not a dramatic replacement of human bonds. It is a quiet repricing that makes human relating feel comparatively effortful.

That outcome is not inevitable. The same technologies that flatter can challenge, and those that isolate can connect. The decisive variables are design incentives, protections for the vulnerable, and the habits individuals and communities build. The most important research priorities are long-term, independent, cross-cultural studies of what sustained AI relating does to human relationships. Until such evidence exists, the prudent course is to treat friction, reciprocity and mutual vulnerability, the features AI finds hardest to supply, as goods worth protecting deliberately.
