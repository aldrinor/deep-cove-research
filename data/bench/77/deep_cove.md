# Need for Cognitive Closure and Misinformation Acceptance

Need for cognitive closure appears to be a small, context-sensitive risk factor for accepting some misinformation, especially conspiracy-related claims and misinformation introduced into memory. The clearest direct estimates are positive but modest: German trait closure correlated with COVID-19 conspiracy belief at *r* = 0.12, with an adjusted standardized coefficient of *B* = 0.08; eyewitness misinformation susceptibility correlated at *r* = 0.25. Other studies find null, negative, or dimension-specific results. Need for closure is therefore a conditional contributor, not an established cause or sufficient explanation, and the direct evidence does not establish an NCC-specific effect on sharing or belief persistence.

The likely route is that ambiguity, threat, time pressure, or costly processing makes a definite answer especially attractive. A person may then seize on an accessible source, familiar claim, or politically congruent explanation and freeze the judgment before checking alternatives. Whether that shortcut produces accurate acceptance or misinformation depends on the cue, the source, prior knowledge, ideology, analytic engagement, and timing.

This synthesis covers research through 13 September 2026. It first defines need for closure and the different meanings of “acceptance,” then compares direct findings across misinformation domains and outcomes. It next explains the proposed cognitive chain, examines moderators and causal evidence, and ends with practical implications for correction and uncertainty communication.

## What need for closure and misinformation acceptance mean

### Need for cognitive closure is a motivation, not a truth judgment

**The core construct.** Need for cognitive closure is the motivated desire for a definite answer instead of confusion and ambiguity. Webster and Kruglanski described it as wanting “an answer on a given topic, any answer,” which means that closure is about ending uncertainty, not about preferring a particular false explanation. The construct therefore should not be equated with certainty itself, dogmatism, low intelligence, or a general inability to reason. [1]

A person high in closure may accept a conspiracy theory when it is the most accessible simple explanation, but the same motive may support acceptance of a trusted official account when that account is readily available and credible. The theoretical model describes two linked processes: “seizing,” or quickly adopting information that appears to satisfy the need for an answer, and “freezing,” or maintaining the judgment so that uncertainty does not reopen. [2]

**Trait and state differ.** Trait or dispositional need for closure is a relatively stable individual difference. The original Need for Closure Scale used 42 items on a six-point response scale and represented closure through preferences for order, predictability, decisiveness, structure, and reduced ambiguity. One validation study reported test–retest reliability of *r* = 0.8611 over 12–13 weeks. Shorter instruments are now common: the German misinformation study used a five-item, one-to-five NFCS-5 with α = 0.67; the Polish study used 15 items; and an Argentine psychometric study reduced a revised measure to eight items organized around urgency and permanence. [1][3][4]

State need for closure is a temporary increase caused by circumstances. Time pressure, environmental noise, fatigue, cognitive load, or making additional processing costly can make ambiguity more aversive and encourage faster closure. Accountability can push in the opposite direction by making people anticipate having to justify their judgment. Classic closure experiments found less information search under closure-inducing conditions, but those tasks measured recognition, confidence, primacy, or anchoring rather than misinformation acceptance. [2]

Leman and Cinnirella’s conspiracy experiment illustrates the distinction without providing a perfect state-NCC test. Participants were randomly assigned to an accountability condition in which they expected to justify their judgment before a large audience. Lowered closure was associated with weaker effects of prior conspiracy beliefs on a novel event, but the study did not directly validate that the accountability manipulation changed participants’ NFC scores. [5]

**Related constructs are not interchangeable.** Intolerance of uncertainty or ambiguity overlaps with closure but captures a different psychological tendency. Dogmatism concerns rigidity of belief, whereas closure concerns the motivation to end uncertainty; a person can seek a definite answer without holding a rigid ideology. The distinction matters because a Polish study found that avoidance of ambiguity was positively related to conspiracy belief, while a separate misinformation paradigm found no link between intolerance of uncertainty and misinformation. Those findings do not support treating every discomfort with uncertainty as the same construct.

### Misinformation acceptance has several outcomes

**Belief is one outcome.** Belief acceptance usually means agreement with, or perceived likelihood of, a claim. In the German study, participants rated three COVID-19 conspiracy claims from one, “extremely unlikely,” to seven, “extremely likely.” In Poland, participants rated 30 COVID-19 facts, opinions, and conspiracy claims on a five-point agreement scale. In the United States, a study used ten World Health Organization myth-busting items and counted “probably true” or “definitely true” as endorsement. These measures capture reported claim acceptance, not necessarily durable belief or behavior. [3][6][7]

**Accuracy is different.** A perceived-accuracy judgment asks whether a headline is accurate, unbiased, or true. Accuracy can also be measured as discrimination: whether a person distinguishes true from false items, often with signal-detection sensitivity, *d′*. A participant may label a claim “probably false” without changing a broader belief, or may believe a claim while still deciding not to share it.

**Credibility concerns the source.** Credibility is perceived trustworthiness and expertise. It can function as a shortcut when readers lack time, motivation, or knowledge to assess the content itself. A credibility judgment can therefore influence acceptance without being identical to belief in the claim. A source-credibility review of 91 studies and 64,162 participants found source effects more consistently related to perceived accuracy than to sharing intentions, reinforcing the need to keep cognitive and behavioral outcomes separate. [8]

**Sharing is behavioral acceptance.** Sharing intention asks whether someone would consider posting or forwarding an item. Actual sharing records what a person does in a real or simulated platform. In a U.S. study of political headlines, participants were randomly assigned either to rate accuracy or to state whether they would share; the two outcomes were deliberately separated. The sharing measure was self-reported willingness, not observed behavior. [9]

**Persistence is delayed acceptance.** Belief persistence concerns whether a claim continues to influence judgment after time passes, competing information appears, or a correction is delivered. It is not equivalent to an immediate belief rating. Because fewer than 7% of misinformation experiments in a 2016–2022 scoping review included any delay between exposure and outcome measurement, persistence is substantially less studied than immediate acceptance. [10]

## What direct evidence shows

### The overall association is small and inconsistent

**The strongest population result is modest.** Jedinger and Masch used a probability-based German panel with 2,883 adults. Trait NCC was measured before the pandemic, from December 2018 to February 2019, and COVID-19 conspiracy belief was measured from August to October 2020. The bivariate association was *r* = 0.12; in a covariate-adjusted, design-weighted regression with 2,491 cases, the standardized NCC coefficient was *B* = 0.08, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.04 to 0.11. [3]

The outcome was belief in three claims: that the coronavirus was developed as a biological weapon, that it was being used to restrict civil rights and create surveillance, and that its danger and spread were deliberately exaggerated. The average belief score was 2.50 on a seven-point scale. Only 6.7% of participants scored in the very-likely range of six or seven, so NCC predicted movement within a generally skeptical population rather than determining who accepted the claims.

**Eyewitness evidence points in the same direction.** Hejniak and colleagues exposed 174 students and high-school pupils to a silent-film robbery, followed by a description containing six misleading details. Among participants exposed to misinformation, those classified as high in NFCC produced an average of 1.84 misinformation-consistent responses, compared with 1.36 among the low-NFCC group. The high-versus-low effect was small, η² = 0.03, and the continuous correlation between NFCC and susceptibility was *r* = 0.25. [11]

The experiment is useful because misinformation was introduced after an original event, allowing researchers to observe memory susceptibility rather than merely asking whether participants endorse a political narrative. Yet the NFCC measure was not experimentally manipulated, the high and low groups were created by a median split, and the sample was young and student-heavy. The result is therefore evidence for a small association in a specific memory context, not a general causal effect across online misinformation.

**Polish facets point in different directions.** A survey of 380 Polish adults measured five closure dimensions and belief in COVID-19 conspiracy claims. Structural equation modeling found a negative path from need for predictability to conspiracy belief, β = −0.14, a positive path from avoidance of ambiguity, β = 0.16, and a positive path from closed-mindedness, β = 0.10. The predictors explained 3% of the variance in the dependent outcome. [6]

This is not a minor statistical detail. It shows why a single total NCC score can conceal opposing processes. Wanting predictable information may sometimes orient people toward stable conventional accounts, while avoidance of ambiguity or closed-mindedness may make a simple conspiratorial account more attractive. The direction therefore depends partly on which component of closure is measured and what answer is available.

**Null findings remain important.** In Leman and Cinnirella’s preliminary study of 30 London undergraduates, NFCC correlated −0.05 with existing conspiracy beliefs and −0.05 with assigning a fictitious plane crash to a conspiracy. The authors’ second study found that lowering closure through accountability weakened the influence of prior conspiracy beliefs, but the small first study and the imperfect manipulation test do not support a uniform positive relationship. [5]

The 2024 German article’s review of earlier work likewise reports null findings from Imhoff and Bruder and from Leman and Cinnirella, a small negative association in Jones and colleagues, and small positive correlations in studies by dos Reis and Swami. In the dos Reis study, the closure association became nonsignificant after demographic and political adjustment. This spread from null to small negative and small positive results is why the overall relationship should be described as positive in some settings but inconsistent across studies. [3]

**The Argentine result adds a newer context.** Roca and colleagues studied 204 Argentine adults using a revised NCC test and a COVID-19 fake-news belief questionnaire. Correlational and regression analyses found a significant association between NCC and belief in COVID-19 fake news, especially the urgency dimension. The abstract-level report gives the sample size and significance pattern but no coefficient or effect size; it also does not supply a sharing estimate despite asking about willingness to share. [12]

A broader 2025 meta-analysis helps set the scale but should not be mistaken for an NCC meta-analysis. Across 971 effect sizes from 279 independent studies and 137,406 participants, broad epistemic motives correlated with conspiracy belief at *r* = 0.14, while existential and social motives each correlated at *r* = 0.16. The associations were heterogeneous and weaker in experimental than correlational designs. These values show that motives related to certainty may matter, but they do not isolate need for closure or generalize to sharing, credibility, or persistence. [13]

### The effect differs by domain and outcome

**Conspiracy-related claims have the clearest direct evidence.** The German, Polish, and Argentine studies all measured conspiracy-related or fake-news belief, and their estimates range from small positive effects to dimension-specific reversals. The German result is especially informative because it combines a large probability sample, temporal separation between predictor and outcome, and adjustment for demographic and political variables. Even there, however, NCC’s coefficient was much smaller than trust in institutions.

In the German regression, pre-pandemic political trust predicted lower conspiracy belief at *B* = −0.33, and concurrent trust in political and medical institutions predicted lower belief at *B* = −0.51. The NCC coefficient was *B* = 0.08. Political trust and crisis trust explained more of the model’s variance than closure. The practical judgment is therefore that NCC may make a person more receptive to a simple explanation when uncertainty is high, but institutional trust and political context can matter much more.

**Political misinformation is only partly characterized.** Pennycook and colleagues measured a 14-item NFCC scale in two U.S. samples totaling 1,973 participants who evaluated false, hyperpartisan, and true political headlines for accuracy or sharing. The reported headline analyses center on the Cognitive Reflection Test rather than an NFCC coefficient, so the study establishes that the construct was measured but does not quantify NCC’s political misinformation effect. [9]

The same study found that analytic thinking was generally associated with better discrimination and lower willingness to share false or hyperpartisan headlines, although effects were weaker and less consistent in the quota-sampled Lucid sample. This is relevant to the proposed mechanism but should not be relabeled as an NCC effect. Need for cognition, CRT performance, and NFCC are related but distinct constructs.

Political identity itself can alter misinformation judgments. A source-cue experiment found that liberal and conservative participants rated misinformation from politically congruent sources as more accurate, with the reported effects larger for liberals than conservatives. The dissertation containing these results also reports a conflicting directional summary elsewhere, so the numerical pattern should be treated as suggestive rather than definitive. Crucially, political congruence is not evidence that NCC moderates the effect. [14]

**Fake-news findings are promising but thin.** The Argentine study directly connects NCC with COVID-19 fake-news belief, particularly urgency, but supplies no effect size. It does not establish that people high in NCC are more willing to share the fake news, nor that they retain the belief after correction. “Fake news” itself is a narrower category than misinformation: it generally refers to fabricated content presented in a news-like format, whereas misinformation also includes misleading framing, selective omission, distorted statistics, and claims that mix true events with false interpretation. [9]

**Eyewitness misinformation may involve a different pathway.** In the Hejniak study, the misleading detail was recent and supplied by an apparently external source. The participant had to decide which information to use when reconstructing memory. That situation may favor a clear, externally supplied answer more directly than an ordinary social-media headline does. The study’s false-confession results also illustrate outcome dependence: although a chi-square association with coerced-compliant confession reached *V* = 0.19, the detailed high-versus-low comparison of who signed was nonsignificant, while internalization showed *V* = 0.22. A closure effect on one kind of acceptance should not automatically be generalized to another.

**Accuracy and credibility are suggestive rather than settled.** Kaufman, Haupt, and Dow found that participants high in NFCC were less aligned with experts when judging the truthfulness of COVID-19 tweets in a sample of 265 MTurk workers and students. The abstract gives no direct coefficient. Expert alignment is an accuracy-assessment outcome, however, not a direct measure of belief strength, credibility, sharing, or later persistence. [15]

A Korean study of 225 adults used signal-detection sensitivity, *d′*, to measure the ability to distinguish real from fake news. Analytic thinking improved accuracy sensitivity at average or low NFCC, but at high NFCC accuracy sensitivity no longer varied with analytic-thinking scores. This is a significant NFCC × analytic-thinking moderation, although the abstract gives no interaction effect size and the outcome remains accuracy detection rather than belief or sharing. [16]

**Sharing and persistence require separate conclusions.** The direct literature does not establish an NCC-specific effect on sharing intention or actual sharing. The necessary evidence would measure trait or state NFC alongside a misinformation sharing task and, ideally, link the task to observed behavior. A belief association cannot be converted into a sharing coefficient because people may share for identity, entertainment, social approval, or strategic reasons even when they doubt a claim.

The same separation applies to persistence. No direct study in this evidence base reports an NCC-by-delayed-belief or NCC-by-correction interaction. The decisive design would measure NFC before exposure, assess initial belief, deliver a correction or competing source, and test belief again after a meaningful delay. Until then, NCC-specific persistence should be treated as an open conditional question rather than inferred from immediate acceptance.

## How closure could produce misinformation acceptance

### Ambiguity raises the value of a quick answer

**The proposed chain begins with uncertainty.** A complex or threatening event creates a gap between what a person wants to understand and what the available evidence supports. When information is incomplete, contradictory, or difficult to evaluate, a strong need for closure increases the subjective value of reaching a definite answer. That motive is not directed toward falsehood; it is directed toward ending the uncomfortable state of not knowing.

This explains why conspiracy narratives can be attractive. They often provide a compact structure: a hidden actor, a deliberate intention, and a causal story connecting otherwise confusing events. The German study describes conspiracies as simple and structured explanations, while also noting that high NCC could in principle support a trusted official account if that is the most accessible closure-satisfying explanation. [3]

**Ambiguity is not the same as uncertainty intolerance.** The Polish finding that avoidance of ambiguity predicted conspiracy belief at β = 0.16 fits the proposed route, but the Westrych misinformation paradigm found no misinformation link for intolerance of uncertainty. These measures may differ in whether they assess a motivational desire for closure, a general emotional response to uncertainty, or a preference for predictable situations. The inconsistency is theoretically useful: it suggests that the relevant mechanism may be the active search for a definite explanation rather than discomfort alone.

### Seizing and freezing turn a cue into a judgment

**Seizing selects an answer.** Under closure pressure, people may rely on the first information that appears to resolve the problem. Classic work reviewed by Kruglanski and Webster found the lowest information search under closure-inducing conditions and higher search when participants were encouraged to avoid closure. Other studies linked time pressure, noise, dullness, and costly processing to stronger primacy and anchoring effects. Those findings demonstrate a processing tendency, not a misinformation effect by themselves.

**Freezing stabilizes the answer.** Once a judgment appears to restore order, further search becomes less attractive. Conflicting evidence may be ignored, discounted, or treated as a threat to the newly achieved certainty. This is the point at which a correction can meet resistance: it does not merely add information but reopens the uncertainty that the original judgment closed.

The eyewitness experiment gives this process a concrete form. Participants who remembered both the original film and the misleading description had to decide which detail to use. Hejniak and colleagues proposed that high-NFCC participants may anchor on the more recent, accessible misinformation, analyze it superficially, and stop searching memory once an answer is available. The paper presents this as an explanation, not as a separately manipulated mediation test. [11]

**The external source can become the anchor.** When people doubt their own memory or expertise, information supplied by an apparently authoritative source may feel more definite than an internal recollection. In a social-media setting, the equivalent cue may be a familiar publisher, an expert label, a friend’s endorsement, a large engagement count, or a politically similar source. Closure can therefore increase reliance on whatever cue supplies a clear answer, whether that cue is accurate or misleading.

### Shallow processing competes with analytic comparison

**Shallow processing narrows the search.** The Polish article describes high NCC as associated with shallow analysis and a search for information consistent with an existing knowledge structure. Leman and Cinnirella summarize earlier work in which high NFCC increased reliance on confirmation heuristics, while lower NFCC supported more systematic examination of evidence. In misinformation contexts, this could mean checking whether a headline fits a familiar narrative rather than asking whether its evidence is sufficient.

This is a plausible mechanism, but direct tests remain limited. Most studies measure NCC and acceptance, or measure analytic thinking and accuracy, without observing the information search itself. A mediation claim therefore requires more than a correlation between closure and belief: it should show that closure changes search, cue use, or stopping behavior, which then changes acceptance.

**Analytic thinking can interrupt the chain.** Analytic thinking involves holding an initial response in mind and testing it against evidence, alternatives, or base rates. In the Korean study, analytic thinking improved *d′* at average and low NFCC but not at high NFCC. One interpretation is that analytic ability helps only when the person is willing to keep the judgment open; a strong closure motive may terminate the search before analytic resources are fully used.

Adjacent misinformation studies show the same contrast without measuring NCC. In a U.S. experiment, higher CRT predicted better discrimination between true and false headlines, while ideological congruence shifted response bias toward calling congenial headlines true. In another experiment, six seconds of time pressure reduced discrimination but did not reliably change the overall tendency to call headlines true. These results support a distinction between the ability to separate truth from falsehood and the direction of a response bias. [17][9]

**Analytic effort is not automatically neutral.** Higher CRT can improve discrimination, but it can also interact with political congruence. The time-pressure study found that ideological congruence increased the tendency to rate headlines as true, and the CRT-by-congruence interaction on response bias was positive in the control condition. Thus, analytic processing may sometimes improve accuracy and sometimes help people defend a congenial interpretation. NCC should not be treated as a simple substitute for analytic thinking or partisan motivation.

### Sources and familiarity provide ready-made answers

**Credibility can resolve uncertainty.** Source credibility combines perceived expertise and trustworthiness. When a message is complex and the reader lacks time or knowledge, credibility can act as a low-effort acceptance cue. A person seeking closure may be especially willing to use that cue because it allows a judgment without evaluating every claim.

A 2024 experiment with 790 U.S. participants manipulated the credibility and political similarity of a fictitious news publisher. Both high credibility and political similarity increased perceived reliability of misinformation, but credibility had the stronger effect. Similarity amplified the effect of credibility; it did not compensate for a source explicitly described as low credibility. Lower CRT participants relied more heavily on credibility cues, while higher CRT participants showed more consistent skepticism across conditions. [14]

This study did not measure NCC, so it is mechanism-adjacent rather than direct evidence. It nevertheless clarifies what a closure mechanism would predict: when a credible source supplies a simple answer, high closure may reinforce acceptance; when a source is clearly discredited, the cue may block rather than promote acceptance. The direction depends on the value of the cue.

**Familiarity creates fluency.** Familiar claims are processed more easily and can feel more truthful. In the time-pressure study, self-reported familiarity strongly increased the tendency to label headlines true, with a response-bias coefficient of β = 1.50 and a negative discrimination coefficient of β = −0.40. A person high in closure may be especially likely to treat that feeling of recognition as sufficient evidence because it offers a quick route to certainty. [17]

Familiarity is not always equivalent to belief. Ecker and colleagues found a small familiarity-backfire effect on claim-congruent inferences after fact-check-only exposure, with ηp² = .014, but direct false-claim belief was virtually identical between no-exposure and fact-check-only groups: 4.38 versus 4.39 on a zero-to-ten scale. That divergence shows why inferential acceptance, direct belief, accuracy, and sharing should not be collapsed. [18]

**Social proof can become a collective cue.** Visible endorsements, comments, or judgments from other users may tell a person that a claim is widely accepted. In a set of experiments summarized in Traberg’s dissertation, explicit endorsement cues increased misinformation reliability judgments through perceived public consensus, with a reported total effect of *d* = 0.53. This is not an NCC result, but it supplies another route through which closure pressure may operate: the apparent agreement of others can substitute for independent evaluation.

The route remains conditional on attention. In the social-cue experiments, effects were weaker among participants who failed attention checks, suggesting that social information influences judgment only when it enters processing. High NCC might increase reliance on such cues, but that interaction has not been directly tested.

## Conditions that strengthen, weaken, or redirect the relationship

The mixed direct findings make more sense when the environment is treated as part of the mechanism. Closure matters most when a definite answer is needed, the discrepancy is not noticed, and a plausible cue supplies a ready-made conclusion. It matters less when conflicting information is salient, sources are clearly unreliable, the person has relevant knowledge, or the task rewards careful comparison.

### Ambiguity and discrepancy awareness

**Unnoticed conflict is especially important.** In Westrych’s four-phase misinformation paradigm, high-NCC participants were more susceptible than low-NCC participants when they were unaware of the discrepancy between original and later information. When participants noticed the discrepancy, the predicted high-versus-low difference was not supported. Awareness may force the person to confront uncertainty rather than allowing the later claim to become a seamless answer.

This pattern fits the seizing-and-freezing account better than a simple “high NCC equals gullibility” claim. Closure should matter when it can terminate an unnoticed conflict; once conflict is made explicit, people may engage in source monitoring, comparison, or uncertainty acknowledgment. [19]

**Ambiguity dimensions diverge.** The Polish avoidance-of-ambiguity coefficient was positive, β = 0.16, but its intolerance-of-uncertainty comparison showed no misinformation relationship. This suggests that the active motive to obtain a clear interpretation may be more relevant than a broad tendency to feel uncomfortable when outcomes are uncertain. Future work should measure the constructs together rather than assuming that one stands in for the others.

### Time pressure changes discrimination more reliably than response direction

**Pressure reduces quality of separation.** In a preregistered U.S. experiment with 760 participants, a six-second deadline reduced overall accuracy from 72.7% to 69.6%. Signal-detection analysis estimated a reduction in discrimination of β = −0.26, with a 95% credible interval from −0.34 to −0.18. The response-bias contrast was β = 0.05, with an interval from −0.02 to 0.13, so the data did not show a reliable shift toward labeling headlines true.

Time pressure is a classic state closure inducer, but this study did not measure NCC. It therefore shows what a closure-like processing condition can do without proving that NCC caused the decline. The result also cautions against the phrase “time pressure makes people accept misinformation” when the more precise finding is that it makes true–false discrimination worse.

**Cues remain active under pressure.** Ideological congruence and familiarity continued to influence judgments under the deadline. Familiarity’s response-bias effect was slightly weaker under time pressure, but it remained substantial. Closure pressure therefore does not eliminate all cues; it changes the balance between information search, discrimination, and accessible shortcuts.

### Source credibility, ideology, and institutional trust

**Trust has stronger direct evidence than NCC.** In Germany, political trust and crisis-specific trust strongly predicted lower conspiracy belief, while their interactions with NCC were both *B* = 0.00, with 95% confidence intervals from −0.03 to 0.04. Rightward ideological orientation predicted higher belief at *B* = 0.05 to 0.07, but there was no evidence that trust systematically changed the NCC association. [3]

The implication is not that trust and ideology never moderate closure. It is that the German COVID-19 study did not support that moderation in its particular information environment. NCC and trust may operate as partly independent influences: closure increases the attraction of a simple answer, while trust determines which source is treated as a credible provider of that answer.

**Political congruence supplies a competing explanation.** Source experiments show that people often rate information from politically similar outlets as more credible or accurate. In the 790-person fictitious-source experiment, however, explicit low credibility blocked the similarity effect. This indicates that political identity can amplify a source cue but may not override all credibility information.

The Argentine study also found significant differences in fake-news belief by government trust, education, and political ideology, but the abstract gives neither the directions nor effect sizes of those group differences. The safest conclusion is that the Argentine relationship was heterogeneous across social and political characteristics, not that any one ideology universally magnified NCC.

**Credibility and similarity are not NCC.** A dissertation chapter tested whether Need for Cognition, not Need for Cognitive Closure, moderated source effects. Need for Cognition concerns enjoyment or willingness to engage in effortful thinking; NCC concerns motivation to reach closure. Conflating them would make the evidence appear more direct than it is. The source-credibility findings justify testing NCC as a possible moderator, but they do not already establish that result.

### Prior knowledge, repetition, and familiarity

**Prior exposure can weaken deliberation.** In Fazio’s randomized study of 501 U.S. adults, asking participants to explain why a headline was true or false reduced false-sharing intention from 2.11 to 1.79, *d* = 0.27, while true-headline sharing did not change. The reduction was larger for novel false headlines, *d* = 0.35, than for repeated false headlines, *d* = 0.18. Repetition weakened but did not eliminate the benefit of deliberate explanation. [20]

This is relevant to closure because a familiar claim is easier to seize. It also shows why correction may work differently before and after a claim becomes familiar. A direct NCC-by-prior-knowledge coefficient remains unestablished, but the broader pattern predicts that prior exposure can reduce the amount of reflection available for a new correction.

**Knowledge does not guarantee immunity.** Familiarity can increase perceived truth even when people possess relevant knowledge, and repeated exposure can make retrieval fluent. Conversely, prior knowledge can help people detect implausible claims when it is accessible and applicable. Closure may determine whether a person uses knowledge to investigate or uses familiarity as a stopping cue. The relevant moderator is therefore not merely “knowledge present or absent,” but whether knowledge is retrieved and applied before judgment freezes.

### Analytic thinking changes the route, not always the outcome

**High closure can blunt analytic benefits.** The Korean study’s interaction suggests that analytic thinking improved accuracy at average and low NFCC but offered no gradient at high NFCC. One interpretation is that people high in closure may possess analytic capacity but deploy it less consistently when an early answer already satisfies their motivational need.

**CRT findings are mixed by context.** In the U.S. headline study, CRT was generally associated with rejecting false and hyperpartisan content and with better truth discernment. Yet the interaction between CRT and ideological congruence showed that analytic thinking did not remove all partisan response bias. In the time-pressure study, CRT predicted discrimination but not response bias. These findings support a two-part interpretation: analytic thinking can improve the ability to distinguish claims, while identity and source cues can still influence which claims feel acceptable.

### Corrections can reopen or fail to reopen closure

**A correction adds processing demands.** A successful correction must compete with an already accessible claim, explain why the original claim is wrong, and offer a replacement account. That process can be difficult for anyone, but a person strongly motivated to preserve closure may experience the correction as an invitation to reopen an uncomfortable uncertainty.

Classic closure theory predicts a phase-sensitive pattern. Before a judgment crystallizes, a persuasive message that offers a coherent answer may be especially influential. After crystallization, freezing should make arguments that challenge the current answer less persuasive. This is a theory-based prediction rather than a demonstrated NCC-specific correction effect.

**General correction results are mixed.** In a U.K. two-wave panel experiment with 1,841 adults, a fact-check of a false immigration claim immediately changed beliefs for roughly 6% to 12% of respondents depending on the attributed source, but effects dissipated at a follow-up averaging 16 days. No NCC measure was included. [21]

A 2024 U.S., Polish, and Hong Kong intervention program with 6,127 participants found that fact-checking, media-literacy tips, and misinformation coverage reduced belief in false claims but also increased skepticism toward true information in the United States and Poland. In the U.S. sample, effects disappeared at a one-week follow-up. These results make discernment essential: a correction that lowers false-claim belief while also lowering trust in true information is not an unqualified success. [22]

**Inoculation improves some judgments.** A meta-analysis of 42 independent studies and 42,530 participants found that psychological inoculation reduced misinformation credibility, *d* = −0.36, with substantial heterogeneity, *I*² = 93.1%. Across 12 studies, the pooled sharing-intention effect was *d* = −0.35, but its 95% confidence interval ranged from −0.79 to 0.09 and included zero. Inoculation therefore has stronger support for credibility assessment than for sharing intention, and neither result identifies NCC as the moderator. [23]

## Uncertainty communication and social context

### Uncertainty can either invite heuristics or support calibrated trust

**Context determines the direction.** During the COVID-19 crisis, preliminary evidence often contained both deficient uncertainty, meaning preliminary evidence lacks sufficient data, and consensus uncertainty, meaning scientists had not yet agreed on its interpretation. A Weibo study analyzed 3,439 posts and 10,380 reposts about whether pets could transmit COVID-19. Ambiguous attitudes toward preliminary evidence and the early uncertain-evidence stage were associated with more likes and reposts of misinformation-related posts and weaker propagation of refutations. [24]

The authors propose that ambiguous evidence encourages people to construct a quick explanation from personal experience, naïve theories of science, and accessible heuristics. That explanation may provide enough certainty to guide action, while a detailed refutation requires more effort and may introduce further uncertainty. This is closely compatible with a closure mechanism, but the Weibo study did not measure NCC or test mediation of closure.

**Uncertainty communication is not uniformly harmful.** The same article cites a systematic review of 48 experimental risk-communication studies in which communicating deficient or consensus uncertainty usually reduced belief in, perceived credibility of, or intentions to follow recommendations. A transparent statement such as “the evidence is preliminary, here is what is known, here is what is not known, and here is when the next update will arrive” may reduce overconfidence rather than encourage misinformation.

The practical distinction is between unstructured ambiguity and structured uncertainty. Unstructured ambiguity leaves the audience to supply a simple explanation. Structured uncertainty identifies the evidence boundary, the level of expert agreement, the consequence of acting now, and the conditions that would change the conclusion. Whether high-NCC people respond differently to these two forms has not been directly tested.

**Consensus can replace independent checking.** The Weibo findings suggest that people may infer a claim from preliminary evidence and then spread it through social cues. Other experiments show that visible endorsement can increase perceived public consensus and misinformation reliability. For someone seeking closure, apparent agreement may function as a collective answer. Yet social consensus can also support accurate correction when the visible norm favors checking and source verification.

## How strong is the causal evidence?

### Most direct findings are correlational

**Survey associations support prediction, not causation.** The German panel, Polish structural model, Argentine fake-news study, and Kaufman accuracy study all show relationships between closure-related measures and misinformation outcomes. Statistical adjustment can reduce confounding, but it cannot by itself demonstrate that closure caused acceptance. Beliefs, trust, ideology, education, age, prior exposure, platform recruitment, and information access can all influence the association.

The German study improves temporal ordering because NCC was measured before COVID-19 conspiracy belief. It still remains observational: participants were not randomly assigned to high or low closure, and the researchers did not measure which conspiratorial or scientific information each participant encountered between waves. Temporal precedence strengthens predictive validity but does not establish a causal pathway.

**Reverse direction remains plausible in some designs.** In cross-sectional data, people who repeatedly encounter conspiratorial information may become more committed to stable explanatory structures, more distrustful of institutions, or more likely to endorse closure-related questionnaire items. That possibility does not explain the German time-separated result completely, but it illustrates why a trait measure should not automatically be treated as an independent cause.

### Experiments manipulate surrounding conditions more often than closure itself

**Misinformation experiments usually measure NCC.** Hejniak randomized exposure to misleading eyewitness information but measured NFCC as an individual difference. The experiment therefore establishes that misinformation can distort memory and that measured NFCC covaried with susceptibility; it does not establish that increasing NCC would produce the distortion.

**Closure experiments usually use other outcomes.** Classic studies manipulated time pressure, noise, task dullness, or processing cost and measured information search, primacy, confidence, or anchoring. Leman randomized accountability and evidence direction in a conspiracy-vignette task, but did not directly confirm that accountability changed the NFC score. These studies support the proposed mechanism while leaving the direct misinformation causal effect open.

**Mediation models need temporal evidence.** The Polish study modeled conspiracy belief as a mediator between closure dimensions and coronavirus fear using 5,000 bootstrap samples. Its paths are informative about statistical structure, but cross-sectional mediation does not establish that closure came first, belief followed, and fear changed afterward. The source itself cautions against cause-and-effect interpretation.

A decisive causal study would randomly induce state closure, manipulate ambiguity or time pressure, measure the manipulation, record information search and cue use, and then assess belief, accuracy, credibility, sharing, and delayed persistence. It would also include a low-closure or accountability condition and true claims alongside false ones.

### Measurement differences limit comparisons

**Scale length changes the construct being sampled.** Published studies use the original 42-item NFCS, a 46-item version in Leman and Cinnirella, 15-item forms, a 14-item form in the Pennycook study, a five-item German form, and an eight-item Argentine form. The forms do not necessarily represent the same balance of predictability, order, ambiguity avoidance, decisiveness, and closed-mindedness.

Neuberg, Judice, and West argued that the NFCS overlaps with personal need for structure and fear of invalidity. They distinguished a nonspecific desire for any decisive answer from a more specific desire to create and maintain simple structures. A total score can therefore combine motives that have different relationships with misinformation. [25]

**Reliability is uneven across facets.** The German five-item scale had α = 0.67. In the Polish 15-item study, subscale alphas ranged from .56 for avoidance of ambiguity to .59 for closed-mindedness, although the authors noted that brief scales often have lower internal consistency. Measurement noise can attenuate or destabilize associations, while a scale that emphasizes one facet can produce a different direction from a total score.

**Cross-national comparability is partial.** A cross-cultural NFCS study with Polish, Flemish, Korean, and American samples found structural invariance, partial metric invariance, and partial scalar invariance. The construct has a broadly similar structure across countries, but comparisons are not perfectly equivalent. A 2025 Peruvian psychometric study likewise found unacceptable fit for the full revised instrument and acceptable fit for a shortened model. [26][27]

### Samples and outcomes constrain generalization

**The evidence base is geographically concentrated.** Direct misinformation studies include Germany, Poland, Argentina, the United States, Korea, and eyewitness samples, so the literature is not confined to one country. Yet a scoping review found that 78% of misinformation studies from 2016–2022 sampled the United States or Europe. Robust cross-cultural replication of the specific NCC-to-misinformation relationship remains limited. [10]

**Self-report dominates behavior.** The same review found belief in misinformation in 78% of studies, online sharing intention in about 18%, and actual behavior in only a small fraction. It warns against inferring offline behavior from belief or intention. For NCC, this means the best-supported claim concerns some forms of reported belief or susceptibility, while actual sharing and real-world action require their own evidence.

**Immediate judgments dominate persistence.** Most studies measure an answer immediately after exposure to a short text or headline. Real misinformation campaigns involve repetition, competing sources, social feedback, emotional engagement, and delays. A person high in closure may be vulnerable at initial exposure but receptive to a later correction, or may freeze a belief and resist it; immediate studies cannot distinguish these trajectories.

## Practical implications for correction

### Use general accuracy tools before profiling people

**General tools have a reasonable evidence base.** The individual-intervention literature includes accuracy prompts, debunking, friction, inoculation, lateral reading, media-literacy tips, social norms, source-credibility labels, and warning or fact-checking labels. A toolbox review synthesized evidence from 81 scientific papers across these nine intervention types. None of the summarized effects was stratified by NCC. [28]

For immediate practice, an accuracy prompt or explanation task is a defensible way to create a pause. Fazio’s experiment reduced false-sharing intention with a short explanation prompt, while leaving true-sharing intention unchanged. The larger effect for novel headlines suggests that a prompt can be particularly useful before familiarity and repetition have made a claim fluent.

**Source checking addresses the proposed cue route.** Lateral reading, independent source comparison, and explicit credibility information make the cue itself an object of scrutiny. In the source-discreditation experiment, reliance on misinformation averaged 6.87 without intervention, 4.62 after correction, 5.17 after source discreditation, 4.17 after both, and 4.11 in a no-misinformation control on a zero-to-ten scale. The combined intervention performed best, though the fictional, nonpolitical materials call for cautious application. [29]

**Inoculation can build broader resistance.** The Bad News game and related inoculation studies reduced perceived reliability of manipulative content across German, Greek, Polish, and Swedish versions, with reported effects in one Swedish pilot ranging from *d* = .15 to .18 for selected techniques and *d* = .24 across fake items. These studies did not measure NCC, but they show that teaching people how manipulation works can improve discernment without requiring a diagnosis of an individual’s closure level. [30]

### Individualized NCC correction remains a hypothesis

**Personalization needs direct evidence.** The current findings do not justify labeling people as “high closure” and assigning them a special correction. No NCC-tailored experiment establishes that high-NCC participants benefit more from explanations, source labels, friction, inoculation, or uncertainty framing. The practical recommendation is therefore to use broadly supported accuracy and verification tools while testing, rather than assuming, whether closure level changes their effects.

A tailored strategy could eventually be useful if it targets a demonstrated mechanism. For example, people who seize on source credibility might benefit from lateral-reading prompts; people who freeze after a claim crystallizes might benefit from corrections that arrive before repetition and include a coherent alternative explanation. Those are testable hypotheses, not current prescriptions.

**Timing may matter more than personality labels.** The seizing-and-freezing model predicts that a correction before belief crystallization could be more receptive than a correction after a claim has become part of a stable explanatory structure. A direct experiment should compare pre-exposure prebunking, immediate correction, delayed correction, and repeated correction, while measuring NCC, initial belief, final belief, confidence, sharing intention, and observed sharing.

### Communicate uncertainty with structure

**Do not confuse honesty with ambiguity.** Uncertainty communication should identify what is known, what is preliminary, how much expert agreement exists, what action is justified now, and what future evidence would change the recommendation. This format gives readers a clear decision structure without pretending that the evidence is complete.

**Test both sides of the trade-off.** In a crisis, ambiguity can encourage heuristic construction and engagement with inferred misinformation, as the Weibo case suggests. But calibrated uncertainty can also reduce unjustified credibility and recommendation intentions across risk-communication studies. The effect depends on whether uncertainty is experienced as an invitation to invent an answer or as a transparent boundary around a trustworthy process.

A closure-sensitive message would therefore pair uncertainty with a provisional answer: “The evidence currently supports X; the main uncertainty is Y; action Z is justified because of the current risk; the next update will depend on W.” Whether this format particularly benefits people high in NCC remains an empirical question.

## A practical reading guide

The evidence supports a modest, mechanism-based approach rather than a personality-based diagnosis.

| Check | Practical action | Evidence anchor | Target outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name the outcome | Separate belief, accuracy, credibility, sharing, and delayed persistence | Misinformation scoping review | Match the intervention to the goal |
| Locate closure pressure | Look for ambiguity, time pressure, unnoticed conflict, familiarity, or a ready-made source cue | NCC theory; time-pressure and familiarity studies | Identify when fast closure is likely |
| Add deliberate comparison | Ask why the claim is true or false, check the source, and compare independent evidence | Fazio; source-discreditation study | Improve immediate discernment and sharing judgment |
| Protect calibrated trust | Test false and true items together and monitor skepticism toward accurate information | Three-country intervention study | Reduce false belief without indiscriminate distrust |
| Avoid profiling | Use general tools until NCC-stratified trials show a reliable interaction | No direct NCC-tailored correction effect established | Prevent overconfident personalization |

### Priorities for stronger evidence

**Separate trait from state.** Future studies should measure stable NCC and manipulate situational closure independently. A factorial design could cross trait level with time pressure, ambiguity, accountability, and processing cost, while checking whether the manipulation actually changes state closure.

**Measure the mechanism directly.** The strongest test would record information search, time spent on sources, attention to corrections, use of familiarity, and stopping behavior. It would show whether seizing and freezing mediate the association rather than simply naming them after a correlation appears.

**Use matched outcomes.** The same participants should rate belief, perceived accuracy, source credibility, true–false discrimination, sharing intention, observed sharing, and delayed persistence for the same items. This would reveal whether an NCC effect is broad or confined to one stage of acceptance.

**Build delayed and behavioral tests.** Follow-ups should occur after repetition, correction, and realistic intervals. Simulated feeds, browser-based sharing tasks, or consenting platform-linked measures could connect self-reported judgments to behavior while preserving ethical safeguards.

**Replicate across cultures.** The next generation of studies should use measurement-invariant closure scales and comparable misinformation materials in countries outside the heavily represented United States and Europe. Germany, Poland, Argentina, Korea, and Brazil provide useful starting points, but direct NCC-outcome replication should include broader regions and information environments.

**Test corrections by closure level.** Researchers should compare accuracy prompts, debunking, source labels, friction, inoculation, and structured uncertainty messages across NCC levels. The decisive outcomes are not only immediate accuracy but also belief persistence, confidence calibration, true-news skepticism, sharing, and actual behavior.

**Reading the numbers.** Correlations, standardized regression coefficients, eta-squared values, and Cohen’s *d* above are study-specific associations or contrasts, not a common NCC risk score. Belief scales, accuracy judgments, credibility ratings, sharing intentions, and observed behavior use different units and denominators; the Argentine fake-news result is reported as significant with *N* = 204 but without a coefficient, while NCC-specific sharing and persistence effects remain unestablished because the decisive trait-or-state measures have not been paired with those outcomes. The evidence is also dominated by self-report and immediate judgments, so claims about delayed belief or real-world sharing require direct behavioral and longitudinal tests.

Need for cognitive closure is best understood as a conditional pressure toward a definite answer. When ambiguity, time pressure, unnoticed conflict, or accessible cues make that answer attractive, seizing and freezing may increase acceptance of misinformation—particularly conspiracy-related claims and misleading information in memory. But the average association is small, the direction varies by closure facet and context, and trust, ideology, familiarity, source credibility, analytic thinking, and correction timing can redirect the result. NCC is therefore a vulnerability factor worth studying, not a diagnosis of misinformation acceptance and not a sufficient explanation of why people believe or share false claims.
