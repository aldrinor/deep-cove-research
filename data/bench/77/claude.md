# Need for Cognitive Closure and Misinformation Acceptance: What the Evidence Actually Shows

Need for cognitive closure (NFC) is a real but weak, inconsistent and strongly context-dependent predictor of misinformation acceptance: it reliably makes people commit faster and hold more tightly to whatever explanation is most available, but on its own it adds only about r ≈ .10 to conspiracy belief and is often non-significant for fake-news headlines once other variables are controlled. Its main role is to amplify, not to cause. NFC leads to misinformation mainly when the false account is the most salient, simplest or earliest explanation available, and it can just as easily lock people into true beliefs when an official account comes first.

## TL;DR

- **The effect is small and depends on conditions.**  Meta-analyses put the NFC–conspiracy-belief correlation at about r = .10 (Bowes, Costello & Tasimi, 2023, *Psychological Bulletin* ; k = 13 studies, N = 11,583). Biddlestone et al.’s (2024, *Psychological Bulletin* ) “need for structure and closure” estimate was not significant in the main model (r = .05) and only small (r ≈ .09–.14) after outlier removal or bias correction. Fake-news headline studies mostly find null or weak effects (r ≈ .10–.25, depending on headline type).
- **The clearest causal evidence shows NFC is “direction-agnostic.”**  High-NFC people seize on whichever explanation is salient. Marchlewska, Cichocka & Kossowska (2018, *EJSP* ) found that NFC predicted conspiracy belief when a conspiratorial explanation was salient and an official one was missing, but the link *reversed*  when an official explanation was available. NFC raises commitment to the first adequate answer, not gullibility as such.
- **For interventions, act early and fill the gap.**  Pre-empt misinformation by getting there first (prebunking), always replace a debunked claim with a coherent alternative explanation, and communicate uncertainty in ways that offer structure. Do not expect NFC to be a useful screening variable: political trust, conspiracy mentality, intolerance of ambiguity and analytic thinking predict misinformation belief more strongly.

## Key Findings

1. **Theory predicts a strong link; the data show a weak one.**  Lay epistemics theory gives NFC a central role (“seizing and freezing”). Yet in every meta-analysis it ranks among the *weakest*  epistemic correlates of conspiracy belief, weaker than intolerance of ambiguity (r = .25), actively open-minded thinking (r = −.25) and cognitive reflection (r = −.17) in Bowes et al. (2023). [newswise](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/168744596980985_The%20Conspiratorial%20Mind.pdf)
2. **Context moderates the effect so much that the main effect can disappear.**  The NFC–belief link depends on which explanation is accessible (Marchlewska et al., 2018), on prior beliefs (Leman & Cinnirella, 2013; Nan & Daily, 2015), and on whether general or specific conspiracy theories are measured (b = .16 vs .05 in Bowes et al., 2023). [newswise +2](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/168744596980985_The%20Conspiratorial%20Mind.pdf)
3. **NFC mostly amplifies confidence and entrenchment rather than credulity.**  Experimental evidence shows high NFC magnifies primacy effects, biased assimilation and memory-based misinformation effects (Pica et al., 2014). Lowering NFC weakens the influence of prior beliefs on how evidence is evaluated (Leman & Cinnirella, 2013). [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)
4. **For fake-news headlines and illusory truth, the evidence is mostly null.**  De keersmaecker et al. (2020, *PSPB* ; 7 studies, combined N = 2,196, with NFC tested in Study 1) found “no evidence for moderation by any of the cognitive measures across studies,” NFC included. Szebeni et al. (2021) found NFC did not independently predict fake-news belief once conspiracy mentality was included. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.790848/full) Sultan et al.’s (2024, *PNAS* ) large individual-participant meta-analysis of the headline paradigm did not even include NFC. [rncd](https://rncd.org/pesquisas/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Manuscript-Preprint-Susceptibility-to-Online-Misinformation_-A-Systematic-Meta-Analysis-of-Demographic-and-Psychological-Factors-1.pdf)
5. **COVID-19 studies show small effects swamped by trust.**  In a prospective German panel, pre-pandemic NFC correlated only r = .12 with later COVID-19 conspiracy belief, while political and institutional trust were far stronger predictors (Jedinger & Masch, 2025). [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full) Vaccine skepticism was unrelated to NFC in Petersen and colleagues’ cross-national experiment (2021, *PNAS* ; b = −0.01).
6. **Direct evidence on corrections is almost absent.**  No well-powered study has tested NFC as a moderator of the continued influence effect (CIE). Reviews explicitly flag this as a gap, noting that NFC “is primarily used as a control variable in studies of misinformation and is rarely the main construct of interest.” [cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/social-media-and-democracy/misinformation-and-its-correction/61FA7FD743784A723BA234533012E810)

## Details

### 1. Definition and background

**The construct.**  Kruglanski defines need for cognitive closure as the desire for “a firm answer to a question and an aversion toward ambiguity” (Kruglanski & Webster, 1996, *Psychological Review* ). It is a *nonspecific*  epistemic motive: the person wants *some*  answer, not a particular one. This distinguishes it from the need for *specific*  closure (wanting a particular, desirable conclusion), which corresponds to directional motivated reasoning. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic_motivation) NFC is conceptualized as a continuum, with a “need to avoid closure” at the opposite pole. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_(psychology))

**Lay epistemic theory (LET).**  NFC grew out of Kruglanski’s lay epistemics, a general account of how people form knowledge by generating and validating hypotheses. LET produced three interrelated research programs: need for closure, the unimodel of social judgment, and epistemic authority. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic_motivation) The epistemic-authority strand matters for misinformation because it holds that whom one trusts as a knowledge source (including oneself) shapes which hypotheses get “frozen.”

**Seizing and freezing.**  Kruglanski and Webster (1996) proposed two sequential tendencies: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic_motivation)

- **Seizing:**  an urgency to reach closure quickly, so people grab early or accessible cues.
- **Freezing:**  a permanence tendency to preserve closure once it is reached, so people resist new information and become more confident.

Their review documents primacy effects, reliance on early cues, stereotyping, reduced hypothesis generation and elevated confidence under heightened NFC.

**Measurement.**

- **Webster & Kruglanski (1994, *JPSP* )**  introduced the Need for Closure Scale (NFCS; 42 items including a lie subscale). It has five facets: preference for order, preference for predictability, decisiveness, discomfort with ambiguity and closed-mindedness. Their validation studies linked high NFC to stronger primacy effects and greater resistance to persuasion. [communicationcache](http://www.communicationcache.com/uploads/1/0/8/8/10887248/individual_differences_in_need_for_cognitive_closure.pdf)
- **Factor structure was disputed.**  Neuberg and colleagues argued the scale contains two largely orthogonal factors: decisiveness and need for structure. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_(psychology))
- **Revisions.**  Roets & Van Hiel (2007) rewrote the decisiveness items, which had tapped the *ability*  rather than the *motivation*  to decide. [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320925272_'Fake_news'_Incorrect_but_hard_to_correct_The_role_of_cognitive_ability_on_the_impact_of_false_information_on_social_impressions) Kruglanski and colleagues acknowledged this as an “improved version.” Roets & Van Hiel (2011, *Personality and Individual Differences* ) then validated a 15-item short form (three items per facet) that correlates highly with the full scale and is now standard in misinformation research. [wiley](https://asistdl.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/meet.2014.14505101066)[uw](http://icelab.psych.uw.edu.pl/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Intelligence-and-fake-news.pdf)
- **Short forms vary.**  Some panel studies use five-item versions (e.g., Jedinger & Masch, 2025, used the five-item short form proposed by Rinke, 2014). [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full)

**Dispositional vs. situational NFC.**  NFC can be measured as a trait or induced experimentally:

- It is **raised**  by time pressure, noise, fatigue, cognitive load and dull tasks. [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)
- It is **lowered**  by accountability, fear of invalidity and making errors costly (e.g., Kruglanski & Freund, 1983; Ford & Kruglanski, 1995). [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)

This matters because much of the causal evidence for “seizing and freezing” comes from situational manipulations, whereas most misinformation research uses only the trait scale.

### 2. Theorized mechanisms linking NFC to misinformation

| Mechanism | Theoretical prediction | Status of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seizing on early/available information | Whatever arrives first, including a false claim, is adopted quickly | Strong in basic research on primacy; shown for conspiracy salience (Marchlewska et al., 2018) |
| Freezing on existing beliefs | Initial misinformation resists later correction | Plausible; indirect support (Leman & Cinnirella, 2013; Nan & Daily, 2015); no direct CIE test |
| Reduced information search | Fewer hypotheses generated, less verification | Basic-lab support (Mayseless & Kruglanski, 1987); little misinformation-specific data |
| Preference for simple, coherent explanations | Conspiracy theories and rumors offer closure | Core theoretical rationale (Douglas et al., 2017); meta-analytic effect is small |
| Continued influence effect | Retractions leave a “gap” that high-NFC people cannot tolerate | Proposed in reviews (Wittenberg & Berinsky, 2020; Rapp & Salovich, 2018); untested |
| Motivated reasoning | NFC is nonspecific, so it interacts with *specific*  motives (partisanship, identity) | Supported for biased assimilation (Nan & Daily, 2015) |
| Heuristic vs. analytic processing | High NFC promotes heuristic, confirmatory processing | NFC is distinct from cognitive reflection; the illusory truth effect is not moderated by NFC (De keersmaecker et al., 2020) |
| Uncertainty and threat | Crises raise state NFC and the appetite for any explanation | Theoretically central for COVID-19; observed effects are small |

Two theoretical points are crucial for interpreting the evidence:

- **NFC is direction-agnostic.**  Kruglanski, Webster and Klem (1993, *JPSP* ) showed that high-NFC people *resist*  persuasion when they already have an opinion but are *more*  persuadable when they lack one. [zimbardo](https://www.zimbardo.com/need-for-closure-psychology-definition-history-examples/) So NFC should increase misinformation acceptance only when the misinformation reaches people before a credible alternative does, or when it fits an existing belief. If accurate information arrives first, NFC should protect against later misinformation.
- **Crises cut both ways.**  The pandemic raised uncertainty, so situational NFC should have pushed people toward any available explanation. But in most Western contexts authoritative explanations were also widely available, so NFC could be satisfied by official sources. This is exactly the logic Jedinger & Masch (2025) tested. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full)

### 3. Empirical evidence

#### (a) Fake news headlines and truth discernment

The headline paradigm, in which participants rate the accuracy of true and false headlines, is the dominant method in fake-news research. NFC fares poorly in it.

- **Szebeni, Lönnqvist & Jasinskaja-Lahti (2021, *Frontiers in Psychology* )** : Hungarian sample, N = 295, 2019 election run-up, 15-item NFC scale. [frontiersin](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.790848/pdf)
    - Zero-order correlations: NFC correlated with belief in non-political fake news (r = .24, p < .001) and pro-government fake news (r = .25, p < .001), but not anti-government fake news (r = −.09). [frontiersin](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.790848/pdf)
    - In multivariate models including conspiracy mentality, NFC was not a consistent predictor (pro-government fake news β = .001). Its coefficient for non-political fake news (β = .13) was reported inconsistently in the paper. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.790848/full)
    - The authors concluded that predispositions including need for closure “were not associated with fake news belief”; conspiracy mentality was the only consistent predictor. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.790848/full)
    - The NFC correlations track the pro-government (Fidesz-aligned) content, which suggests ideological confounding.
- **De keersmaecker, Dunning, Pennycook, Rand, Sanchez, Unkelbach & Roets (2020, *PSPB* , 46, 204–215)** : seven studies (combined N = 2,196) using trivia statements and fake-news headlines, with NFC measured in Study 1. The illusory truth effect (repetition increasing perceived truth) appeared in every study, and the authors report “no evidence for moderation by any of the cognitive measures across studies,” including NFC, cognitive ability and cognitive style. This is the most rigorous null in the literature, although its NFC test rests on a single study.
- **A boundary condition on that null:**  Stump and colleagues (2021, *Psychological Research* , 86, 12–36, “Is it all about the feeling?”) found a *stronger*  truth effect among high-NFC participants at a short (10-minute) repetition interval (repetition × NFC interaction b = 0.371, p < .001 in Experiment 1; replicated at b = 0.219, p = .013 in Experiment 2), concluding that “the truth effect is increased for individuals with a higher need for cognitive closure.” The authors reconcile this with De keersmaecker et al.’s null by noting that the latter used a 5–7-day interval. [springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-020-01459-1) NFC may amplify fluency-based truth judgments only when the earlier exposure is recent, which fits the “seizing” account. This is a single paper and needs replication.
- **Education as a buffer:**  a two-study paper in *Personality and Individual Differences*  (vol. 251, 2026, article 113567, published online November 2025, “When education fails”; N = 354 and N = 306) found that at +1 SD of NFC the education advantage in misinformation discernment (MIST) “shrinks to near-zero.” However, the paper reports that “the moderated regression models for need for closure on misinformation revealed non-significant interactions terms at α = 0.05,” so this is preliminary.
- **Sultan et al. (2024, *PNAS* )** : an individual-participant meta-analysis of 31 headline experiments (11,561 participants; 256,337 choices) using signal detection theory. [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11588074/) It examined age, gender, education, political identity, analytic thinking, ideological congruency, motivated reflection and familiarity, but **not NFC** . [rncd](https://rncd.org/pesquisas/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Manuscript-Preprint-Susceptibility-to-Online-Misinformation_-A-Systematic-Meta-Analysis-of-Demographic-and-Psychological-Factors-1.pdf) The best synthesis of fake-news susceptibility simply has no NFC data to pool, which shows how peripheral NFC has been in this literature.
- **Downstream behavior:**  an ACM CSCW study (“WARNING This Contains Misinformation,” arXiv 2407.02710) found NFC was among the strongest predictors of attitudes toward misinformation *warning tags* . It notes that “prior work has shown NFCC to be negatively correlated with misinformation identification accuracy,” while acknowledging that “the effect of NFCC in other information evaluation domains has been contested.” Those earlier findings come from smaller, often non-psychology samples.

**Verdict:**  For headline belief and discernment, NFC is at best a weak correlate (r ≈ .10–.25 bivariate, often null when adjusted). It does not moderate the illusory truth effect over typical delays.

#### (b) Conspiracy theory beliefs

This is where most NFC work has been done.

- **Leman & Cinnirella (2013, *Frontiers in Psychology* )** :
    - Study 1 found *no*  association between dispositional NFC and belief in real-world conspiracy theories. The authors had predicted this, because they treat NFC as a general process rather than a conspiracist trait. [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)
    - Study 2 (N = 86) showed that people high in conspiracy belief rated a conspiracy explanation of a novel event as more likely after reading ambiguous evidence, while low believers rated it as less likely. When NFC was experimentally *lowered*  (via accountability), this prior-belief bias diminished. [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)[researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/244482781_Beliefs_in_conspiracy_theories_and_need_for_cognitive_closure)
    - Implication: NFC drives *freezing on prior beliefs*  in both directions.
- **Marchlewska, Cichocka & Kossowska (2018, *European Journal of Social Psychology* , 48, 109–117), “Addicted to answers”** :
    - Experiment 1: NFC predicted endorsement of conspiracy explanations for an event lacking an official explanation. [semanticscholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Addicted-to-answers:-Need-for-cognitive-closure-and-Marchlewska-Cichocka/41f98c45f8eb2e6c7e070d6ecacc9de606c3a386)
    - Experiment 2: when conspiratorial explanations were made salient, NFC predicted belief in a conspiracy behind a mysterious plane crash. But “the link between NFCC and beliefs in conspiratorial explanations was reversed in the case of a plane crash with an official, non-conspiratorial, explanation.” [wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.2308)
    - Conclusion: high-NFC people “seize on conspiratorial explanations for uncertain events when such explanations are situationally accessible.” [wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.2308) This is the field’s key moderator finding, and it has not been directly replicated at scale.
- **Bowes, Costello & Tasimi (2023, *Psychological Bulletin* , 149, 259–293)** : 170 studies, N = 158,473. [ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/plbul/fulltext/10.1037/bul0000392~the-conspiratorial-mind-a-meta-analytic-review-of)
    - Need for closure: k = 13 studies, 15 samples, 24 effect sizes, N = 11,583, **r = .10, 95% CI [.05, .15]** , with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² ≈ 80% at level 3). [apa](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000392.pdf)[newswise](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/168744596980985_The%20Conspiratorial%20Mind.pdf)
    - The link was stronger for general conspiracy beliefs (b = .16) than for specific theories (b = .05). [apa](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000392.pdf)[newswise](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/168744596980985_The%20Conspiratorial%20Mind.pdf)
    - Comparison correlates: intolerance of ambiguity r = .25, dogmatism r = .14, actively open-minded thinking r = −.25, cognitive reflection r = −.17. [newswise](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/168744596980985_The%20Conspiratorial%20Mind.pdf)
    - The authors describe need for closure, need for control and analytic thinking as “small correlates… smaller than expected given the hypothesized theoretical import of these variables.” [newswise](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/168744596980985_The%20Conspiratorial%20Mind.pdf)
- **Biddlestone, Green, Douglas, Azevedo, Sutton & Cichocka (2024, *Psychological Bulletin* )** : preregistered; 279 studies, N = 137,406, organized around Douglas et al.’s epistemic/existential/social motive framework. [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359841963_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analytic_synthesis_of_the_motives_associated_with_conspiracy_beliefs)
    - Epistemic motives overall correlated r = .14 (k = 114) with conspiracy belief. [kent](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/107924/3/Main%20text_CLEAN.pdf)
    - The specific “need for structure and closure” estimate was non-significant in the main model (r = .05, 95% CI [−.07, .17]). [kent](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/107924/3/Main%20text_CLEAN.pdf)[kent](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/107924/3/Main%20text_CLEAN.pdf)
    - It was small and significant when modeled alone (r = .09), after outlier removal (r = .09) and after PET-PEESE correction (r = .14). [kent](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/107924/3/Main%20text_CLEAN.pdf)[uu](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/257106456/Reasons_to_Believe_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analytic_Synthesis_of_the_Motives_Associated_With_Conspiracy_Beliefs.pdf)
    - There were no experimental effect sizes for this motive, and the average poorly represented the individual effects. This makes it one of the least robust epistemic correlates.
- **Other single studies are mixed.**  Jedinger & Masch (2025) note that two earlier studies found no association (Imhoff & Bruder, 2014; Leman & Cinnirella, 2013), one found a small *negative*  association (Jones et al., 2023), [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full) and one found the link non-significant after adjusting for demographics and political orientation. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full)

**Verdict:**  A small positive association with general conspiracist ideation (r ≈ .10) is reasonably well established meta-analytically. Its size is modest, it is heterogeneous, and its causal status rests mainly on one experimental paper (Marchlewska et al., 2018) showing that it depends on which explanation is salient.

#### (c) COVID-19 and vaccine misinformation

- **Jedinger & Masch (2025, *Frontiers in Social Psychology* )** : German panel; NFC (five-item short form) measured before the pandemic (December 2018–February 2019) and COVID-19 conspiracy belief measured during the pandemic. [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388272109_Need_for_cognitive_closure_political_trust_and_belief_in_conspiracy_theories_during_the_COVID-19_pandemic)
    - NFC correlated r = .12 (p < .001) with later conspiracy belief: “individuals with a high need for cognitive closure are more likely to accept conspiracy narratives, but the effect size is small.” [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full)
    - Pre-pandemic political trust correlated r = −.37 with conspiracy belief. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full)
    - The hypothesized NFC × trust interaction was not supported. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full)[frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/pdf)
    - The authors judge NFC’s role “rather marginal.” The prospective design is a strength. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/full)
- **Staszak et al. (2022, *IJERPH* )** : Polish sample, N = 380. The relations of avoidance of ambiguity and closed-mindedness with coronavirus fear were fully mediated by COVID-19 conspiracy beliefs, which suggests facet-level effects. [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9690611/)[semanticscholar](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9d49/ae2e777835e1e9c73fa9ebd881fef8c026c5.pdf) The data are cross-sectional.
- **Petersen and colleagues (2021, *PNAS* )** : cross-national experiments on transparent vaccine communication. Vaccine skepticism was not associated with NFC (b = −0.01 [−0.04; 0.02], p = .50), and tolerance of ambiguity correlated only r = .05 with vaccine acceptance. The authors infer that much vaccine skepticism reflects cost–benefit reasoning rather than intolerance of uncertainty. [pnas](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2024597118)
- **Nan & Daily (2015, *Journal of Health Communication* )** : N = 338 undergraduates, HPV vaccine. Exposure to mixed pro/anti blogs *polarized*  beliefs about vaccine efficacy, and only among high-NFC participants. Those with favorable priors became more positive; those with skeptical priors became more negative. [mdpi](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/14/1/73)[mdpi](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/11/6094) This is a clean demonstration of NFC-driven biased assimilation of conflicting health information. It is cited in Philipp-Muller, Lee & Petty’s (2022, *PNAS* ) review of anti-science attitudes as evidence that high-NFC people “become entrenched in their existing views and reject the mixed information.” [pnas](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.2120755119)
- **Meyer and colleagues (*Episteme* )** : in a sample of 998 US adults using the 15-item Roets & Van Hiel NFC scale, NFC was only modestly related to COVID-19 misinformation endorsement (coefficient 0.09), and an “epistemic vice” scale was “more strongly associated with endorsement of COVID-19 misinformation than other psychological measures, including personality, dogmatism, the Cognitive Reflection Test, and need for closure.”

**Verdict:**  During COVID-19, NFC’s link to misinformation was small. Trust, ideology and conspiracy mentality dominated. The most interesting effect is interactive: under mixed messaging, NFC amplifies polarization in whatever direction prior beliefs point.

#### (d) Resistance to corrections and the continued influence effect

This is the weakest part of the evidence base, despite strong theoretical expectations.

- **The theory.**  Wittenberg & Berinsky’s (2020) chapter in *Social Media and Democracy*  argues that high-NFC individuals “may be more trusting of initial misinformation, which provides closure through explaining the causes of events, and more resistant to corrections, which may sow feelings of confusion and uncertainty” (citing Rapp & Salovich, 2018). [cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/social-media-and-democracy/misinformation-and-its-correction/61FA7FD743784A723BA234533012E810) They may be “especially vulnerable to the continued influence effect” if they cannot tolerate gaps in their mental models.
- **The authors’ own caveat.**  They note that NFC “is primarily used as a control variable in studies of misinformation and is rarely the main construct of interest.” [cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/social-media-and-democracy/misinformation-and-its-correction/61FA7FD743784A723BA234533012E810)
- **CIE researchers flag the gap.**  Rich & Zaragoza’s (2016, *JEP: LMC* ) study of implied vs. explicit misinformation explicitly speculated that individual differences in resistance to correction “may be associated with individual differences in personality (e.g., need for cognitive closure).” [apa](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/xlm-0000155.pdf)
- **The closest direct evidence:**
    - *De keersmaecker & Roets (2017, *Intelligence* ), “‘Fake news’: Incorrect, but hard to correct”* : MTurk, N = 390. After a retraction, lower cognitive ability predicted lingering negative impressions (β = .27 for ability). NFC was entered only as a control, and “controlling for need for closure… did not influence the relationship between cognitive ability and attitude adjustment.” No separate NFC effect was reported. [uw](http://icelab.psych.uw.edu.pl/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Intelligence-and-fake-news.pdf)
    - *Pica, Pierro, Bélanger & Kruglanski (2014, *Social Cognition* , 32, 337–359)* : three experiments showing NFC augments retrieval-induced forgetting, which “in turn magnifies misinformation effects in eyewitness situations.” [apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2014-34316-002)[arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2206.15237v1) This is causal evidence in the Loftus-style memory paradigm, not the news-retraction paradigm.
    - *A Polish forensic-psychology study (Problems of Forensic Sciences, 2019)* : higher NFC was associated with greater susceptibility to post-event misinformation and proneness to false confession. [ies](https://arch.ies.gov.pl/images/PDF/2019/vol_118/118_Hejniak_m.pdf) This is a small, single study.
- **Mechanistic support from the CIE literature.**  Providing a causal alternative explanation is the most effective way to reduce continued influence, because it “fills the causal gap.” [apa](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/xlm-0000155.pdf) This is exactly what NFC theory predicts high-NFC people need, but no study has tested whether alternative explanations help high-NFC people *more* .

**Verdict:**  Whether NFC moderates the CIE is an open empirical question. The prediction is theoretically compelling but untested.

#### (e) Political misinformation, polarization and selective exposure

- **Meirick & Bessarabova (2016, *Analyses of Social Issues and Public Policy* )** : N = 486, 2012 U.S. election. NFC was positively related to Republican-serving misperceptions, but not after control variables were added. [wiley](https://spssi.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/asap.12101) Partisan media exposure was the stronger driver.
- **Ideology as a confound.**  NFC correlates with political conservatism (Jost et al., 2003; Golec de Zavala & Van Bergh, 2007). [tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03637751.2024.2354252) Many apparent NFC–misinformation links may therefore reflect ideology or partisan media diets. The Hungarian pattern (NFC predicting pro-government but not anti-government fake news) points the same way.
- **Group-centrism.**  Kruglanski, Pierro, Mannetti & De Grada (2006, *Psychological Review* ) show that high NFC fosters group-centrism: conformity, preference for homogeneous groups, ingroup favoritism and reliance on ingroup epistemic authorities. This offers a plausible route to partisan selective exposure and echo-chamber belief, but direct tests with misinformation outcomes are sparse.

#### (f) Stereotyping, rumors and memory

- **Stereotyping and primacy.**  Classic lab work shows heightened NFC (via time pressure, noise or the trait) increases stereotypic judgments, primacy effects and anchoring (Kruglanski & Freund, 1983; Webster & Kruglanski, 1994). This is the best-replicated part of the NFC literature and the strongest basis for expecting a misinformation link. It shows that NFC increases reliance on *any*  readily available schema. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_(psychology))
- **Rumors.**  The rumor literature (Allport & Postman; DiFonzo & Bordia) emphasizes that rumors circulate to reduce anxiety under uncertainty. [sagepub](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/processes/chpt/rumor) NFC is a natural moderator, and Kruglanski’s group has studied NFC and trans-generational transmission of group norms (Livi et al., 2015, *OBHDP* ). However, I found little direct, peer-reviewed evidence linking dispositional NFC to rumor *acceptance or transmission*  as such. This connection is better treated as theoretical extrapolation than established finding.
- **Memory-based misinformation.**  Pica et al. (2014) provide the strongest experimental link between NFC and false memory. Their framework treats retrieval-induced forgetting as motivated: high NFC suppresses competing memories to reduce uncertainty, [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2206.15237v1) unless the to-be-forgotten material serves the person’s goals. [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6206073/)

### 4. Nuances, contradictions and related constructs

**Does NFC increase misinformation acceptance specifically, or just confidence?**  The weight of evidence favors the second interpretation. NFC increases the speed and confidence of commitment to *whatever*  explanation is accessible and the resistance to revising it. It produces misinformation acceptance only when the false account is the accessible one. This explains why:

- effects reverse when official explanations are salient (Marchlewska et al., 2018); [wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.2308)
- NFC polarizes rather than uniformly shifts beliefs (Nan & Daily, 2015);
- lowering NFC reduces prior-belief bias in both directions (Leman & Cinnirella, 2013); [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)
- NFC does not affect discrimination in headline tasks where nothing is especially “first” or “salient.”

**Identified moderators:**

- **Availability and salience of explanations:**  the strongest moderator (Marchlewska et al., 2018). [wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.2308)
- **Prior beliefs:**  high NFC amplifies their effect (Leman & Cinnirella, 2013; Nan & Daily, 2015). [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)
- **Type of conspiracy measure:**  general vs. specific (Bowes et al., 2023). [newswise](https://www.newswise.com/pdf_docs/168744596980985_The%20Conspiratorial%20Mind.pdf)
- **Epistemic authority and trust:**  theoretically central. Jedinger & Masch (2025) found trust dominated but did not interact with NFC. [frontiersin](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1447313/pdf)
- **Retention interval:**  the truth effect is amplified by NFC only after short delays (Stump et al., 2021, *Psychological Research* ).
- **Situational inducers:**  time pressure, noise, fatigue and cognitive load raise state NFC. Accountability lowers it. [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/)
- **Education:**  possibly a protective factor that NFC erodes (*PAID* , vol. 251, 2026; non-significant interaction at α = .05).

**Distinguishing NFC from related constructs:**

| Construct | Relationship to NFC | Meta-analytic link to conspiracy belief (Bowes et al., 2023) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Need for cognition (Cacioppo & Petty) | Separate dimension (enjoyment of effortful thought); modest negative correlation with NFC | — |
| Intolerance of ambiguity/uncertainty | Overlaps with the NFC “discomfort with ambiguity” facet, but is affective rather than motivational | r = .25 (stronger than NFC) |
| Dogmatism | Overlaps with closed-mindedness; content-focused rigidity | r = .14 |
| Cognitive reflection (CRT) | A performance measure of overriding intuition; conceptually distinct | r = −.17 |
| Actively open-minded thinking | Conceptually close to the inverse of closed-mindedness, but normative and belief-revision focused | r = −.25 |
| Epistemic motives (Douglas et al., 2017) | NFC is one of several epistemic motives | Epistemic motives overall r = .14 (Biddlestone et al., 2024) |

Measures centered on *belief revision*  (AOT) or *affective*  discomfort (intolerance of ambiguity) outperform NFC. This suggests NFC’s predictive power comes mainly from its closed-mindedness and ambiguity facets, and that the order, predictability and decisiveness facets add noise. The few facet-level studies (e.g., Staszak et al., 2022) support this, but systematic facet analyses are lacking.

**Methodological issues:**

- Heterogeneous scales (42-, 41-, 15- and 5-item versions).
- Heavy reliance on cross-sectional, single-country convenience samples.
- Almost no experimental manipulations of *state*  NFC in misinformation tasks.
- Ideological confounding.
- Evidence of possible publication bias in the closure literature (Biddlestone et al., 2024).

### 5. Implications for interventions

1. **Prebunking and inoculation fit NFC theory best.**  If high-NFC people freeze on the first adequate explanation, being first with accurate information (or with a warning about manipulation techniques) is the strategic priority. Inoculation has been shown to reduce the CIE, especially when corrections come from highly credible sources (*PLOS ONE* , 2022). [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9049321/) NFC-specific inoculation trials have not been done.
2. **Always pair debunks with an alternative causal explanation.**  This is the best-supported correction technique in the CIE literature, and NFC theory says high-NFC audiences need it most. A bare “this is false” leaves a gap that invites re-seizing on the myth.
3. **Communicate uncertainty with structure.**  Crisis communication should offer provisional but clear explanations, timelines for updates and authoritative sources, so official channels can satisfy closure needs. Petersen et al.’s (2021) finding that transparency preserves trust suggests honesty about uncertainty need not backfire.
4. **Avoid “balanced” mixed messaging for high-NFC audiences.**  Nan & Daily (2015) show that presenting conflicting views side-by-side polarizes high-NFC people rather than moderating them.
5. **Reduce situational NFC at the point of judgment.**  Accuracy prompts, accountability cues and slowing down could lower state NFC. Lab work shows accountability reduces NFC-driven bias (Leman & Cinnirella, 2013). [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3694217/) Whether accuracy nudges work *through*  lowering NFC is untested.
6. **Do not target interventions using NFC scores.**  Its predictive validity is too low. Trust, conspiracy mentality, AOT and partisanship are better segmentation variables.

**Open research questions:**

- Does dispositional or induced NFC moderate the CIE, and do alternative explanations help high-NFC people disproportionately? This needs preregistered, high-powered tests.
- Can Marchlewska et al.’s (2018) salience-reversal effect be replicated with real-world misinformation and multi-site samples?
- Which facets drive the effects, using latent-variable models alongside AOT and intolerance of ambiguity?
- Does NFC interact with source credibility and epistemic authority, as LET predicts?
- Can state NFC be manipulated (time pressure, cognitive load) within the headline paradigm and analyzed with signal detection theory to separate discrimination from response bias?
- How does NFC function in non-WEIRD and high-polarization contexts?

## Caveats

- **Evidence strength varies widely.**  The best-replicated findings are basic-lab NFC effects (primacy, stereotyping, resistance to persuasion) and the small meta-analytic NFC–conspiracy correlation. The salience-reversal effect, NFC-driven polarization, NFC effects on eyewitness misinformation and NFC-amplified truth effects at short delays each rest on one paper or one lab.
- **Some figures come from secondary sources.**  Bowes et al. (2023) figures were taken from the APA author PDF. De keersmaecker et al. (2020) results are from abstracts and secondary reports, and NFC coefficients from Yan et al. (2023, *Media Psychology* ) could not be verified. The Szebeni et al. (2021) regression table contains an internal inconsistency (reported t does not match B/SE).
- **Most studies are correlational,**  so third variables (ideology, education, trust) may explain apparent NFC effects.

## Summary: How strong is the relationship?

The relationship between need for cognitive closure and misinformation acceptance is **real, small and conditional rather than general.**  Meta-analytically it sits at about r = .10 for conspiracy beliefs, at the low end of epistemic predictors. In fake-news headline studies it is often null after controls, and it does not moderate the illusory truth effect over typical delays. In COVID-19 data it is dwarfed by institutional trust. NFC is better understood as an **amplifier of commitment** : it makes people seize on the most accessible explanation and freeze on prior beliefs, which leads to misinformation acceptance when falsehoods arrive first or fit existing views, and to resistance to misinformation when accurate accounts do. The most important unanswered question, whether NFC makes corrections less effective, has barely been tested. Practically, NFC argues for getting accurate explanations out early and always filling the gap a debunk leaves, rather than for treating high-NFC individuals as a distinct “gullible” group.
