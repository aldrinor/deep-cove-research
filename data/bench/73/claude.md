# A New Paradigm of Holistic Empowerment in Elementary English Education and Teaching
## Integrating Core Competencies, Teacher Growth, Responsible Technology, and Learning Ecosystems: A Practitioner's Framework for Novice Teachers

Holistic empowerment works in elementary English only when four things change together: what students do in class, how teachers grow, how technology is used, and how assessment, homework, and families support learning. China's 2022 English Curriculum Standards already call for all four. The weak point in most schools is putting them into practice coherently, and novice teachers feel that gap most.

**Assumptions applied.** (1) Setting: English as a foreign language (EFL) in primary school, anchored in China's *Compulsory Education English Curriculum Standards (2022 Edition)* (义务教育英语课程标准 2022年版), with international frameworks (CEFR Pre-A1/A1, Cambridge Young Learners, TBLT, CLIL) for transferability. (2) "Holistic empowerment" covers four connected dimensions: students, teachers, technology, and the learning ecosystem. (3) The paper is practitioner-oriented, for a school-based teaching-research project or a frontline teachers' journal, and structured for novice teachers. All vignettes, sample plans, and rubrics are **illustrative examples written by the author**, not reported empirical findings.

## TL;DR

- Holistic empowerment means developing the four core competencies (language ability, cultural awareness, thinking capacity, learning ability) through unit-based activity chains (learning and understanding → applying and practising → transferring and creating), with teacher development, careful technology use, and assessment and home–school reform designed together rather than added one by one.
- Evidence is strongest for formative assessment, structured collaborative professional development, and balanced input/output practice. It is moderate for AI pronunciation feedback. For AI lesson-planning time savings it is moderate but growing: two Education Endowment Foundation trials, both given a "high" security rating, found time savings without loss of quality, which the EEF calls "far from a 'silver bullet'". It is thin or contested for growth-mindset interventions and for generative AI's direct effect on young children's learning.
- Novice teachers should master routines and one well-designed unit per term, rely on the teaching-research group (教研组) and a mentor, keep AI in the teacher's hands (China's 2025 guidance bars primary pupils from independent use of open-ended generative AI), and collect simple classroom evidence from the start.

## Abstract

Elementary English teaching in many EFL contexts still suffers from fragmented "knowledge-point" instruction, test-oriented practice, passive learners, uneven teacher capacity, and superficial technology use. China's 2022 Standards changed the subject's goal from "overall language ability" to four core competencies (language ability, cultural awareness, thinking capacity, learning ability), and introduced the English Learning Activity Approach, thematic unit organisation, and teaching-learning-assessment alignment.\[1\] This paper proposes a "Holistic Empowerment" paradigm that puts these reforms into practice across four interdependent dimensions: student empowerment, teacher empowerment, technology- and AI-empowered teaching, and learning-ecosystem empowerment (assessment, homework, home–school collaboration, reading). Drawing on second language acquisition theory, task-based and content-integrated approaches, formative assessment and professional development research, and 2023–2026 guidance from UNESCO, the OECD, and China's Ministry of Education, it sets out principles, a conceptual model, and an old-versus-new comparison. It then offers practical tools: grade-banded strategies, a worked illustrative Grade 4 unit plan, lesson-study and mentoring structures, a three-year roadmap for novice teachers, AI workflows with safeguards, a sample rubric, "double reduction"-compatible homework, and checklists. It argues that the dimensions must be designed together, because gains in one (for example, AI-generated materials) are lost without changes in others (teacher judgement, formative assessment). It closes by marking where evidence is strong, where it is thin, and how teachers can build local evidence.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The problem as seen from the classroom

After many years in elementary English classrooms, I see the same problems in urban and rural schools alike:

1. **Fragmented knowledge-point teaching.** Lessons revolve around isolated words and patterns ("Today: *apple, banana*; *I like…*") with little coherent meaning across a unit. Children recite but cannot use the language.
2. **Test orientation.** Practice in higher grades narrows to dictation, gap-fills, and memorised texts.
3. **Passive learners.** In classes of 45–55, the teacher talks, a few volunteers answer, and the class repeats. Many children rarely produce a sentence of their own.
4. **Uneven teacher capacity.** Many primary English teachers, especially rural ones, teach several grades or subjects. A survey of 262 primary EFL teachers in south-eastern China found no correlation between their self-efficacy and their measured knowledge of basic language constructs (Luo et al., 2020): confidence and knowledge do not always match.\[2\]
5. **Superficial technology use.** Slides replace the blackboard without changing how children learn; generative AI raises that risk further.
6. **Novice-teacher struggle.** New teachers often get the hardest classes, heavy non-teaching duties, and little structured mentoring.

### 1.2 Why a new paradigm, and why "holistic"

The 2022 Standards already set the direction. Built around four core competencies, they organise content by themes ("man and self," "man and society," "man and nature"), promote an activity-based approach moving from "learning and understanding" to "applying and practising" to "transferring and creating," emphasise teaching-learning-assessment alignment, and call for deeper ICT integration (Ministry of Education [MOE], 2022; English translation hosted by the British Council).\[3\]

Policy alone does not change classrooms. Reforms fail when introduced as separate campaigns: a "unit design" training this term, an "AI classroom" competition next term, a new homework rule after that. Each makes sense alone; together, uncoordinated, they overload teachers and confuse children. "Holistic empowerment" means designing the reforms as one system in which each part strengthens the others.

### 1.3 Research questions

1. What theoretical and empirical foundations support a holistic empowerment paradigm for elementary EFL?
2. What does the paradigm look like in practice for students, teachers, technology, and the ecosystem?
3. How can novice teachers implement it within real constraints?
4. Where is the evidence strong or thin, and what should teachers and researchers do next?

### 1.4 Significance

For **novice teachers**, the paper offers a map, tools, and realistic expectations. For **teaching-research groups**, it offers a shared framework for collective preparation and lesson study. For **policy implementation**, it shows how the 2022 Standards, the 2021 "double reduction" policy, and the 2025 AI guidance fit together in daily practice. Its international grounding makes it transferable to other EFL primary contexts.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations and Literature Review

### 2.1 Scaffolding and the ZPD

Vygotsky's (1978) zone of proximal development is the gap between what a child can do alone and with support; "scaffolding" (Wood, Bruner, & Ross, 1976) is temporary support that bridges it and is then removed. EFL scaffolds include pictures, gestures, sentence frames, modelling, mind maps, and peers. The 2022 activity chain can be read as a scaffolding sequence: heavy support in "learning and understanding," less in "applying," minimal in "creating."

### 2.2 Comprehensible input and balanced practice

Krashen's (1982) input hypothesis and "affective filter" are hard to test, but their classroom implications (plenty of understandable input, low anxiety) are widely accepted for young learners. Nation's (2007) "four strands" are more operational: a balanced course gives roughly equal time to **meaning-focused input**, **meaning-focused output**, **language-focused learning**, and **fluency development**.\[4\] Many Chinese primary classrooms are heavily weighted toward language-focused learning; the four strands are a simple audit tool to rebalance.

### 2.3 Teaching languages to young children

Cameron (2001) argues that children's foreign-language learning is driven by meaning and social activity; children are enthusiastic but have short attention spans and limited ability to analyse language, so teachers must build discourse (stories, routines, dialogues), not just vocabulary. Total Physical Response (Asher, 1969) remains useful in Grades 1–3 because it ties comprehension to movement without forcing early speech. Picture books provide repeated, meaningful, emotionally engaging input. In China, Wang, Chen, and Qi (2020) describe a national project introducing graded readers alongside mandated coursebooks, with a case study of 290 Grade 4 students and 8 teachers in a suburban Beijing school from 2017 to 2019.\[5\]\[6\]

**Phonics** is widely used but unevenly understood. A survey of 213 primary EFL teachers in two provinces found most held positive attitudes but saw phonics mainly as a "word-attack skill," and that materials and strategies were closely linked to its effectiveness (Zhong & Kang, 2021).\[7\] Phonics should be taught systematically and always connected back to meaningful reading.

### 2.4 Task-based teaching and CLIL

Task-based language teaching organises learning around tasks with a communicative outcome (Ellis, 2003; Willis, 1996); Content and Language Integrated Learning teaches subject content through the target language (Coyle, Hood, & Marsh, 2010). Both share the activity approach's emphasis on real situations. With young beginners, tasks must be short, scaffolded, and visual; "task-supported" teaching embedded in a textbook unit is more realistic than a pure task-based syllabus.

### 2.5 The 2022 Standards' constructs

- **Core competencies.** Language ability is the foundation, cultural awareness concerns value orientation, thinking capacity reflects intellectual development, and learning ability is "the key driving force behind them all" (MOE, 2022). A preparatory level for Grades 1–2 focuses on viewing, listening, and speaking; Level 1 covers Grades 3–4 and Level 2 Grades 5–6.\[3\]
- **English Learning Activity Approach.** Students take part in "a series of interrelated and progressive language learning activities" around thematic meaning, moving through learning and understanding, applying and practising, and transferring and creating (MOE, 2022).\[3\]
- **Unit-based teaching and big ideas.** Yao and Wang (2023) describe competence-oriented teaching as "formulating the mainline of the unit and setting up teaching aims at different levels."\[8\] Wang Qiang and colleagues' big-idea unit design, developed for senior high school, has been widely adapted to primary schools.\[9\]
- **Teaching-learning-assessment alignment.** Teachers should embed assessment throughout teaching, help students become "designers, participants, and collaborators" in assessment, and combine formative and summative assessment (MOE, 2022).\[3\]

Wang and Luo describe the 2017 senior high standards as moving "beyond a focus on language abilities to a focus on developing students' core competencies";\[10\] the 2022 standards extend this logic downward, within China's broader curriculum reform (Luo et al., 2023).\[11\]

**Evidence note.** I found no rigorous, English-language, peer-reviewed evaluation of the activity approach or unit-based teaching in Grades 1–6. Most work is conceptual or single-lesson case description, some in low-tier outlets (e.g., Huang, Yang, & Yang, 2024).\[12\] This gap is an opportunity for school-based research.

### 2.6 Differentiation, UDL, and SEL

Differentiated instruction adjusts content, process, and product to learners' readiness and interests (Tomlinson, 2014). CAST's *UDL Guidelines 3.0* (July 2024) keep the three principles of multiple means of **engagement**, **representation**, and **action and expression**, adding attention to identity, belonging, and "joy and play" (CAST, 2024).\[13\] CASEL's social-emotional learning framework covers self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making; a meta-analysis of 213 school-based SEL programmes with over 270,000 students found an 11-percentile-point achievement gain (Durlak et al., 2011).\[14\] Role-play, turn-taking, and expressing feelings are both language and SEL goals.

### 2.7 Growth mindset, with caution

Dweck's (2006) growth-mindset theory is popular, but evidence for stand-alone interventions is contested. Macnamara and Burgoyne's (2023) meta-analysis found no significant effect on achievement in the highest-quality studies (d̄ = 0.02);\[15\] Tipton et al. (2023) reanalysed the data and argued for meaningful effects in at-risk subgroups.\[16\] Practically: praise effort and strategy and normalise mistakes, but do not expect a poster or one-off lesson to raise English achievement.

### 2.8 Formative assessment

Black and Wiliam (1998) reported typical formative-assessment effect sizes of 0.4 to 0.7, "larger than most of those found for educational interventions," often with the biggest gains for lower achievers.\[17\]\[18\]\[19\]\[20\] The cycle is: clarify goals, elicit evidence, give feedback that moves learning forward, and activate students as resources for themselves and peers. This underpins teaching-learning-assessment alignment.

### 2.9 Teacher knowledge and professional development

Shulman's (1986) **pedagogical content knowledge (PCK)** is knowing how to make specific content understandable to specific learners, such as knowing that Chinese children confuse /θ/ and /s/. Mishra and Koehler (2006) extended it to **TPACK**, adding technological knowledge. Darling-Hammond, Hyler, and Gardner (2017), reviewing 35 rigorous studies, found that effective professional development is content focused, uses active learning, supports collaboration, models effective practice, provides coaching, offers feedback and reflection, and is sustained.\[21\]

China's **teaching-research system** (教研) matches these features: since the 1950s, subject teaching-research groups (教研组) and grade lesson-preparation groups (备课组) have organised collective planning, open lessons, and peer observation, a Chinese form of lesson study (cf. Lewis, 2002, on the Japanese model).\[22\]\[23\] Reflective practice (Schön, 1983; Farrell, 2015) turns experience into learning. On **induction**, Ingersoll and Strong's (2011) review of 15 empirical studies found most supported positive effects on retention, practice, and achievement, depending on the kind and number of supports.\[24\]\[25\]

### 2.10 Technology and AI: current evidence

- **Policy.** UNESCO's *Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research* (2023) recommends a human-centred, age-appropriate approach and proposes 13 as a minimum age for independent classroom use.\[26\]\[27\] In May 2025, China's MOE issued the *Guidelines for the Use of Generative AI in Primary and Secondary Schools (2025)* together with guidelines on AI general education (Global Times, 12 May 2025). The issuing body was the MOE's Basic Education Teaching Steering Committee. The guidelines **prohibit "students from independently using open-ended content generation at primary schools"** and ban "teachers from using generative AI as a substitute for their core teaching responsibilities" (People's Daily Online, 2025; Global Times, 2025).
- **Learning effects.** The *OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026* finds generative AI can support learning "when guided by clear teaching principles," but outsourcing tasks to it improves performance "with no real learning gains"; in one reported experiment, students practising with a general chatbot did better in practice but worse on later closed-book tests (OECD, 2026).\[28\]\[29\]
- **Pronunciation.** A meta-analysis of 15 studies (38 effect sizes) found a medium effect of automatic speech recognition (ASR) on pronunciation (g = 0.69), larger with explicit feedback, longer treatment, and peer practice, and clearest for adults and intermediate learners; short treatments showed no advantage (Ngo, Chen, & Lai, 2024).\[30\]\[31\] Evidence for young beginners is weaker.
- **Teacher workload.** In an Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) trial with 259 secondary science teachers in 68 schools, ChatGPT plus a guide cut weekly preparation time from 81.5 to 56.2 minutes (31%) with no drop in rated resource quality (EEF, 2024).\[32\]\[33\] A follow-up EEF/NFER randomised trial ran in autumn 2025 with 464 Key Stage 2 (Years 3–6) teachers in 108 schools, testing Oak National Academy's Aila tool. Aila users spent "76% of the time on resource and lesson preparation" that non-users did, about 2.5 hours against 3 hours 19 minutes a week, "an average saving of around 49 minutes a week", although use declined over the 10 weeks (EEF press release, October 2026). The EEF still warned that "time saved does not necessarily mean better planning or teaching."
- **Reading.** In a 6-month quasi-experiment with 85 Grade 5 pupils in Hangzhou, a class reading bilingual e-picture books with an AI robot gained more in reading motivation, amount, and comprehension than a paper-book class (Feng & Wang, 2023).\[34\] It is a single-school study; novelty effects cannot be ruled out.
- **Infrastructure.** China's National Smart Education Platform (国家智慧教育平台), launched in March 2022, reported over 164 million registered users and over 110,000 primary and secondary resources by April 2025 (MOE, 2025).\[35\]\[36\]

## 3. The "Holistic Empowerment" Paradigm

### 3.1 Definition

**Holistic empowerment** in elementary English is *a coordinated design approach that develops students' core competencies and whole-person growth by simultaneously strengthening (a) students' agency and capability, (b) teachers' professional judgement and collaboration, (c) purposeful, safe technology use, and (d) an ecosystem of assessment, homework, family, and reading support, each designed to reinforce the others.* "Empowerment" means increasing real **capability**, **agency**, and **support**, not leaving children alone or giving teachers more slogans.

### 3.2 Guiding principles

1. **Competency over coverage:** plan from the core competencies and the unit's big idea, not the word list.
2. **Meaning first:** every language-focused activity feeds a meaningful use.
3. **Graduated responsibility:** scaffold, then remove the scaffold.
4. **Every child speaks, every lesson:** pair work, choral-to-individual, mini-whiteboards.
5. **Assessment is part of teaching:** success criteria and checks built in from the start.
6. **Teachers learn in community:** the 教研组 is the engine.
7. **Technology serves pedagogy, under adult control.**
8. **Whole child, whole community:** wellbeing, values (立德树人), and family partnership are goals, not add-ons.
9. **Evidence-informed and honest:** prefer strong-evidence practices, pilot weaker ones, collect local data.

### 3.3 Conceptual model

At the centre is a **core engine: unit-based activity chains with built-in teaching-learning-assessment alignment**, aimed at the goal of core competencies plus whole-person growth. Four dimensions surround and feed it: teachers design it, technology supplies resources and feedback, the ecosystem extends it beyond the classroom, and students drive it through participation. Each dimension also connects directly to its neighbours (students ↔ teachers ↔ technology ↔ ecosystem ↔ students).

| Dimension | Key question | Main levers | Indicators of success |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student | Can every child understand, use, and create with English, and want to? | Activity chains, tasks, stories, differentiation, strategy training, SEL | Talk time per child, task completion, reading volume, enjoyment |
| Teacher | Can the teacher design, diagnose, adapt, and keep growing? | Lesson study, mentoring, reflection, observation, PCK/TPACK | Unit-plan quality, use of formative evidence, confidence, retention |
| Technology | Does the tool add learning value safely? | Smart Education Platform, ASR, levelled texts, AI planning support | Time saved and reinvested, feedback quality, no privacy breaches |
| Ecosystem | Do assessment, homework, families, and reading reinforce class learning? | Rubrics, portfolios, policy-compliant homework, family reading, class library | Homework done without burden, parent understanding, books read |

### 3.4 Why the dimensions must work together

- **AI materials without teacher PCK:** a novice produces polished levelled texts but cannot tell when a "Level A" text still has six unknown words; the OECD's (2026) performance-without-learning warning applies to teachers' tools too.
- **Activity-approach lessons without assessment reform:** if the term test rewards only dictation, teachers and parents drift back to drilling.
- **Homework cuts without an ecosystem:** if written homework disappears and nothing meaningful replaces it, some families buy private alternatives and equity worsens.

### 3.5 Old versus new paradigm

| Aspect | Traditional paradigm | Holistic empowerment paradigm |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Knowledge points, scores | Core competencies and whole-person growth |
| Planning unit | Single lesson, word list | Unit with a big idea and a meaningful final task |
| Activity design | Present–drill–test | Understand → apply → transfer and create |
| Student role | Receiver, repeater | Participant, creator, self-assessor |
| Teacher role | Transmitter, sole designer | Designer, diagnostician, coach, community member |
| Assessment | End-of-unit test | Embedded checks, rubrics, portfolios, fair summative tests |
| Homework | Copying, workbook pages | Short oral/reading/practical tasks within policy limits |
| Technology | Digital blackboard or uncritical AI | Purposeful, teacher-controlled tools with safeguards |
| Differentiation | One pace for all | Tiered tasks, flexible grouping, UDL options |
| Family | Homework checkers | Reading partners and informed collaborators |
| Teacher growth | One-off training | Sustained lesson study, mentoring, reflection |

## 4. Student Empowerment in Practice

### 4.1 Grade-banded strategies

**Grades 1–2 (preparatory level).** Daily routines and TPR (greetings, feelings check-ins, "Touch your nose"); songs, chants, and rhymes; repeated read-alouds of patterned picture books; phonological awareness games (rhymes, initial sounds, clapping syllables) before formal phonics; role-play corners (shop, doctor, picnic). No written homework, by policy.\[37\]

**Grades 3–4 (Level 1).** Systematic phonics in a planned sequence (short vowels → blends → digraphs), applied immediately in decodable sentences and stories; situational units (birthday party, zoo visit); short tasks (information gaps, class surveys); three-level questions ("What does Sam eat?" → "Why does Sam feel sick?" → "What would you tell Sam?"); cultural comparisons of breakfasts, festivals, and greetings, starting from children's own lives.

**Grades 5–6 (Level 2).** Thematic narrative and expository texts with graphic organisers; projects (a "travel guide to our town," a festival poster exhibition); strategy training (prediction, guessing from context, planning and checking writing); graded readers and reading circles; model-based paragraph writing with peer checklists.

### 4.2 The activity chain as planning backbone

| Level (2022 Standards) | Typical verbs | Primary-classroom examples |
|---|---|---|
| Learning and understanding (学习理解) | notice, retrieve, sort, summarise | Picture walk and predict; listen and point; complete a mind map |
| Applying and practising (应用实践) | describe, explain, role-play | Retell with picture prompts; pair dialogues with frames; act out the story |
| Transferring and creating (迁移创新) | reason, evaluate, create | Design a menu for a friend; write a new ending; present a survey |

A common novice error is rushing to "creating" before children have enough language, or never reaching it. A rough rule: in a four-to-six-lesson unit, the first lessons are mainly understanding, the middle ones mainly applying, and the last one or two mainly creating.

### 4.3 Differentiation in large, mixed-ability classes

- **Same goal, different support:** word banks, sentence frames, or a partner; tiered worksheets (★/★★/★★★) children can choose.
- **Flexible grouping:** mixed groups for creative tasks; brief same-level groups for targeted practice.
- **Group roles:** reader, writer, speaker, checker, so quieter learners have a defined contribution.
- **UDL options:** present language with picture, sound, and gesture; accept drawn, spoken, or written answers.
- **Extension cards** for fast finishers instead of "more of the same."

### 4.4 Motivation and classroom management

Predictable routines (start-up song, silence signal, materials monitors) lower anxiety and save minutes. Keep rules few and positive, and practise them like language. Praise effort and strategy specifically ("You used the picture to guess the word"). Use team points sparingly and never to shame. Give brain breaks every 10–15 minutes in Grades 1–3. Teach SEL language ("I feel… because…", "Can I help you?", "It's OK to make mistakes").

### 4.5 Illustrative example: Grade 4 unit plan

*An illustrative plan written for this paper, not a report of an implemented study.*

**Theme:** *Healthy Food, Happy Me* (Man and self: life and learning). **Level:** Grade 4, Level 1. **Time:** 5 × 40 minutes.
**Big idea (大观念):** *Healthy choices help me grow, and sharing food is a way to care for others.*
**Driving question:** *How can we plan a healthy, tasty lunch for a friend?*
**Final task:** Groups of four design and present a "Healthy Lunch Box" for a classmate, explaining choices in English.

**Objectives.** Students will be able to:
- *Language ability:* use 12–15 food words and "I like / I don't like…", "Do you like…?", "…is good for you"; identify key information in an illustrated story; write 3–4 model-based sentences.
- *Cultural awareness:* compare lunches in China and one or two other countries; respect different food habits.
- *Thinking capacity:* classify foods ("eat more / some / a little"); justify and evaluate choices.
- *Learning ability:* use picture clues and word banks; self-assess with a checklist; cooperate in a group role.

| Lesson | Main level | Key activities | Assessment point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Understanding | Mystery bag; food chant; listen and point; favourite-food survey; mind map | Mini-whiteboard check of 6 words; thumbs self-rating |
| 2 | Understanding → applying | Picture book about a boy who eats only sweets; predict, sequence, three-level questions; phonics /ʃ/ vs /tʃ/ | Sequencing task; exit card "Why did Tom feel sick?" |
| 3 | Applying | Sort foods; pair dialogues with frames; information-gap menus; canteen role-play | Teacher checklist; "two stars and a wish" |
| 4 | Applying → transferring | Lunches in China, Japan, the UK; Venn diagram; partner interviews | Venn review; oral checklist |
| 5 | Creating | Design lunch box (drawing, labels, 3–4 sentences); gallery walk; presentations | Rubric (Section 7.3); self- and peer assessment |

**Lesson 2 sequence:** chant and TPR warm-up (4 min) → cover prediction (6) → teacher read-aloud, pupils check predictions (8) → pairs sequence picture cards, retrieval questions (8) → inference and evaluation with frame "Tom should eat… because…" (8) → sh/ch sort (4) → exit card (2).

**Differentiation.** *Support:* picture word banks, desk frames, retelling buddy, draw-and-label option. *Extension:* add comparisons ("Rice is healthier than chips because…"); write a class menu card. *UDL:* poster, teacher-recorded video, or live presentation.

**Values.** Respect for food habits (no "yuck" words), not wasting food (光盘行动), kindness in planning for a friend.

**Homework.** Tell family three food words; sort fridge foods into "more / some / a little" with a parent; optionally read the story aloud at home. Each under 15 minutes.

**Reflection.** Which children spoke least? Did the final task need the unit language? What evidence changed my next step?

## 5. Teacher Empowerment in Practice

### 5.1 Collective preparation and lesson study

The 教研组/备课组 system embodies most of Darling-Hammond et al.'s (2017) features when used well; used poorly, it becomes copying a "master plan." An effective four-week cycle (illustrative):

1. **Study:** choose one unit and one student-learning problem ("Why can't our Grade 5s retell in their own words?"); read the relevant Standards section.
2. **Plan:** co-design the big idea, activity chain, and assessment points; anticipate student responses.
3. **Teach and observe:** one teacher teaches; colleagues each track three or four focus students, recording what they say, do, and produce.
4. **Reflect** on evidence of student learning, not the teacher's "performance."
5. **Revise and reteach** with another class; file a one-page summary.

### 5.2 Observation protocol (illustrative)

| Focus | What to record |
|---|---|
| Student talk | How many children produce a full meaningful utterance |
| Activity chain | Which level each activity serves; whether "creating" gets enough time |
| Thinking | Count of retrieval, inference, and evaluation questions |
| Assessment | Checks for understanding and how the teacher used the results |
| Inclusion | Participation of quiet or struggling focus students |

### 5.3 Mentoring

Pair each novice with a same-grade mentor; protect at least one co-planning session and one observation per fortnight in year one; use "I do, we do, you do" (watch, co-teach, be observed); focus on one or two goals at a time; keep developmental feedback separate from appraisal where possible. Induction effects depend on the quality and combination of supports (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011):\[24\] a mentor who never observes is not a mentor.

### 5.4 Reflective practice

Write a short journal entry after two lessons a week: *What happened? What evidence of learning do I have? Why might it have happened? What will I try next?* (Schön, 1983; Farrell, 2015). Video-record one lesson a month, with permission and without publishing pupils' images.

### 5.5 PCK and TPACK growth paths

| Area | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content | Accurate pronunciation; phonics sequence | Child-level grammar; text structure | Selecting graded readers and supplementary texts |
| PCK | Common learner errors; modelling, TPR | Three-level questions; scaffolding retelling and writing | Big-idea units and performance tasks |
| TPACK | Platform resources, audio | ASR and levelled texts under teacher control | Data-informed formative cycles; mentoring others on safe AI use |

### 5.6 Teacher agency and wellbeing

"Double reduction" brought more after-school service hours for many teachers, and reviews note workload concerns (Xue & Makela, 2024; Chen & Lin, 2024).\[38\] Leaders should protect planning time, cut duplicated paperwork, and count lesson study as core work. Teachers can share resources across the grade group, batch-prepare per unit, and use AI for drafting, not finishing.

### 5.7 A "first three years" roadmap

| Phase | Priorities | Milestones | Support to request |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1, Term 1 | Routines, clear instructions, accurate modelling | Routines run unprompted by Week 6; one pair activity per lesson; journal twice weekly | Weekly mentor check-in; observe 6+ colleagues |
| Year 1, Term 2 | Lesson → unit; basic formative checks | One full unit reviewed by the group; weekly exit cards | Co-plan a unit; one observed lesson with feedback |
| Year 2 | Questioning, tiered tasks, rubrics; first lesson study | Two units with tiered tasks; one student co-designed rubric; teach a research lesson | Join a 教研组 research question; attend district open lessons |
| Year 3 | Big-idea units, projects, portfolios, purposeful technology; action research | One project unit with portfolio; one action-research report; help mentor a newcomer | School research project; present at district 教研 |

## 6. Technology- and AI-Empowered Teaching

### 6.1 Principles

1. **Pedagogy first:** start from the goal, then ask whether a tool helps.
2. **Teacher control at primary level:** per MOE (2025), pupils do not independently use open-ended generative AI; teachers may use it for preparation and careful demonstrations.\[39\]
3. **Verify everything:** AI output can contain errors, unnatural English, or bias.
4. **Protect privacy:** never enter pupils' names, photos, or voices into public AI tools.
5. **Limit screen time:** keep technology segments short and purposeful.
6. **Equity:** never require devices or paid apps for homework.

### 6.2 Uses that add value

| Use | Value | Evidence | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smart Education Platform resources | Free, curriculum-aligned materials | Wide reach; little outcome data | Adapt; don't just play videos |
| Supervised ASR pronunciation practice | Individual feedback in big classes | Moderate, adult-heavy | Struggles with child voices; peer practice works better |
| AI-levelled texts and question sets (teacher use) | Faster differentiation | Promising on workload | Check vocabulary load and accuracy |
| AI-assisted lesson drafting | Time savings | RCT evidence (secondary) | Teacher judgement essential |

### 6.3 Example workflows

**A. Three-level reading texts (teacher only).** Prompt: *"Rewrite this story for Grade 4 EFL learners at three levels. Level A: 60–80 words, present simple, mostly words from this list [paste]. Level B: 100–120 words plus five new food words. Level C: 150 words with one 'because' sentence. Keep the plot; no brand names."* Then check unfamiliar-word load (aim for about 95–98% known words), grammar, and cultural content; read aloud for rhythm; add pictures. All groups share the same plot, so the whole class can do one post-reading task.

**B. Activity-level questions.** Prompt for 4 retrieval questions, 3 applying prompts (retell, role-play), and 2 creating tasks in simple English; then remove ambiguous items, ensure creating tasks need the unit language, and add frames for weaker learners.

**C. Pronunciation.** Choose 8–10 items with a known difficulty (/θ/ vs /s/, final consonants); model and practise; then pairs share one teacher-controlled device, one speaking and one watching the feedback; the teacher corrects where the tool fails. Keep it to 5–8 minutes over several weeks, since evidence favours explicit feedback, peer practice, and sustained use (Ngo et al., 2024).\[30\]

**D. Unit drafting.** Let AI draft an outline from your big idea and objectives, then critique it in the 备课组. The EEF trial's savings came from targeted use (one or two activities per lesson) after a short learning period, with teachers checking quality.\[40\]

### 6.4 Tool evaluation questions

Does it serve a clear unit goal? Is the English accurate and the audio clear? Is content age-appropriate and culturally respectful? What data does it collect and where is it stored? Does it work for all students? Does it increase active language use or just screen time? Is it consistent with policy on AI for primary pupils?

### 6.5 Safeguards and ethics

No independent open-ended generative AI for primary pupils (MOE, 2025); UNESCO's (2023) age of 13 is an international reference.\[26\]\[41\] Teach AI literacy through teacher-led demonstrations, such as spotting an error in an AI-made sentence together. Tell parents which tools are used and why. Both the OECD (2026) and MOE insist AI must not replace the teacher's core role;\[42\]\[43\] with young children, relationship and interaction *are* the learning.

## 7. Assessment and Ecosystem Empowerment

### 7.1 Teaching-learning-assessment integration

The 2022 Standards ask that teaching and learning "complement and improve each other" through assessment (MOE, 2022).\[3\] In practice, write the assessment evidence before designing activities (backward design: Wiggins & McTighe, 2005) and place quick checks at each stage of the activity chain.

### 7.2 Formative tools for large classes

Mini-whiteboards (everyone answers at once); traffic-light or thumbs self-ratings; exit cards; "two stars and a wish" peer feedback; observation checklists covering 8–10 children per lesson so every child is seen weekly; hinge questions that decide whether to move on; "Today I can…" goal displays.

### 7.3 Illustrative rubric: Grade 4 "Healthy Lunch Box" presentation

| Criterion | ★ Beginning | ★★ Developing | ★★★ Achieving |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content and ideas (thinking) | Names some foods | Names and groups foods | Groups foods and gives a reason |
| Language use | Single words, with help | Short sentences using unit frames, some errors | Clear sentences; errors don't block meaning |
| Pronunciation and delivery | Hard to understand | Mostly understandable | Clear, audible, eye contact |
| Cultural respect and care | — | Mentions friend's likes | Balances friend's likes and health; respectful food language |
| Teamwork (learning ability) | Little part | Did own role | Did own role and helped others |

Show ★★★ examples beforehand, let children self-assess with stickers, and give one specific next step. For Grades 1–2, use smiley-face versions and oral feedback only.

### 7.4 Portfolios

A paper folder or class digital album with unit "can-do" checklists (loosely aligned with CEFR Pre-A1/A1 or the Standards' level goals), best work samples, a reading log, one oral recording per term on school-approved systems, and self-reflection cards ("I'm proud of… / Next I want to…"). Portfolios build reflection and give parents more than scores.

### 7.5 Homework under "double reduction"

The 2021 *Opinions on Further Reducing the Burden of Homework and Off-Campus Training for Students in Compulsory Education* bans written homework in Grades 1–2 and caps average daily written homework in Grades 3–6 at 60 minutes across all subjects (Chen & Lin, 2024).\[37\]\[44\] English homework must be short and high-value:

| Type | Example | Grades |
|---|---|---|
| Listening/oral | Sing the unit song to family; "teach your parent three words" | 1–6 |
| Reading | 10 minutes of a graded reader plus reading log | 3–6 |
| Practical/creative | Label five items at home; draw a comic with a new story ending | 2–6 |
| Choice board | Pick 2 of 6 tasks at different levels each week | 3–6 |

The shift is from quantity of copying to quality of use, with teachers giving feedback on what they assign.

### 7.6 Home–school collaboration

Explain to parents why the class uses stories, projects, and rubrics, and what they can do (listen, praise, read together) even without English. Send one short weekly WeChat message with a song link and a family activity; avoid daily score rankings. Design tasks a child can do alone or with any family member, including grandparents. Celebrate effort, not ranks.

### 7.7 Reading ecosystem

Build a colour-levelled class library with borrowing routines; schedule 10 minutes of sustained or paired reading; read aloud weekly to all grades; run reading circles in Grades 5–6; use free platform resources where devices exist. Wang, Chen, and Qi's (2020) Beijing project shows graded readers can run alongside mandated coursebooks rather than replacing them, a realistic model for most schools.\[5\]

## 8. Implementation Guide for Novice Teachers

### 8.1 Ten steps for one unit

1. Read the unit and your grade's level goals in the Standards.
2. Find the big idea.
3. Design a final task that needs the unit language.
4. Write one or two objectives per competency.
5. Define the assessment evidence for each.
6. Plan the activity chain across lessons.
7. Add support, extension, and UDL options.
8. Plan policy-compliant homework.
9. Get 备课组 feedback.
10. Teach, collect evidence, reflect, revise.

### 8.2 Lesson design checklist

- [ ] "Today I can…" goal linked to the big idea
- [ ] New language presented in context with visuals and models
- [ ] Activities progress from understanding to applying (and creating where ready)
- [ ] Every child says or writes at least one meaningful sentence
- [ ] At least one check for understanding, with a fallback plan
- [ ] Three-level questions planned
- [ ] Support and extension planned
- [ ] Closure with self-assessment; technology only where it adds value

### 8.3 Classroom routine checklist

- [ ] Start-up routine and attention signal, taught and practised
- [ ] Instructions: demonstrate → have a child repeat → start
- [ ] Pair/group formation and materials roles
- [ ] Noise-level system and "when finished" options
- [ ] Positive behaviour system without humiliation
- [ ] End-of-lesson routine

### 8.4 Assessment checklist

- [ ] Success criteria shared with examples
- [ ] Checks at each stage of the activity chain
- [ ] Specific, actionable feedback ("Next time, add *because*")
- [ ] Self- and peer-assessment at least once per unit
- [ ] Records kept (notes, exit cards, portfolio samples)
- [ ] Summative assessment covers all four skills appropriately
- [ ] Evidence used to adjust the next lesson

### 8.5 Common pitfalls

| Pitfall | How to avoid |
|---|---|
| Many games, little learning | Link every activity to the goal; fewer, deeper activities |
| Skipping "learning and understanding" | Ensure enough input and modelling first |
| Final task doesn't need unit language | Write the final task first, then check its demands |
| Teacher talks most of the lesson | Plan pair work; time your own talk |
| Only fast learners answer | Mini-whiteboards, random selection, think–pair–share |
| Copying AI output unchecked | Always verify level, accuracy, cultural fit |
| Homework as copying | Choice boards, oral/reading tasks; explain to parents |

### 8.6 FAQs from frontline experience

**Classes of 50+: is this realistic?** Yes, with strong routines. Partners already sit side by side; use choral-to-individual sequences and mini-whiteboards, and observe a sample each lesson.

**Parents want dictation scores.** Keep short, purposeful spelling practice, but show portfolio samples and rubrics. Most parents value confident speaking and reading once they see it.

**How much Chinese?** Mostly English with gestures and visuals; brief Chinese for complex instructions, strategy talk, or cultural discussion in lower grades. Don't translate everything.

**A child never speaks.** Lower the stakes: choral and pair speaking first, non-verbal options, rehearsal time, praise for any attempt; consult the class teacher (班主任) about possible underlying issues.

**AI apps at home?** No independent open-ended generative AI for primary pupils (MOE, 2025).\[45\] Recommend only checked, curriculum-related listening and reading apps, used with parents, and never make them compulsory.

**I'm overwhelmed.** Routines and clear instructions first, then one pair activity per lesson, then one well-designed unit per term.

### 8.7 Reflective prompts

Which child did I notice least today, and why? Where did students *use* English rather than repeat it? What evidence showed learning? How did today connect to the unit's big idea? What will I ask my mentor?

## 9. Challenges, Limitations, and Future Directions

### 9.1 Large classes and limited time

Two or three lessons a week with 45–55 pupils limit individual practice.\[46\] The paradigm does not remove this constraint; it maximises available time through routines, pair work, and purposeful homework.

### 9.2 Rural–urban gaps

Rural schools face shortages, multi-grade and multi-subject loads, and limited resources. Jiang and Yip (2024, *Asia-Pacific Journal of Teacher Education*, 52(3)) analysed China's rural teachers' living subsidy policy. They report that the rural primary teaching workforce "decreased substantially from 1,771,923 in 2017 to 1,614,360 in 2021 (157,563 or 8.9%)", which they link to lower pay, status, and working conditions. A CIPP-model evaluation in *Frontiers in Psychology* (June 2024) surveyed "42 primary English teachers and 404 primary students from 90 urban and rural primary schools in 13 regions in the Hunan Province" (19 urban and 23 rural teachers). It also reported rural English-teacher shortages alongside improving teacher quality. National platforms help only where connectivity, devices, and teacher time exist; clustered urban–rural teaching-research networks and online lesson study are promising.

### 9.3 Teacher workload

After-school services, administration, and competitions add workload; without protected time, lesson study becomes a formality.\[38\] AI drafting may save time only if the savings are reinvested in feedback and planning.

### 9.4 Technology access and safety

Device gaps, privacy, ASR problems with children's voices, and screen-time creep are real. The 2025 MOE guidance and UNESCO's recommendations favour caution at primary level, which this paper supports.\[26\]\[47\]

### 9.5 Evidence gaps

The activity approach and unit-based teaching in Grades 1–6 have mostly conceptual or case-based support. AI studies with under-12s are scarce, and most ASR evidence comes from adults.\[30\] Mindset interventions are contested. Long-term outcomes of primary reforms are rarely tracked.

### 9.6 Future directions for frontline research

School-based action research on one unit-design question with pre/post evidence; comparative lesson study across urban and rural partner schools; small, ethical pilots of ASR or levelled-text workflows against non-technology alternatives; three-year tracking of novice teachers' PCK and confidence; studies of low-cost family reading.

---

## 10. Conclusion

The new paradigm is not a new slogan. The 2022 Standards supply the framework: core competencies, themes, activity chains, and assessment integration. International research supports its main ideas: comprehensible input and balanced practice, scaffolding, meaningful tasks, formative assessment, and sustained collaborative professional learning. What schools need is **coherence**: designing student activity, teacher growth, technology use, and the learning ecosystem as one system.

For novice teachers the message is realistic and encouraging. Start with routines and one good activity chain. Learn with colleagues through the teaching-research group. Use assessment to see what children can actually do. Treat AI as a tool for the teacher, not a substitute for the relationship at the heart of teaching young children. Over three years, these steps add up to the teaching the curriculum describes: children who understand, use, and create with English, think and care about others, and enjoy learning.
