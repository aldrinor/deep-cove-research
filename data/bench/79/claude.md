# Between Fatwa, Festival and Farce: Transgender Themes in Middle Eastern and North African Cinema

Cinema from the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) has represented gender crossing since at least the 1950s, but trans subjectivity has reached the screen mainly through three filters: local religious and legal rules that decide which bodies may be shown and how; national genre traditions (comedy, melodrama, arabesk, the art film) that set the terms for gender variance; and a transnational festival-and-humanitarian circuit that rewards some trans stories and erases others.

**TL;DR**

- Read together, *Al-Anisa Hanafi* (Egypt, 1954), *Aynehaye Rooberoo / Facing Mirrors* (Iran, 2011) with *Be Like Others* (2008), and *Lola + Bilidikid* (Germany/Turkish diaspora, 1999) show how trans themes in MENA cinema pass through these three filters. Each film turns trans embodiment into an allegory of nation, modernity or migration, and each partly escapes that allegory.
- The key analytic line runs between Chris Straayer's "temporary transvestite" comedy, in which gender crossing is reversible play, and films about trans people's lived lives. Law and religion in the region repeatedly blur this line by sliding between intersex (*khuntha*), homosexuality and transsexuality, and the films stage those slides.
- The most important development since about 2015 is a move from films *about* trans people, often made for Western festivals, toward co-authored, activist and self-made work. Critics should judge this work less by "visibility" and more by who controls the frame, the funding and the reveal.

## Abstract

This paper surveys transgender themes in the cinemas of the Middle East and North Africa, broadly defined to include the Arab world, Iran, Turkey, Israel/Palestine and their diasporas. It combines trans studies (Stone, Stryker, Prosser, Halberstam, Serano, Bettcher, Aizura, Snorton) with film theory (Mulvey, Straayer, Garber, Dyer, Naficy, Shafik, Schoonover and Galt) and regional scholarship on gender and sexuality (Najmabadi, Shakhsari, Massad and his critics, Savcı, Zengin, Ertür and Lebow). It argues that trans representation in MENA cinema is negotiated among three forces: religious-legal regimes that medicalize and conditionally permit gender transition while criminalizing gender "imitation"; national genre conventions that give gender variance a recognizable but limiting form; and a transnational festival and humanitarian gaze that rewards stories of suffering and rescue. Three in-depth case studies follow: the Egyptian sex-change comedy *Al-Anisa Hanafi* (1954); the Iranian drama *Facing Mirrors* (2011), read alongside the documentary *Be Like Others* (2008); and the Turkish-German melodrama *Lola + Bilidikid* (1999), set against Bülent Ersoy's star-text and Turkish law. Shorter readings of *Paper Dolls*, *Upon the Shadow* and *Warsha* support a closing discussion of comedy versus subjectivity, the reveal, surgery, compulsory transition, sex work and violence, family, asylum, pinkwashing and the rise of trans self-representation.

## 1. Introduction: Thesis, Questions and Scope

A man wakes from emergency surgery as a woman, and Cairo laughs. A trans man in Tehran waits for a passport so he can have surgery abroad, while a devout taxi driver decides whether to help him. A Turkish trans woman performer in Berlin is pressed by her lover to have surgery and "return home" as his wife, and is found dead in a canal. These three scenes come from *Al-Anisa Hanafi* (1954), *Aynehaye Rooberoo* (*Facing Mirrors*, 2011) and *Lola + Bilidikid* (1999). They span almost half a century and three languages, and they share a structure: in each, a body that crosses gender becomes the place where questions of nation, religion, class and migration are argued out.

This paper argues that trans-themed cinema from the MENA region has to be read through the interaction of three forces. The first is a **religious-legal regime** that in several states, unlike most Euro-American law until recently, has *permitted* and even sponsored surgical transition while punishing gender ambiguity, "imitation" and same-sex desire. The second is a set of **national genre traditions**: Egyptian farce, Iranian art cinema and its codes of modesty, Turkish arabesk melodrama and Turkish-German "cinema of the in-between". These provide ready-made shapes for gender variance. The third is a **transnational circuit** of festivals, co-production funds, queer film festivals and broadcasters, whose demand for legible victims and liberated subjects strongly shapes which MENA trans stories get financed, titled and seen. The best films in this corpus neither simply submit to these forces nor simply resist them. They work inside them, often against the grain.

Three questions guide the analysis. First, how do these films separate (or fail to separate) the reversible gender crossing of comedy and disguise from trans subjectivity as a lived, durable form of life? Second, how do they handle the legal and medical framing of trans bodies, especially the surgery scene and the reveal? Third, how does the festival and humanitarian gaze shape documentary and fiction differently, and what changes when trans people from the region take the camera?

**Scope.** "MENA" is used inclusively here: the Arab states from Morocco to the Gulf, Iran, Turkey and Israel/Palestine, plus diasporic and transnational productions by filmmakers from the region or centrally about trans subjects from it. Turkish-German cinema and Iranian-diaspora documentary are therefore included. South Asian hijra and khwaja sira cinema and Afghanistan are excluded except as passing comparisons. Following Susan Stryker's umbrella usage, "transgender themes" covers films with explicitly trans characters or documentary subjects as well as gender-crossing, cross-dressing and passing narratives that trans and film theory have taken up (Stryker 2008). The difference between these categories is treated as an analytic problem rather than settled by definition. Fiction and documentary are both covered, and their different ethical stakes are discussed throughout.

## 2. Terminology and Method

*Trans* and *transgender* are used as umbrella terms, *trans woman* and *trans man* for people who live in a gender different from the one assigned at birth, and the chosen names and pronouns of real people throughout. Many sources, including festival catalogues, trade reviews and older scholarship, describe the subjects of these films as "transvestites", "transsexuals" or, of trans women, "young gay men". Where that language matters to the argument it is quoted and critiqued rather than adopted. Regional vocabularies are not translated as though they were equivalent to Anglo-American categories. These include Persian *taraJensi* (trans) and the state's psychiatric category of "gender identity disorder"; Arabic *mukhannath* (effeminate man), *khuntha* (intersex) and colloquial *khawal*; and Turkish *travesti*, *lubunya* and the slang Lubunca. Afsaneh Najmabadi's warning against reading Iranian trans subjects through a Western identity grid (Najmabadi 2014), and Joseph Massad's critique of the "Gay International" (Massad 2002; 2007), mark the risks. Critics including Sahar Amer (2010), Ghassan Moussawi (2020) and Maya Mikdashi (2022) have shown that the opposite error is also possible: treating regional queer and trans lives as purely local, or purely produced by the West, denies those subjects the ability to borrow, translate and remake.

Methodologically, the paper combines close formal analysis with industrial and reception history and with legal-historical context. Film facts (credits, dates, festivals, awards) were checked against at least two sources where possible: festival records, trade press, distributor catalogues and peer-reviewed scholarship. Unresolved conflicts are noted in the text. The paper paraphrases rather than quotes and keeps direct quotation very short.

## 3. Theoretical Framework

### 3.1 Trans theory: from the "posttranssexual" to trans necropolitics

Trans studies as a field begins with a critique of representation. Sandy Stone's "The Empire Strikes Back" (1992) argued that the classic transsexual autobiography and the clinic produced a plausible, passing, history-erasing subject, and called on trans people to read themselves back into their own pasts instead of disappearing into "wrong body" narratives. Susan Stryker's "My Words to Victor Frankenstein" (1994) turned the monstrous trans body into a source of rage and critique. Her later "(De)Subjugated Knowledges" (2006) framed the field as a challenge to the expert knowledges (medicine, psychiatry, law) that had long spoken *for* trans people. Jay Prosser's *Second Skins* (1998) countered queer theory's taste for performativity by insisting on the material, narrative and bodily reality of transsexual transition. This tension between Prosser and Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Undoing Gender* (2004) runs through this corpus: comedy prefers performativity, while documentary and melodrama prefer the body.

Julia Serano's *Whipping Girl* (2007) identified two dominant media types of the trans woman: the "deceptive" figure, whose reveal produces disgust or violence, and the "pathetic" figure, whose failed femininity produces laughter or pity. Talia Mae Bettcher (2007) showed how the deceiver/make-believer double bind justifies transphobic violence by framing trans people's appearance as fraud. Both are essential for reading the reveal scene in Middle Eastern film. Jack Halberstam's *Female Masculinity* (1998) and his discussion of the "transgender look" in *In a Queer Time and Place* (2005) explain how cinema aligns the viewer with a trans character's gaze, and how often films punish that alignment.

Transnational and trans-of-color critique adds a geopolitical dimension. Aren Aizura's *Mobile Subjects* (2018) and Trystan Cotten's *Transgender Migrations* (2012) show how transition is entangled with travel, medical tourism, asylum and the uneven global distribution of surgical care. C. Riley Snorton and Jin Haritaworn's "Trans Necropolitics" (2013) and the collection *Queer Necropolitics* (Haritaworn, Kuntsman and Posocco 2014) show how Western states value some trans lives, usually white, citizen and rescued, by setting them against others marked for death. Sima Shakhsari's chapter in that collection theorizes the "politics of rightful killing", in which Iranian queer and trans refugees are rhetorically "saved" by the same rights discourse that justifies sanctions and war against the population they come from (Shakhsari 2014; 2020). The collection *Trap Door* (Gossett, Stanley and Burton 2017), building on Peggy Phelan's argument that visibility is a trap (Phelan 1993), warns against treating visibility as progress. Dean Spade (2011) and Paisley Currah (2022) remind us that the state's administration of sex classification, not representation alone, determines trans life chances. That point matters a great deal in a region where a fatwa, a civil-code article or an anti-prostitution amendment can redraw the boundary of the livable.

### 3.2 Regional scholarship on gender and sexuality

Najmabadi's *Women with Mustaches and Men without Beards* (2005) showed how Iranian modernity "heteronormalized" earlier aesthetics in which the beardless youth (*amrad*) was an object of male desire. *Professing Selves* (2014) is the indispensable ethnography of how Iranian trans people, psychologists, clerics and bureaucrats negotiate the post-revolutionary transition regime. Raha Bahreini's "From Perversion to Pathology" (2008) traces the medicalization of gender non-conformity under the Islamic Republic. For Arabic-speaking contexts, Everett Rowson's study of the *mukhannathun* of early Medina (1991), Khaled El-Rouayheb's *Before Homosexuality in the Arab-Islamic World* (2005) and Dror Ze'evi's *Producing Desire* (2006) historicize gendered and sexual categories that do not map onto modern identities. Scott Siraj al-Haqq Kugle (2010) reconstructs Islamic juridical discussion of gender variance, including the long-standing distinction between the *khuntha* (intersex person, a recognized legal category) and the *mukhannath*. Joseph Boone's *The Homoerotics of Orientalism* (2014) and Jarrod Hayes's *Queer Nations* (2000) show how European fantasy and Maghrebi literature each turned sexual marginality into an allegory of the nation. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), Gayatri Spivak's diagnosis of "white men saving brown women from brown men" (Spivak 1988), and Lila Abu-Lughod's "Do Muslim Women Really Need Saving?" (2002) give the critical vocabulary for the rescue narratives that frame many trans documentaries. Jasbir Puar's theory of homonationalism (2007; 2013) and the 2010 *GLQ* special issue on Palestine/Israel edited by Gil Hochberg (2010) frame pinkwashing debates. For Turkey, Evren Savcı's *Queer in Translation* (2021), Deniz Kandiyoti's "Pink Card Blues" (2002), Aslı Zengin's work on violence and intimacy in the trans everyday (Zengin 2016; 2024), and the edited volume of Cüneyt Çakırlar and Serkan Delice (2012) are central.

### 3.3 Film theory: looking, laughing, crossing

Laura Mulvey's account of a cinema organized by a gendered gaze (1975), Mary Ann Doane's theory of femininity as masquerade (1982), Teresa de Lauretis's *Alice Doesn't* (1984) and Kaja Silverman's *Male Subjectivity at the Margins* (1992) all assume a binary that trans cinema unsettles. Two works matter most here. Chris Straayer's *Deviant Eyes, Deviant Bodies* (1996) defines the "temporary transvestite film" as a genre in which a character cross-dresses out of necessity, gains insight into the "other" sex, and is restored to their original gender by the end, so that the gender order is reaffirmed through its temporary suspension. Marjorie Garber's *Vested Interests* (1992) argues that the cross-dresser marks a "category crisis" and that criticism too often "looks through" the transvestite to the man or woman underneath. Vito Russo's *The Celluloid Closet* (1981) and Richard Dyer's work on stereotypes (1993) and stars (1979) explain how gender-variant figures are made legible through types and through the star's existing image. Russo's title was explicitly adapted for the Arab context by Garay Menicucci (1998).

Recent trans film theory moves from image to sensation and time. Eliza Steinbock's *Shimmering Images* (2019) reads cinema's ability to cut and join as analogous to trans change. Cáel Keegan's "Revisitation" (2016) proposes a trans phenomenology of the moving image. Rebecca Bell-Metereau's *Transgender Cinema* (2019) and Laura Horak's *Girls Will Be Boys* (2016) supply genre history. B. Ruby Rich's *New Queer Cinema* (2013) and Rosalind Galt and Karl Schoonover's *Queer Cinema in the World* (2016) push the field beyond the Euro-American canon, the latter with direct readings of Iranian work.

For the region, Hamid Naficy's *An Accented Cinema* (2001) and his *Social History of Iranian Cinema* (2011–2012), Negar Mottahedeh's *Displaced Allegories* (2008), Michelle Langford's *Allegory in Iranian Cinema* (2019), Viola Shafik's *Arab Cinema* (1998) and *Popular Egyptian Cinema* (2007), Walter Armbrust's *Mass Culture and Modernism in Egypt* (1996), Laura U. Marks's *Hanan al-Cinema* (2015), Kay Dickinson's *Arab Cinema Travels* (2016), Lina Khatib (2006), Josef Gugler's *Film in the Middle East and North Africa* (2011), Ella Shohat's *Israeli Cinema* (1989), Raz Yosef's *Beyond Flesh* (2004) and Gönül Dönmez-Colin's *Turkish Cinema* (2008) provide the industrial and aesthetic context. Documentary ethics draws on Bill Nichols (1991), Trinh T. Minh-ha (1991), Rey Chow's analysis of the ethnographic gaze (1995) and Pooja Rangan's *Immediations* (2017), which criticizes the humanitarian documentary's habit of making marginalized subjects "give voice" in formats that confirm the viewer's benevolence. Festival studies, including Thomas Elsaesser (2005), Marijke de Valck (2007) and Skadi Loist on queer festival programming (2012), explains why the festival is an economy and not only a showcase. Melodrama theory (Elsaesser 1972; Brooks 1976; Williams 1991) and José Esteban Muñoz's concepts of disidentification (1999) and queer utopia (2009), together with Sara Ahmed's *Queer Phenomenology* (2006), give the vocabulary for affect, orientation and futurity.

## 4. Historical and Regional Overview

### 4.1 Precedents: mukhannathun, köçek and the comic crossing

Gender-crossing figures appear throughout the region's pre-modern history: the *mukhannathun* musicians of early Islamic Medina (Rowson 1991); Ottoman *köçek*, boy dancers in women's dress whose public performance was gradually suppressed in the nineteenth century; and the *amrad*, the beardless youth of Persian and Arabic love poetry (Najmabadi 2005; El-Rouayheb 2005). Turkish *zenne* (male dancers performing female roles) continue this genealogy, and Caner Alper and Mehmet Binay's *Zenne Dancer* (*Zenne*, 2012) names it in its title, although that film's main subject is homophobic "honor" violence. Early Arab cinema absorbed these traditions mainly through comedy. Menicucci's survey of Egyptian film found cross-dressing to be a long-standing comic device and argued that it was largely de-eroticized and put to work for class and nationalist themes (Menicucci 1998).\[1\]

### 4.2 Law, religion and the medicalized trans body

The legal landscape is not one of uniform prohibition. It is a patchwork in which **permission to transition and punishment of gender variance often coexist**.

In **Iran**, Ayatollah Khomeini had already discussed sex change as permissible in his juridical treatise *Tahrir al-wasila* in the 1960s, as Najmabadi documents (Najmabadi 2014).\[2\] In the mid-1980s he answered a direct petition from Maryam Khatoon Molkara (1950–2012), whose campaign turned the earlier opinion into a practical route to state-recognized transition.\[3\]\[4\]\[5\] Sources give dates for this ruling from 1983 to 1987.\[6\]\[7\] Since then the Islamic Republic has developed a psychiatric-legal pathway of diagnosis, commission approval, surgery and re-issued documents, backed by state financial support: government spokesperson Ali Rabiei announced low-interest loans of 10 million toman (about US$400) toward surgery for trans people holding the required court permits (IranWire n.d.), and a Tehran welfare official told the ILNA news agency that grants run up to 250 million rials (Tehran Times n.d.). Najmabadi shows that this regime is neither "liberal" nor simply a cover for eliminating homosexuality. It is a field of negotiation in which trans and gay people, clerics and clinicians "profess" selves that will be recognized (Najmabadi 2014; Bahreini 2008).\[8\]

In **Egypt**, the decisive case is that of Sally Mursi, a medical student at al-Azhar who had surgery on 29 January 1988.\[9\]\[10\] Al-Azhar refused to readmit her as a woman. After the Doctors' Syndicate wrote to him on 14 May 1988, Grand Mufti Muhammad Sayyid Tantawi issued a fatwa accepting surgery where medically warranted (Human Rights Watch 2012).\[11\]\[12\] Corinne Fortier argues that the fatwa's logic in fact applies to intersex conditions rather than to trans identity. This conflation of *khuntha* with trans has shaped medical gatekeeping in Egypt ever since, and Fortier reports that a medical committee with al-Azhar representation approved no trans surgeries after 2014 (Fortier 2025).\[13\]\[14\] Mursi won administrative-court rulings, but al-Azhar still refused to readmit her (Mada Masr 2015).\[9\]\[11\]

In **Turkey**, the 1980 military coup produced a ban on trans and "effeminate" performers, issued by the Interior Ministry in March 1981. It was aimed in large part at the singer Bülent Ersoy, who had surgery in London in April 1981 (Ertür and Lebow 2014; Altınay 2008).\[15\]\[16\]\[17\]\[18\]\[19\] Ersoy's performance ban was lifted on 7 January 1988, and that year an amendment to Article 29 of the Civil Code allowed changes to civil-registry sex after surgery.\[15\]\[20\]\[21\]\[22\] The 2001 Civil Code, in force from 2002, moved the provision to Article 40 and added court authorization, age over eighteen, unmarried status, a health-board report and permanent infertility.\[23\]\[24\] The European Court of Human Rights found the infertility precondition for surgery incompatible with Article 8 in *Y.Y. v. Turkey* (2015), and Turkey's Constitutional Court struck down the sterilization requirement in 2017 (Amnesty International 2015; Taşcıoğlu 2021).\[23\]\[25\]\[26\]

Elsewhere the trend has been more punitive. **Kuwait**'s 2007 amendment to Article 198 of the Penal Code criminalized "imitating the opposite sex". Human Rights Watch documented its effects (Human Rights Watch 2012), and the Constitutional Court struck it down on 16 February 2022 as vague and incompatible with personal freedom (Amnesty International 2022).\[11\]\[27\] **Oman**'s Penal Code, issued by royal decree on 15 January 2018, criminalized non-normative gender expression for the first time: Article 266 punishes any man who "appears dressed in women's clothing" with one month to one year in prison, a fine of 100 to 300 riyals, or both, and on 15 February 2018 a court in Salalah convicted four people of "imitating the opposite sex" under it (Human Rights Watch 2020). In **Iraq**, parliament amended the 1988 Law on Combating Prostitution on 27 April 2024 to criminalize same-sex relations and "biological sex change based on personal desire", punishing trans people and the doctors who treat them, and targeting effeminacy in men (Human Rights Watch 2024).\[28\]\[29\]\[30\] Penal provisions on same-sex acts, including **Tunisia**'s Article 230, **Morocco**'s Article 489 and **Lebanon**'s Article 534 on sexual acts "contrary to nature", have been used against trans women. Lebanese judges have also narrowed Article 534 and, in individual cases, allowed changes of registered sex. In January 2016 Judge Janet Hanna of the Beirut Court of Appeals upheld a trans man's right to change his official papers, ruling that his surgery had been a medical necessity (Safdar 2016). In January 2017 Metn judge Rabih Maalouf cited Penal Code Article 183 to question how Article 534 was being applied (Human Rights Watch 2017). **Israel** has an administrative route to changing registered gender. That fact, as Section 6 shows, is itself part of a contested visibility politics.

### 4.3 Censorship and industry

Censorship shapes what can be shown. Egyptian censorship has long policed sexual content while tolerating comic gender play. Post-revolutionary Iranian regulations require women to be veiled on screen even in domestic scenes and forbid touch between unrelated men and women. This produces what Naficy calls an aesthetics of veiling and the "averted look" (Naficy 2011–2012), and what Mottahedeh reads as the displacement of desire into film form itself (Mottahedeh 2008). Turkey's broadcasting regulator RTÜK and Lebanon's General Security have both restricted LGBT content. Production for international circulation increasingly depends on European co-production (ARTE, regional funds), Gulf and pan-Arab funds (the Doha Film Institute, AFAC) and festival markets. Dickinson (2016) and Marks (2015) show how these funds shape Arab filmmaking. The trans corpus is an especially clear case, because "LGBTQ in the Muslim world" is a highly marketable festival theme.

## 5. Case Studies

### 5.1 *Al-Anisa Hanafi* / *Miss Hanafi* (Egypt, 1954): sex change as carnival and national allegory

**Production and context.** *Al-Anisa Hanafi* was directed by Fatin Abdel Wahab and written and produced by Galil al-Bendari. It was released in Egypt on 3 July 1954, two years after the Free Officers' revolution and in the first months of Nasser's consolidation of power.\[31\]\[32\] (Menicucci dates the film to 1953. Release records consistently give 1954.)\[1\]\[32\] It was one of many comedies Abdel Wahab made with Ismail Yassin, then Egypt's biggest comic star, whose name later became a film brand in itself, as in *Ismail Yassin in the Army* and *Ismail Yassin in the Navy*.\[33\]\[34\] Mada Masr reports that al-Bendari adapted a real 1947 press story from *Al-Mussawar* about a woman who became a man after surgery at Qasr al-Aini hospital, and reversed the direction of transition for comic effect (Shawky 2014).\[35\]\[36\] The film ranks No. 17 on the Bibliotheca Alexandrina's 2006 list of the 100 most important Egyptian films, chosen by a committee of three critics and historians for the centenary of Egyptian cinema (Al-Ahram Weekly 2007), and it is still a television staple.

**Synopsis.** Hanafi (Yassin), the domineering son of a butcher (Abdel Fatah al-Kasri), is to marry his stepsister Nawaem (Magda), who loves Hassan (Omar al-Hariri), a working-class rival. Hanafi orders the household's women about and shuts the windows on them.\[36\] On the eve of the wedding he collapses with abdominal pain and is rushed to hospital. The emergency operation leaves him a woman, who takes the name Fifi.\[36\]\[37\] Fifi's new life puts her under exactly the constraints Hanafi had enforced, imposed now by her stepmother (Zeinat Sedki) and the neighborhood.\[36\] The film runs through courtship, rivalry and marriage. According to Menicucci, it culminates in Fifi marrying her beloved, a butcher's assistant, and even giving birth to quadruplets before the official wedding (Menicucci 1998).\[1\]

**Formal analysis.** The film is a studio-era *baladi* comedy built around Yassin's elastic face, broad gestures and wide mouth, which he habitually made into a joke at his own expense.\[33\] Its comedy depends on a **performance gap**: Fifi is Yassin in a dress, wig and makeup, and the audience's pleasure lies in seeing the star show through the role. Garber's warning against "looking through" the cross-dresser applies in reverse here. The film *requires* that we look through, because the joke is the visible mismatch. The mise-en-scène sets that mismatch in the alleys, the butcher's shop and the domestic interior of a popular quarter, and the gendered architecture of windows, thresholds and the street becomes the film's real subject. In its most-quoted reversal, Fifi wants to open the windows Hanafi had closed, and society, embodied by the stepmother, will not let her (Shawky 2014).\[36\] Musical numbers and wedding sequences, standard in Egyptian comedy of the time, give the gender switch the rhythm of festive inversion.

The **surgery** is the film's reveal, and it is located entirely in the medical institution. Hanafi does not choose his transformation. A hospital imposes it on him. This matters theoretically. The film imagines sex change as something done *to* a man by modern medicine, a fantasy of reversal rather than an expression of identity, and so stays within what Straayer calls the temporary transvestite structure. Yet Hanafi is never restored to manhood. The "temporary" crossing is made permanent, and the film has to imagine a female future for its male star, ending, in Menicucci's account, in marriage and childbirth.

**Reception and scholarship.** The most sustained academic reading is Menicucci's "Unlocking the Arab Celluloid Closet" (1998). He calls the film a "nationalist parable" in which Yassin's drag "is not meant to be erotic". The cross-dressing carries little homosexual double meaning and serves instead as a comic vehicle for class and for the transition from traditionalism to a nationalist modernity. Menicucci contrasts it with *Sukkar Hanem* (1960), whose drag he finds overtly sexual.\[1\] In a 1999 paper given after a screening at the Women and Memory Forum in Cairo, Walid El Hamamsy read the film's narration, camera work, editing and lighting as presenting Hanafi/Fifi as a social product, and therefore gender roles as socially constructed (El Hamamsy 1999).\[38\] Mada Masr's critic Amany Ali Shawky praised it in 2014 as a film that "breaks every social norm and cinematic cliché of its time". She called it unusual for its progressive questioning of gender, and observed that nothing comparable appeared until Raafat al-Mihi's *Al-Sada al-Rigal* (*Misters*, 1987), in which a married woman becomes a man (Shawky 2014).\[36\] Shafik's broader account of Egyptian popular cinema's gender and class politics (Shafik 2007) and Armbrust's analysis of mass culture and modernism (Armbrust 1996) provide context, though neither is cited here for a reading of this specific film.

**Theoretical reading.** Bakhtin's carnival gives the first frame. *Miss Hanafi* stages a world turned upside down in which the patriarch becomes the woman he oppressed, and laughter clears the way for renewal (Bakhtin 1984). Carnival, however, is licensed inversion. Straayer's temporary transvestite film shows how such inversion usually ends by reaffirming the binary, and the farce here ends in marriage and fertility, the most normative outcome possible. The film's pedagogy is reformist rather than queer. It is a male-feminist lesson, in tune with early Nasserist rhetoric about women's place in the new nation: if men knew how it felt, they would open the windows. Fifi is not a trans subject. She has no desire for transition and no history she claims as her own, and her womanhood is a punishment and a lesson.

A trans reading can still recover more than this. First, the film's founding premise is that **sex can be changed by medicine and the changed person can marry, give birth and be recognized by the neighborhood**. In 1954 that was a remarkable imaginative concession, made more than thirty years before Sally Mursi's case forced Egyptian institutions to decide whether such a person could re-enter al-Azhar. Comedy made thinkable what law later refused. Second, Yassin's star-text matters. Dyer's account of stars as bundles of contradictions (Dyer 1979) helps explain why Yassin, whose comic persona was built on mock-humiliation, a body read as unattractive and class subalternity, could carry a gender transformation without threatening the audience. He was already coded as a "failed" masculinity. In Serano's terms, Fifi is a version of the "pathetic" trans figure whose femininity is a joke (Serano 2007). The star's popularity makes that figure loved, not loathed. Third, Garber's "category crisis" applies to the film's politics as much as its gender. The figure that cannot be classified is the old patriarchal order of the butcher's shop, being reformed into a modern national family before the audience's eyes. In Hayes's (2000) sense, the gender switch is a national allegory, and the trans body carries the nation's transformation.

The film also shows what the comic mode **cannot** do. It cannot imagine a trans person who chooses. The later Egyptian record makes that limitation concrete. Sally Mursi's actual transition was treated not as comedy but as fraud, mutilation and suspected homosexuality (Fortier 2025).\[13\]\[39\] This is the structure Bettcher calls the deceiver double bind.

### 5.2 *Aynehaye Rooberoo* / *Facing Mirrors* (Iran, 2011) and *Be Like Others* (2008): melodrama, documentary and the compulsory-transition narrative

**Production and context.** *Facing Mirrors* is the first feature by Negar Azarbayjani. She co-wrote it with the pioneering producer Fereshteh Taerpour (1953–2021), whose daughter Ghazal Shakeri plays Rana and co-produced (Scheib 2011; IranWire 2021).\[40\]\[41\]\[42\] Shayesteh Irani, known from Jafar Panahi's *Offside* (2006), plays Adineh, who calls himself Eddie. The film is widely described as the first Iranian narrative feature with a trans protagonist.\[43\] At home it won the Special Jury's Crystal Simorgh at the 29th Fajr International Film Festival. Abroad, according to its distributor The Film Collaborative, it won the Jury Award for Best Film at the 2011 Montreal World Film Festival, the Grand Prix at the Paris Gay & Lesbian Film Festival and Best Narrative Feature at the Austin Gay and Lesbian Film Festival (The Film Collaborative n.d.). Its awards include the Outstanding First Feature award at Frameline (San Francisco, 2012) and the Circle Award at Filmfest DC (2012), and a contributor to the Ajam Media Collective counted selections or prizes at more than sixty festivals (Ajam Media Collective 2013; IndieWire 2012). Its domestic release was delayed. In April 2012 Taerpour told IndieWire she doubted it would screen in Iran.\[44\] The film received its exhibition permit and opened in Iranian cinemas on 24 October 2012, about a year and a half after its festival premieres (Ajam Media Collective 2013).\[45\] Zara Saeidzadeh reports that Qom's municipality forbade public screenings, but Mofid University in Qom screened it in December 2012 (Saeidzadeh 2025).\[3\]

**Synopsis.** Rana, a devout working-class wife and mother, drives an unlicensed taxi to pay off the debt keeping her husband in prison. She picks up Eddie, a young trans man from a wealthy family who has run away from home. His father plans to force him into marriage with a male cousin, and Eddie is waiting for a passport so he can leave for surgery in Germany.\[43\]\[46\] When Eddie tries to explain that he is trans, Rana panics and nearly drives into a bus (Frameline 2012).\[43\] The rest of the film builds an uneasy alliance between the two. Rana's piety and fear of scandal pull against her growing recognition of Eddie's plight, while the father's pursuit drives the plot toward a confrontation over whether Eddie will be allowed to live as himself.

**Formal analysis.** The film's main formal choice is **confinement**. Much of the drama happens inside the taxi, a well-established figure of semi-public intimacy in Iranian cinema whose best-known precedent is Kiarostami's *Ten* (2002). The car lets the film place an unrelated man and woman (as the state's categories would read them) in sustained conversation without the forbidden touch, and it makes the windscreen and mirrors into figures of the title. The *mirrors* face both ways. Eddie's self-image does not match how the world reflects him, and Rana's sense of herself as a good woman is reflected back to her as complicity. Schoonover and Galt note that the opening taxi scene first suggests lesbian intimacy before the film reveals Eddie as trans.\[47\] In this way the film plays its audience's assumptions against them and refuses to let the reveal function as Serano's "deception" scene. They also report the director's remark that the film had two incompatible audiences: queer festivals abroad and religious conservatives at home (Schoonover and Galt 2016).\[47\]

Iranian modesty regulations shape the film's treatment of the body throughout. A trans man played by a cis actress must be presented within rules that require women performers to cover their hair and forbid touch. Gender presentation is therefore carried by costume, posture, voice, haircut and naming rather than by the body's exposure. In this the film follows the logic Mottahedeh describes, in which constraint moves desire into the formal register. Unlike Western trans dramas it contains no surgical reveal, no nude "truth" scene and no medical spectacle. Surgery is a destination abroad that is never shown. In Keegan's phenomenological terms, the trans experience is rendered as waiting, blockage and motion, time spent in transit rather than a body put on display. The genre is **melodrama** in the full sense Elsaesser (1972) and Brooks (1976) gave it: excess of feeling, moral polarization (the tyrannical father, the conscience-stricken believer) and the expressive use of space. As in Williams's account of melodrama's "body genres" (Williams 1991), the spectator's tears are the film's argument.

**Reception and debate.** The film's Iranian reception shows how far a trans narrative could travel within official culture. It was a Fajr prizewinner, Saeidzadeh calls it "the first box office hit" about a trans man in contemporary Iranian cinema, and it was the subject of a panel with an expert on trans law from Mofid University (Ajam Media Collective 2013; Saeidzadeh 2025).\[3\]\[45\] That reception was not uncritical. Saeidzadeh records criticism from trans activists, including Saman Arastoo's complaint that the filmmakers ignored the gender spectrum.\[3\] She argues that the film's reception illustrates how knowledge about trans Iranians is produced within a medicalized, surgery-centered model, a form of epistemic injustice (Saeidzadeh 2025). Emily O'Dell reads *Facing Mirrors* as the key film in a wave of Iranian trans theatre and cinema that used art for social change and tolerance without the vocabulary of queer theory, and that provoked debate among artists and activists about how trans bodies should be shown (O'Dell 2020).\[48\] Abroad, the Ajam contributor argued that Western mainstream media's near-silence about the film contrasted with the attention given to Maryam Keshavarz's lesbian drama *Circumstance* (2011). In the contributor's view, this was because *Facing Mirrors* refused the Orientalist script of a sexuality crushed by the regime and awaiting rescue (Ajam Media Collective 2013).\[45\]

**The documentary counterpart: *Be Like Others*.** No reading of *Facing Mirrors* can avoid Tanaz Eshaghian's *Be Like Others*. It premiered in the World Cinema documentary competition at Sundance on 19 January 2008, won the Teddy Award for documentary at the 2008 Berlinale, and was broadcast by the BBC as *Transsexual in Iran* (Variety 2008).\[7\]\[49\] Eshaghian, who left Iran as a child and grew up in New York, filmed patients at Dr Bahram Mir-Jalali's surgical clinic in Tehran. Her subjects include Anoosh, who becomes Anahita, alienating her boyfriend Ali; Ali Askar; and Vida, a woman who supports others through the process.\[49\]\[50\]\[51\] The film's full English title, *Be Like Others: The Story of Transgendered Young Women Living in Iran*, names its subjects as women.\[7\] Its trade framing did otherwise: Variety's review described a film about "young gay men who opt for gender change" to escape harassment, and praised it as "a powerful window into a once-hidden side of the country" (Variety 2008).\[7\]\[51\]

That framing is the "compulsory transition" narrative in its purest form: the claim that Iran forces gay men to become women. It has been politically powerful. It has also been sharply criticized. Najmabadi argues that the thesis flattens a complex field in which some people who might identify as gay elsewhere do move into trans categories under pressure, others find in trans categories real and chosen forms of life, and many move between them strategically. Universalizing human-rights campaigns built on the "forced sex change" story can damage the conditions of life for the people they claim to help (Najmabadi 2014).\[8\] Shakhsari pushes further. The image of the Iranian trans or queer subject as the regime's victim, circulated through diaspora media and refugee procedures, feeds a politics of rightful killing that justifies sanctions and militarism while making refugees perform suffering to qualify for protection (Shakhsari 2014; 2020).\[52\]\[53\] Amy Tahani-Bidmeshki offers a more generous reading of the film. She argues that its trans subjects, filmed against murals of revolutionary martyrs and chador-clad crowds, push back against the heteronormative nationalism of the Islamic Republic (Tahani-Bidmeshki 2018).\[54\]\[55\]

**Theoretical reading.** Set side by side, the two films show the different ethical stakes of fiction and documentary. *Be Like Others* has the documentary's authority, Nichols's "discourse of sobriety" (Nichols 1991), and also the documentary's hunger for the body. The clinic, the consultation and the operation give it scenes Iranian fiction could not show. Rangan's critique of "immediation" applies here: the humanitarian documentary presents its subjects' suffering as raw truth while the editing, the English-language narration and the Sundance-to-BBC pipeline shape that truth for a Western audience whose concern confirms its own liberal identity (Rangan 2017). Chow's ethnographic gaze (Chow 1995) operates through the very premise of a "window" onto a "once-hidden" Iran, and the Abu-Lughod and Spivak frame of saving brown women from brown men fits the trans case with uncomfortable precision. The alternative titles, *Be Like Others* (an Iranian subject's desire to be ordinary) and *Transsexual in Iran* (a spectacle category plus a location), condense this tension.

*Facing Mirrors*, made inside the system and approved by it, takes a different path. It does not deny that the state permits surgery, and Eddie's goal is in a sense the state's own model of a cured body. But it moves the conflict from the clinic to the **family**, the place Najmabadi identifies as the main site of trans suffering in Iran. It refuses the reveal-as-deception and makes its central drama the transformation of a cis, religious woman's understanding. Its implied spectator is Rana, not the Western liberal. This is what Schoonover and Galt mean when they say the film "narrates the need for a queer trans presence" in Iranian public space: it neither endorses state policy nor follows the escape-to-the-West plot, even though Eddie's own goal is Germany (Schoonover and Galt 2016). Activist criticism is still warranted. A surgery-centered, binary trans man who is legible to Iranian medical law is the version of trans life the state can best absorb. In Stone's terms, the film affirms a *transsexual* narrative of the wrong body and its correction rather than a *posttranssexual* one. The film's achievement and its limits are the same thing: it enters Iranian cinema through the door the fatwa opened.

### 5.3 *Lola + Bilidikid* (Germany, 1999): the *Transe*, the diaspora and arabesk melodrama

**Production and context.** *Lola + Bilidikid* (*Lola und Bilidikid*) was written and directed by Kutluğ Ataman, an Istanbul-born artist-filmmaker. It was produced in Germany by Zero Film (Martin Hagemann) with WDR/Arte, with James Schamus as executive producer (Variety 1999).\[56\] Ataman originally wanted to shoot in Istanbul, and the finished film was shot entirely in Berlin.\[57\] It opened the Berlinale Panorama in February 1999, shared that year's Teddy jury award, won the audience award at the Istanbul International Film Festival and Best Film at Turin's gay and lesbian festival, and in 2026 was shown in a digitally restored version in the Berlinale Retrospective.\[56\]\[58\]\[59\] Its classification is part of its meaning. It is a German production, in German and Turkish, by a Turkish director, set among Turkish-German queer and trans people in Kreuzberg. Including it in a "MENA" corpus is justified less by geography than by its content. It is a film *about* the export of Turkish gender regimes, *arabesk* affect and post-1980 Turkish sexual politics into the diaspora, and its circulation, notably the Istanbul audience prize, put it into Turkish debate. It also shows the "accented" condition Naficy (2001) theorizes: interstitial production, multilingualism and a structure of feeling built on displacement.

**Synopsis.** Murat (Baki Davrak), a seventeen-year-old Turkish-German, secretly cruises Berlin's parks while his domineering older brother Osman tries to "make a man of him". In the queer nightlife he meets Lola (Gandi Mukli), a singer in a trans and drag cabaret troupe, and learns she is his older brother, expelled from the family by Osman.\[60\]\[61\] Lola's lover Bili (Erdal Yıldız), the macho "Billy the Kid" of the title, wants Lola to have surgery so they can return to Turkey as husband and wife.\[57\]\[62\] Lola refuses to be remade to fit his fantasy. Her friend Kalipso (Mesut Özdemir), by contrast, wants surgery and looks for a wealthy man to pay for it.\[63\] Lola is found dead in the river. Her friends reveal that Osman had raped her when he discovered she was gay. In the climax Murat dresses in Lola's red wig.\[57\] Bili seeks revenge on the neo-Nazis he blames for Lola's murder, the violence spreads, and the truth about Osman's role in Lola's death comes out (Variety 1999).

**Formal analysis.** Ataman builds the film from the materials of **Turkish arabesk melodrama**: doomed love, the suffering singer, the knife fight, the vendetta and family honor. He transposes them onto a Berlin of parks, U-Bahn passages, cabarets and the ruins of the Nazi past, filmed by Chris Squires with Arpad Bondy's score. The genre fit is pointed. Arabesk was the genre through which Bülent Ersoy, Turkey's most famous trans star, had been made into a figure of suffering and redemption. Ertür and Lebow show that Ersoy's semi-autobiographical film *The End of Fame* (1981), which she made while her transition collided with the 1980 coup, "queers" arabesk conventions while anticipating her legal ordeal (Ertür and Lebow 2014).\[18\]\[64\] Lola's cabaret numbers, delivered in a troupe that styles itself as female migrant workers (*Gastarbeiterinnen*), turn the guest-worker category itself into drag.\[62\] The film treats **performance** as both refuge and exposure: on stage, Lola controls the gaze; in the family and the street, she is subject to it. The **reveal** is displaced. There is no scene that exposes Lola's body as "male" for the audience's shock. The film's revelations concern the family's violence: the secret that Lola is Murat's brother, and the secret that Osman raped her. This reverses Serano's deceiver plot. The deceiver is the respectable heterosexual patriarch. The surgery theme is split between two characters: Kalipso, for whom surgery is a desire and a financial project, and Lola, for whom surgery demanded by a lover is a form of possession. This prevents the film from equating trans identity with operative status.

**Reception and scholarship.** *Lola + Bilidikid* is the most-studied film in this corpus. Christopher Clark's "Transculturation, *Transe* Sexuality, and Turkish Germany" (2006) focuses on the figure of the *Transe*, German slang for a trans woman or transvestite. He argues that Ataman uses this figure, and the "transness" of sexual and cultural liminality, to undermine the binaries of male and female, Turkish and German, and tradition and modernity. He also argues that the film uses Berlin's urban space to challenge both German and Turkish fantasies of homogeneity and dominant Western models of sexual identity (Clark 2006, 555–572).\[65\] Later critics have read the film through Butler's theory of gender melancholy, tying drag to a lost motherland. Others have noted the overdetermined German spaces in which the film stages its violence, and criticized its "overloaded" plot. A 2024 German reconsideration observed that in 1999 the film had only a limited vocabulary for gender and ethnicity.\[61\]\[66\]\[67\] Göktürk's work on transnational Turkish-German cinema (2000) and Halle's *German Film after Germany* (2008) place the film within the post-1990s shift from a "cinema of duty" about migrant victims to a cinema of hybrid pleasure.

**Theoretical reading: the Turkish frame.** The film becomes fully legible only alongside the Turkish trans history it carries abroad. Bili's fantasy of taking a surgically "completed" Lola back to Turkey reproduces the logic of Turkey's 1988 legal settlement: the state would recognize Ersoy, and trans women like her, as women *after* surgery, and on that condition alone.\[21\] The 2002 Civil Code later tightened this into court-supervised surgery and sterilization (Taşcıoğlu 2021).\[24\]\[68\] Kandiyoti's "Pink Card Blues" (2002) described how Istanbul's trans women were caught between the pink identity card of legal womanhood and police harassment of the street. The mid-1990s also brought one of the defining episodes of Turkish trans history: the 1996 violence and evictions that drove trans women out of Ülker Sokak in Cihangir, Beyoğlu, before the UN Habitat II summit in Istanbul. Pınar Selek's oral history *Maskeler, Süvariler, Gacılar* (2001) documented it.\[69\]\[70\]\[71\] *Lola + Bilidikid* is set in Berlin, but its world of travesti and lubunya sociability, in which chosen family forms in cabarets, sex work and the street, is the same world. Zengin's concept of "violent intimacies" (2024), the claim that the trans everyday is shaped by and resistant to the violence of the family, police and street, describes the film's dramatic logic exactly.\[72\] Lola's death comes from the most intimate source: her own brother.

Ersoy's star-text is the film's absent center. Altınay (2008) reads Ersoy's later self-presentation as a Muslim, nationalist, upper-class woman as a strategy of reconstruction. Ertür and Lebow (2014) push back, emphasizing how her very existence "unsettled the law".\[64\]\[73\]\[74\] Lola is in a sense the anti-Ersoy. She refuses the surgery-for-recognition bargain that Ersoy fought for and won. Butler's distinction between gender as performance and gender as a norm one is forced to embody (Butler 2004) is staged in Lola's choice. Muñoz's **disidentification** (1999) describes the troupe's tactics: taking the stigmatizing figure of the *Gastarbeiter* and the Turkish woman and reworking them on stage into glamour and critique.

The film is also open to criticism. Its violence falls on the trans woman. Lola dies so that Murat, the gay cis protagonist, can come of age, and in this the film repeats a familiar pattern of queer cinema (Russo 1981) that Snorton and Haritaworn's "trans necropolitics" makes visible. The trans woman's death becomes a resource for others' becoming. The Berlinale and Teddy reception also fitted a German multicultural narrative in which Germany is the space where Turkish queers can be free and Turkish families are the threat. Ataman's inclusion of neo-Nazi violence partly complicates that homonationalist reading (Puar 2007) without escaping it. Even so, for the history this paper traces, *Lola + Bilidikid* is the first major film to put a Turkish trans woman's choice about surgery, rather than her surgery as a spectacle, at the center of the drama.

### 5.4 Shorter readings: Israel, Tunisia, Lebanon

***Bubot Niyar* / *Paper Dolls* (Israel, 2006).** Tomer Heymann's documentary follows Filipino trans women and gay men who came to Israel as caregivers for elderly Orthodox Jewish men and perform on their day off as a drag troupe called the Paper Dolls. It played the international queer festival circuit and won audience awards in Turin and Vienna.\[75\]\[76\] Its context is political in a way the film only partly acknowledges. Israel's reliance on overseas migrant care workers grew after it closed its labor market to Palestinians, so the film's "tolerant" Tel Aviv sits on a structural exclusion.\[77\] The debates over pinkwashing are directly relevant: Puar's homonationalism (2007), Sarah Schulman's 2011 op-ed, Jason Ritchie's work on queer checkpoints (2010), Hochberg's *GLQ* issue (2010) and the Palestinian organization alQaws. The film presents Israel as a relatively liberal refuge for trans migrants, while the subjects' deportability is ever-present.\[77\]\[78\] Viewers have also noted the director's difficulty in distinguishing the trans women among his subjects from cis men in drag, a confusion that the film's English-language marketing ("transvestite Filipinos") repeats.\[79\]\[80\] The film illustrates Aizura's point that trans mobility is organized by global labor markets and state border regimes. Its narrator's own journey from discomfort to affection makes the Israeli filmmaker, not the Filipino subjects, the protagonist of the film's moral education, which is the structure Rangan criticizes.\[81\]

***Fi al-Zill* / *Upon the Shadow* (Tunisia/France, 2017).** Nada Mezni Hafaiedh's documentary follows Amina Sboui, the former Femen activist, and the queer and trans young people who live in her Tunis home after being rejected by their families. Among them is Sandra Neifer, a trans woman whom the producer's synopsis describes with the outdated term "transvestite".\[82\]\[83\]\[84\] The film premiered at the Thessaloniki Documentary Festival and won the Tanit de bronze at the Journées cinématographiques de Carthage in 2017.\[84\]\[85\] That prize at the Arab world's oldest film festival is significant, because it means a queer-and-trans documentary was recognized *within* a regional institution. Some viewers note that it uses re-enactment and stages daily life.\[82\] That blurring of observational and performed modes can be read with Trinh's critique of documentary "truth" (Trinh 1991) as a form of protection and self-authorship for subjects exposed to Tunisia's Article 230 and to police violence. The film's chosen-family structure parallels the cabaret family of *Lola + Bilidikid* and the household of *Paper Dolls*.

***Warsha* (Lebanon/France, 2022).** Dania Bdeir's fifteen-minute short follows Mohammad, a Syrian migrant crane operator in Beirut, played by the Lebanese performer Khansa, who volunteers for the city's most dangerous crane. Alone at the top, he dances an aerial choreography of femme self-expression.\[86\]\[87\]\[88\] *Warsha* won the Short Film Jury Award for International Fiction at Sundance 2022 and Best Fiction at Tampere, and was co-produced in France and Lebanon with an ARTE pre-purchase.\[89\] Bdeir has said the film started from asking what would happen if the crane operator was seeking the privacy to break out of gender norms.\[88\] The film does not name an identity, and that is its point. It belongs to the trans umbrella not as a coming-out narrative but as what Halberstam's *Trans** (2018) calls the asterisk, a space of unfinished, unnamed gender. Its formal language of verticality, isolation and the city below gives Ahmed's "orientation" (2006) a literal form. Its Syrian-refugee protagonist ties gender expression to the precarity of migrant labor, as *Paper Dolls* does.

## 6. Cross-Cutting Themes and Debates

**Comedy and cross-dressing versus trans subjectivity.** The corpus begins in farce. Egyptian comedy from *Miss Hanafi* to *Sukkar Hanem* and *Al-Sada al-Rigal*, and the Iranian disguise films *Daughters of the Sun* (*Dokhtaran-e Khorshid*, Maryam Shahriar, 2000) and *Baran* (Majid Majidi, 2001), in which girls pass as boys to work, use gender crossing as a device for social commentary: class in Egypt, the economic exclusion of women and Afghan refugees in Iran. Straayer's category fits these films, and they should not be retroactively claimed as trans narratives. Yet Garber's insight holds that the crossing figure disturbs more than the plot can contain. A rigorous reading keeps two things in view at once. The comic transvestite is not a trans person, and the comic transvestite is nevertheless the form through which the region's popular cinema first imagined the changeable sexed body.

**Passing, disguise and the reveal.** Serano's deceiver and Bettcher's double bind describe what these films mostly *avoid*. *Facing Mirrors* turns its potential reveal into Rana's crisis of conscience. *Lola + Bilidikid* moves revelation onto the family's crimes. *Warsha* has no witness. The reveal persists in documentary marketing, in titles like *Transsexual in Iran* and in synopses that misgender subjects, as a relation between the film and its foreign audience rather than between characters.

**The trans body as national and religious allegory.** In every case study the trans body carries a national story: Nasserist modernization in *Miss Hanafi*, the Islamic Republic's management of bodies in *Facing Mirrors* and *Be Like Others*, and Turkish-German belonging in *Lola + Bilidikid*. This is the persistent risk Hayes (2000) and Boone (2014) identify. Allegory gives the trans figure significance at the cost of particularity, and Iranian cinema's allegorical habit (Langford 2019) intensifies it.

**The medical gaze and the surgery scene.** Surgery is shown in comedy (*Miss Hanafi*, played for laughs and imposed by accident) and in documentary (*Be Like Others*, where the clinic provides the film's location and spectacle). Fiction made under Iranian censorship cannot show it, and *Facing Mirrors* turns that limit into an aesthetic of waiting. Read with Spade and Currah, the surgery scene is less about bodies than about the state's demand that sex be certified before it is recognized, the demand Turkey wrote into Article 40 and Egypt's medical committees enforce through the intersex/trans distinction.\[14\]\[23\]

**The "compulsory transition" narrative.** The claim that Iran forces gay men into surgery has some factual basis in the pressures Najmabadi documents. As a master narrative, though, it erases trans Iranians who want to transition, ignores the agency of those who move between categories, and supplies the rescue plot Shakhsari criticizes. Viewers should treat documentaries built on it with caution and look at whose words carry the claim. In *Be Like Others* it is largely the framing and the trade press that carry it, more than the subjects.

**Sex work, the street and violence.** From the cabaret and the park in *Lola + Bilidikid* to Ülker Sokak and the police violence documented by Human Rights Watch in Kuwait, sex work and the street are where trans women's economic exclusion and their sociability meet.\[11\] Zengin's "violent intimacies" and Taşcıoğlu's analysis of Turkish trans women's exclusion from licensed sex work and of courts reducing sentences for "unjust provocation" in trans killings show that cinema's association of trans women with the night is a social fact before it is a stereotype.\[21\] The ethical question is whether films treat that fact as fate or as structure.

**Family, kinship and religion.** The family is the main antagonist across the corpus: the butcher's household that is to be reformed, Eddie's father, Osman, and the families who reject Amina's housemates. Religion is more ambivalent. *Facing Mirrors* makes a pious woman its moral center, and the region's fatwas both enable and limit transition. Saba Mahmood's (2005) insistence on taking piety seriously as a form of agency, rather than reading it as false consciousness, helps explain why Rana's arc is persuasive. The chosen families of cabaret, safe house and troupe offer the alternative kinship Muñoz associates with queer utopia.

**Migration and asylum.** Germany is Eddie's destination and Lola's home. Israel draws the Paper Dolls, and Beirut draws the Syrian protagonist of *Warsha*. Aizura and Cotten show that trans mobility is shaped by the uneven geography of surgery, labor and asylum. Shakhsari's ethnography of Iranian trans refugees waiting in Turkish satellite cities shows how asylum regimes demand legible suffering.\[52\]\[53\] Films that end in emigration risk reproducing what Shakhsari calls the "rightful" rescue script. The stronger films keep the destination unresolved.

**Visibility, festivals, pinkwashing and the "Gay International."** Festivals are where this corpus gains value. *Be Like Others* went from Sundance and the Berlinale to the BBC, and *Lola + Bilidikid* opened the Panorama. *Facing Mirrors* travelled the queer festival circuit while being largely ignored by Western mainstream media, and *Paper Dolls* toured queer festivals as a story of Israeli tolerance. The Teddy Award, the queer festival programmers Loist describes and the documentary broadcasters create demand for MENA trans stories in a particular form: suffering, legible and set against an oppressive state. Massad's critique explains the danger. Amer, Moussawi and Mikdashi explain why rejecting all transnational exchange is no answer. The *Trap Door* editors' caution applies too: visibility often brings violence, and Iraq's 2024 law, partly framed as resistance to Western "gender" ideology, shows how international trans visibility can be used against trans people at home.

**Trans authorship and self-representation.** The corpus surveyed here is mostly made *by cis directors about* trans people: Abdel Wahab, Azarbayjani and Taerpour, Eshaghian, Ataman (who is gay but not trans), Heymann, Mezni Hafaiedh and Bdeir. The emerging alternative is co-authorship and self-representation: trans performers shaping roles (as Khansa did with *Warsha*), activist video and short films circulated online, and theatre such as the 2025 Istanbul stage adaptation of Selek's Ülker Sokak oral history, performed by two trans women.\[90\]\[91\] Trans critics such as Saeidzadeh and Iranian trans activists are also contesting how films like *Facing Mirrors* are received. Journals and platforms such as *Kohl*, Jadaliyya and Bekhsoos have built a regional critical sphere in which trans people from the region review these films instead of only appearing in them.\[92\] This shift matters more than any single film. It moves the question from whether trans people are visible to who holds the camera, the funding and the edit.

## 7. Conclusion

Across seven decades, MENA cinema has moved from the accidental surgical sex change played for laughs to trans protagonists who choose, refuse and wait. *Miss Hanafi* imagined a medically changed body that the neighborhood could absorb, decades before Egyptian institutions refused Sally Mursi that recognition. *Facing Mirrors* used the door opened by Khomeini's ruling to bring a trans man into Iranian commercial cinema, and moved the drama from the clinic to the family and the believer's conscience. *Be Like Others* shows both the power of documentary access and its capture by a rescue narrative. *Lola + Bilidikid* carried Turkey's post-1980 trans history to Berlin, where a trans woman's refusal of surgery-as-condition became the moral center of a melodrama that still kills her.

The thesis can now be stated more sharply. **Trans cinema from the MENA region is shaped by the gap between permission and recognition.** States and jurists have often permitted the trans body as a medical object while denying the trans person social, familial and political standing. Comedy, melodrama and documentary each fill that gap differently: comedy by turning the body into a reversible joke, melodrama by turning it into suffering to be redeemed, and documentary by turning it into evidence. The festival circuit rewards the third most of all. The films that last are those that refuse to let any one of these forms settle the question, keeping the trans subject's own time, desire and choice in view. The most promising developments, from *Warsha*'s wordless crane dance to trans-led theatre in Istanbul and critical writing in regional journals, point toward a cinema in which trans people from the region are not the material for national allegory or humanitarian concern but the authors of their own image.
