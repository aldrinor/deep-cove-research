# The Gut Microbiota, Intestinal Inflammation, and Colorectal Cancer: Mechanisms, Probiotics, Prebiotics, Pathogens, and Dietary Translation

## TL;DR

- A diverse, SCFA-producing gut microbiota maintains the intestinal barrier, educates the immune system, and resists pathogens; **dysbiosis** — loss of diversity and bloom of pathobionts — drives chronic inflammation and is causally implicated in colorectal cancer (CRC) through genotoxins (colibactin, BFT, indolimines), secondary bile acids, and inflammatory signaling (NF-κB, STAT3, Wnt/β-catenin). The strongest recent human evidence is the 2025 *Nature* finding (Díaz-Gay et al., *Nature* 643:230–240, 981 CRC genomes across 11 countries) that colibactin exposure is "3.3 times more common in colorectal cancers diagnosed in individuals younger than 40 compared to those over 70" (p-trend = 2.7×10⁻⁷).
- **Probiotics** (notably reclassified *Lactobacillus* genera, *Bifidobacterium*, *Saccharomyces boulardii*, and the 8-strain De Simone/VSL#3 formulation) have solid randomized-trial support for a narrow set of uses — preventing antibiotic-associated and *C. difficile* diarrhea, pouchitis, and necrotizing enterocolitis in preterm infants — but **no proven role in preventing or treating CRC in humans**; "next-generation" strains like *Akkermansia muciniphila* and *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* remain largely preclinical. **Prebiotics** (inulin, FOS, GOS, resistant starch) feed butyrate-producing cross-feeding networks whose end-product fuels colonocytes and suppresses tumor cells via the "butyrate paradox."
- The best dietary advice is unglamorous and robust: eat 30+ g/day of diverse fiber (each 10 g/day of total dietary fiber lowered CRC risk to RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.86–0.94, per Aune et al., *BMJ* 2011), aim for plant diversity (in the American Gut Project, people eating >30 different plant types per week had more diverse microbiomes than those eating ≤10), add fermented foods (which raised microbiome diversity and decreased 19 of 93 inflammatory serum proteins in a 2021 Stanford trial), limit red meat to <500 g cooked/week, avoid processed meat (IARC Group 1), moderate alcohol, limit ultra-processed foods/emulsifiers/some sweeteners, and start CRC screening at age 45 (USPSTF/ACS).

## 1. Background: How the Microbiota Keeps the Gut Healthy, and How Dysbiosis Drives Inflammation and Cancer

### 1.1 Composition of a healthy adult gut microbiota

In the adult fecal microbiota, roughly 90% of commensal bacteria belong to two phyla — **Bacillota** (formerly *Firmicutes*) and **Bacteroidota** (formerly *Bacteroidetes*) — with the remaining ~10% drawn mainly from **Actinomycetota** (formerly *Actinobacteria*), **Pseudomonadota** (formerly *Proteobacteria*), **Verrucomicrobiota** (formerly *Verrucomicrobia*), and **Fusobacteriota** (formerly *Fusobacteria*).\[1\] These phylum renamings follow the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes and are now standard in the 2021–2026 literature.\[2\] About 99.9% of adult gut bacteria are obligate anaerobes.\[1\]

A crucial, often-misunderstood point: **there is no single defined "healthy" microbiota composition.** The Human Microbiome Project showed that healthy individuals differ enormously in taxonomic composition yet converge on similar *functional* gene profiles — a property called **functional redundancy**.\[1\] Hallmarks of a healthy microbiota are therefore better described as high **diversity**, dominance of obligate anaerobe commensals, and **stability/resilience**, rather than the presence of any universal species. Loss of diversity is a recurring signature of disease (IBD, IBS, frailty).\[3\]

### 1.2 How the microbiota supports normal intestinal function

- **Barrier integrity and the mucus layer.** Commensal colonization induces colonic barrier structure and function — mucus production, antimicrobial peptide secretion (e.g., RegIIIγ), and tight-junction regulation. Microbial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), especially butyrate, strengthen the barrier partly by stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) in colonocytes.
- **Immune education.** Gut microbes drive maturation of both innate and adaptive immunity. Specific commensals — *Bacteroides fragilis* (via polysaccharide A, PSA), *Lactobacillus* spp., and *Clostridia* clusters IV/XIVa — induce Foxp3⁺ **regulatory T cells (Tregs)** and **IL-10**, promote **secretory IgA**, and balance **Th17** responses. SCFAs (acetate, propionate, butyrate) promote colonic Treg homeostasis; acetate promotes IgA responses via GPR43. This tolerogenic "education" restrains pro-inflammatory T-cell responses.
- **Colonization resistance.** Commensals exclude pathogens through nutrient competition, bacteriocins, lowering luminal pH, and maintaining an anaerobic, butyrate-rich environment; depletion of butyrate-producing Clostridia drives an oxygen/nitrate-fueled luminal expansion of pathogens such as *Salmonella* and *E. coli*.
- **Vitamin synthesis and bile-acid metabolism.** Gut bacteria synthesize vitamin K2 (menaquinones) and B vitamins, and transform host primary bile acids into secondary bile acids — a double-edged function (section 4).

### 1.3 Dysbiosis → inflammation → carcinogenesis

**Dysbiosis** is an altered microbiota composition relative to the healthy aggregate\[3\] — frequently accompanied by reduced diversity, loss of SCFA producers, and expansion of pro-inflammatory pathobionts (often Pseudomonadota). Dysbiosis compromises the mucus layer and tight junctions ("leaky gut"), permitting microbial translocation, overproduction of IL-6 and IL-17, and chronic low-grade inflammation\[4\] — the soil for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), colitis, and ultimately colorectal carcinogenesis.

Several complementary, partly competing models explain how microbes drive CRC:

- **The "alpha-bug" (keystone pathogen) hypothesis** (Sears & Pardoll): specific pro-oncogenic bacteria — prototypically enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis* (ETBF) — directly initiate tumorigenesis *and* remodel the surrounding microbial community and mucosal immune response to be pro-carcinogenic, "crowding out" protective bacteria.\[5\] Their effect is disproportionately large relative to their abundance.\[6\]
- **The bacterial "driver–passenger" model** (Tjalsma et al., *Nat Rev Microbiol* 2012): intestinal "driver" bacteria (e.g., ETBF, *pks*⁺ *E. coli*, *Streptococcus gallolyticus*, *Enterococcus faecalis*) initiate mucosal changes and are then progressively replaced by opportunistic "passenger" bacteria (e.g., *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, *Peptostreptococcus*) better adapted to the tumor microenvironment, which sustain progression via metabolites.\[7\] This explains why driver bacteria are not always present in established tumors.\[8\]
- **Biofilm formation.** Polymicrobial mucosal biofilms — especially in the proximal (right) colon — breach the protective mucus barrier, increase permeability, and are associated with carcinogenesis; a biofilm-centered extension of the driver–passenger model has been proposed.\[9\]

Mechanistically, these converge on: **direct genotoxicity** (DNA double-strand breaks and characteristic mutational signatures), **chronic inflammation** via NF-κB and STAT3 signaling, **Wnt/β-catenin activation** (proliferation), **oxidative DNA damage** from reactive oxygen species (ROS), and **immune evasion**. A central caveat throughout: most human microbiome–CRC associations are **correlative**, and tumors themselves alter the local microbial niche, so **direction of causality** is frequently unresolved. The firmest causal evidence comes from mutational-signature and gnotobiotic-mouse studies, discussed below.

## 2. Predominant Types of Gut Probiotics

The ISAPP (International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics) consensus defines a **probiotic** as "live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host"\[10\] (Hill et al., 2014). Benefits are strain-specific; genus or species names alone do not guarantee efficacy.

### 2.1 Lactobacillus and the 2020 reclassification

In April 2020, Zheng et al. (*Int J Syst Evol Microbiol*) split the sprawling genus *Lactobacillus* (then >260 species, genomically more divergent than a typical bacterial family)\[11\] into **25 genera** — the emended *Lactobacillus* plus **23 novel genera** — uniting *Lactobacillaceae* and *Leuconostocaceae* into one family\[12\] of (now) 31 genera.\[10\] The generic terms "lactobacilli"/*Lactobacillus* remain colloquially useful.\[13\] Key renamings of common probiotics:

| Former name | Current name | Notable strain(s) |
|---|---|---|
| *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* | *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* | GG (LGG, ATCC 53103) |
| *Lactobacillus casei* | *Lacticaseibacillus casei* | Shirota; DN-114001 |
| *Lactobacillus plantarum* | *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* | 299v |
| *Lactobacillus reuteri* | *Limosilactobacillus reuteri* | DSM 17938 |
| *Lactobacillus acidophilus* | *Lactobacillus acidophilus* (unchanged) | NCFM; LA-5 |
| *Lactobacillus brevis* | *Levilactobacillus brevis* | — |
| *Lactobacillus salivarius* | *Ligilactobacillus salivarius* | — |
| *Lactobacillus fermentum* | *Limosilactobacillus fermentum* | — |

### 2.2 Other major probiotic taxa

- **Bifidobacterium** (Actinomycetota): *B. longum* (incl. subsp. *infantis*, e.g., EVC001), *B. breve*, *B. bifidum*, and *B. animalis* subsp. *lactis* (BB-12, HN019). Dominant early-life colonizers; HMO-specialists.
- **Saccharomyces boulardii** (CNCM I-745): the principal **yeast** probiotic; intrinsically resistant to antibacterial antibiotics.
- **Spore-formers / Bacillota:** *Bacillus coagulans*, *Bacillus subtilis*; **Enterococcus faecium** SF68; **Streptococcus thermophilus** (common in yogurt starter and multi-strain mixes).
- **Multi-strain formulations:** the **8-strain De Simone formulation** (marketed as VSL#3 and, after reformulation disputes, as Visbiome/Vivomixx): *L. paracasei* subsp. *paracasei* DSM 24733, *L. plantarum* DSM 24730, *L. acidophilus* DSM 24735, *L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* DSM 24734, *B. longum* subsp.\[14\] *longum*, *B. breve*, *B. longum* subsp. *infantis*, and *S. salivarius* subsp. *thermophilus*.

### 2.3 "Next-generation probiotics" / live biotherapeutic products (LBPs)

These are gut commensals, mostly not yet in commerce, under development as live biotherapeutics — largely supported by **preclinical (mouse/in-vitro) evidence**:

- **Akkermansia muciniphila** (Verrucomicrobiota): a mucin-degrading symbiont. Strikingly, **pasteurized** *A. muciniphila* often outperforms live cells; the effect is substantially attributable to **Amuc_1100**, a 34-kDa outer-membrane pili protein that is thermostable through pasteurization and signals via **TLR2** to improve barrier function\[15\] and IL-10. EFSA has approved pasteurized *A. muciniphila* as a novel food.\[16\] In CRC it is described as a "double-edged sword."
- **Faecalibacterium prausnitzii:** a major butyrate producer with anti-inflammatory properties; depleted in IBD and CRC.
- **Clostridium butyricum** MIYAIRI 588, **Roseburia** spp., **Eubacterium hallii / Anaerobutyricum**, **Christensenella**, and **Bacteroides fragilis** ZY-312 / PSA.

### 2.4 Postbiotics, paraprobiotics, synbiotics, and fermented foods (ISAPP definitions)

- **Prebiotic** (2017): "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit."\[10\]
- **Synbiotic** (2020): a mixture of live microbes and selectively utilized substrate(s). **Complementary** synbiotics = a qualifying probiotic + qualifying prebiotic acting independently; **synergistic** synbiotics = substrate designed to be used by the co-administered microbe (components need not individually qualify).\[17\]
- **Postbiotic** (2021): "a preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host."\[18\] Purified metabolites and vaccines are excluded;\[18\] a killed probiotic is not automatically a postbiotic.\[19\]
- **Fermented foods** (2021): "foods made through desired microbial growth and enzymatic conversions of food components"; unlike the "biotics," they need not confer a demonstrated health benefit and may contain no live microbes at consumption.

### 2.5 Mechanisms of probiotic action against inflammation and CRC

SCFA production; barrier reinforcement (tight junctions, mucin); **bacteriocins** and competitive exclusion/pathogen displacement; immunomodulation (↑Treg/IL-10, ↓NF-κB); **apoptosis induction** in tumor cells; reduction of fecal **β-glucuronidase** (which otherwise de-conjugates and reactivates carcinogens); **binding/degradation of carcinogens** (e.g., heterocyclic amines); exopolysaccharide (EPS) production; and emerging **modulation of responses to chemotherapy and immune-checkpoint immunotherapy**. Nearly all anti-CRC mechanisms are demonstrated **in vitro or in mice, not in human trials**.

### 2.6 Strength of clinical evidence (critical appraisal)

The **2020 AGA Clinical Practice Guideline** (Su et al., *Gastroenterology*) is deliberately conservative and found mostly low-to-very-low-quality evidence. Its conditional recommendations:

- **Prevention of *C. difficile* infection in adults/children on antibiotics:** conditional recommendation **for** specific products — *S. boulardii*; or the 2-strain *L. acidophilus* CL1285 + *L. casei* LBC80R; or a 3-strain *L. acidophilus/L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus/B. bifidum*; or a 4-strain combination (low-quality evidence).
- **Pouchitis** (prevention/maintenance, after ileal pouch–anal anastomosis for ulcerative colitis): conditional recommendation **for** the 8-strain\[20\] De Simone formulation (very-low-quality evidence).
- **Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) prevention in preterm (<37 wk), low-birth-weight (<2500 g) infants:** conditional recommendation **for** specific combinations (e.g., *L. rhamnosus* + *B. longum* subsp. *infantis*, or *B. animalis* subsp. *lactis*, or *L. reuteri*)\[21\] — the one indication with moderate-to-high-quality evidence\[22\] for reduced all-cause mortality and severe NEC.
- **Against routine use / clinical-trial-only** for: acute infectious gastroenteritis in children, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, IBS, and treatment of active *C. difficile* infection.\[23\]

Strong meta-analytic support also exists for probiotics in **antibiotic-associated diarrhea** (LGG, *S. boulardii*) and **acute infectious diarrhea** (shortening duration), and perioperative **synbiotics in colorectal surgery** reduce postoperative infectious complications. **For CRC prevention or treatment in humans, evidence is preclinical or weak** — no probiotic is approved to prevent or treat CRC.

**Safety.** Probiotics are generally safe in healthy people but carry real risk in **immunocompromised, critically ill, central-line, or short-gut** patients — including bacteremia/fungemia (*Lactobacillus*, *S. boulardii*). A notorious trial (PROPATRIA, 2008) found **increased mortality** when a multispecies probiotic was given to predicted severe acute pancreatitis. Product quality/labeling is inconsistent. Regulators classify most probiotics as foods/supplements, while defined live strains developed as drugs are regulated as **live biotherapeutic products** (FDA/EMA); the first LBPs (fecal-microbiota products for recurrent *C. difficile*, Rebyota/2022 and Vowst/2023) are approved for that indication, not for CRC.

## 3. Prebiotics — Definition and Mechanism

### 3.1 Definition and criteria

The modern **ISAPP 2017** definition: a prebiotic is "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit"\[10\] (Gibson et al., *Nat Rev Gastroenterol Hepatol*). This broadened the original Gibson & Roberfroid 1995 concept (then focused on bifidogenic non-digestible carbohydrates) to include non-carbohydrate substrates and sites beyond the gut. Classic criteria: (1) **resistance** to gastric acidity, host hydrolysis, and absorption; (2) **fermentation** by intestinal microbiota; and (3) **selective stimulation** of beneficial microbes/activity.\[24\] Effective prebiotic doses span roughly **1.5–30 g/day** and responses are highly individual.

### 3.2 Main classes, food sources, and typical doses

| Prebiotic class | Representative food sources | Typical studied dose |
|---|---|---|
| **Inulin** (long-chain fructan, DP 10–60+) | Chicory root (~41\[25\] g/100 g), Jerusalem artichoke, garlic, onion, asparagus | 5–15 g/day (EU bowel-function claim at 12 g/day) |
| **Fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS)** | Onion,\[25\] garlic, banana, asparagus, leek\[25\] | 5–10 g/day (start 2–3 g) |
| **Galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS)** | Legumes; dairy/human-milk derivatives | ~2.5–15 g/day; bifidogenic even at low capsule doses; better tolerated in\[26\] IBS |
| **Human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs; e.g., 2′-FL)** | Human milk; now synthesized for formula | infant formula fortification |
| **Resistant starch (types 1–5; RS2 raw potato/green banana, RS3 cooked-and-cooled starch)**\[27\] | Cooked-cooled potato/rice/pasta, legumes, green banana, whole grains | 10–40 g/day (prebiotic effects seen even ~3.5 g/day for resistant potato starch) |
| **Beta-glucans** | Oats, barley | ~3 g/day |
| **Arabinoxylan / AXOS; xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS)** | Wheat bran, whole grains | XOS effective ~1.4–2.8\[28\] g/day |
| **Pectin** | Apples, citrus peel | variable |
| **Polydextrose; isomalto-oligosaccharides; lactulose; resistant dextrin** | Supplements/foods; lactulose is a drug | variable |
| **Candidate prebiotics:** polyphenols, conjugated linoleic acid/PUFAs | Berries, tea, cocoa, olive oil; fish | not standardized |

### 3.3 Mechanistic role in detail

**Cross-feeding and SCFA production.** Prebiotic fermentation runs through a trophic chain: **primary degraders** (e.g., *Bifidobacterium*, *Bacteroides*) break down complex substrates, releasing **lactate and acetate**, which **cross-feed butyrate producers** (e.g., *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, *Roseburia*, *Anaerobutyricum/Eubacterium hallii*). Net products are the three major **SCFAs — acetate, propionate, butyrate** (roughly 60:20:20 in the colon). Acetate and propionate are largely absorbed and reach the liver (gluconeogenesis/lipogenesis, appetite signaling); **butyrate** is consumed locally.

**Butyrate as colonocyte fuel and the "butyrate paradox."** Normal colonocytes\[29\] derive ~70% of their energy from butyrate via mitochondrial **β-oxidation**. Cancerous colonocytes, by contrast,\[29\] undergo the **Warburg effect** (aerobic glycolysis, preferring glucose), so they oxidize relatively little butyrate; butyrate therefore **accumulates in the nucleus and acts as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor**, suppressing proliferation and promoting apoptosis in tumor cells while *fueling* normal cells — the **butyrate paradox** (Donohoe et al., *Mol Cell* 2012). Butyrate additionally signals through **GPR41 (FFAR3), GPR43 (FFAR2), and GPR109A (HCAR2)**;\[30\] GPR109A suppresses colonic inflammation and carcinogenesis and is frequently silenced in CRC.

**Downstream effects.** SCFAs/prebiotics induce **Tregs and IL-10**, promote **mucin** production and goblet-cell function, **lower luminal pH** (inhibiting pathogens and secondary-bile-acid-producing bacteria), enhance **mineral absorption** (Ca, Mg), and stimulate **satiety hormones GLP-1 and PYY** (via enteroendocrine FFAR activation).

### 3.4 Synbiotics and the downsides/limits

Synbiotics pair prebiotic + probiotic (complementary or synergistic; §2.4). Limitations are real: fermentable prebiotics are **FODMAPs** and can cause gas, bloating, and pain — problematic in **IBS**; tolerance is dose-dependent (titrate up slowly).\[31\] In suspected **small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)**, fermentable substrates may worsen symptoms. Prebiotic response is strongly **individual**, with person-to-person variation often exceeding differences between prebiotic types.

## 4. Pathogenic/Pathobiont Bacteria of Concern and Their Toxic Metabolites

> **Interpretive caution:** Enrichment of these taxa in CRC is largely **associative**; tumors create niches (e.g., Gal-GalNAc display, altered mucus) that recruit particular bacteria, so association ≠ initiation. The strongest causal threads are colibactin (mutational signatures in human tumors) and gnotobiotic-mouse tumorigenesis for ETBF and *C. difficile*.

### 4.1 Key pathobionts → toxins → mechanisms

| Organism | Key toxin/virulence factor | Mechanism in CRC | Evidence level |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fusobacterium nucleatum** (esp. subsp. *animalis* clade **Fna C2**) | **FadA** adhesin; **Fap2** adhesin | FadA binds E-cadherin → **β-catenin/Wnt + NF-κB**, DNA damage (↑CHK2), proliferation. Fap2 binds tumor **Gal-GalNAc** (colonization) and host **TIGIT** → inhibits NK/T cells (immune evasion); drives chemoresistance (autophagy) and metastasis. 2024 *Nature* (Zepeda-Rivera et al.): only **Fna C2** is enriched in tumors vs paired normal tissue and increased intestinal adenomas in mice. | Strong mechanistic (human tissue + mouse); passenger/driver debated |
| ***pks*⁺ *Escherichia coli*** | **Colibactin** (hybrid NRPS-PKS genotoxin) | Alkylates adenines → **DNA interstrand crosslinks and double-strand breaks**, leaving **SBS88 + ID18** mutational signatures, with APC/driver-gene hotspot mutations. 2025 *Nature* (Díaz-Gay et al., 981 CRC genomes across 11 countries): colibactin exposure is "3.3 times more common in colorectal cancers diagnosed in individuals younger than 40 compared to those over 70" (p-trend = 2.7×10⁻⁷), with SBS88 and ID18 ~2.5× and ~4× more common below vs above age 50 — a leading hypothesis for rising **early-onset CRC**. | Strong (signatures in human tumors); causality still being established |
| **Enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis* (ETBF)** | **B. fragilis toxin (BFT / fragilysin)**, a zinc-metalloprotease\[32\] | Triggers E-cadherin cleavage → **Wnt/β-catenin + NF-κB + IL-8**; **IL-17/Th17–STAT3** pro-carcinogenic inflammation; induces **spermine oxidase (SMO)** → **ROS and γH2AX DNA damage**. **Cytolethal distending toxin (CDT)** deserves a general note:\[42\] produced by *Campylobacter*, some *E. coli*, *Shigella*, *Haemophilus*, *Salmonella* (typhoid toxin) and others, its **CdtB** subunit is a nuclease that\[42\] induces DNA damage and cell-cycle (G2/M) arrest — a shared genotoxic theme across Gram-negative pathobionts.

### 4.2 Microbial metabolites of concern

- **Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)** — produced by sulfate-reducing bacteria (**Bilophila wadsworthia, Desulfovibrio**) and *Fusobacterium*. Genotoxic, degrades the protective mucus layer, impairs butyrate oxidation, and promotes inflammation; boosted by high-animal-protein/high-fat Western diets.
- **Secondary bile acids — deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA).** Gut bacteria perform **7α-dehydroxylation**\[43\] of host cholic/chenodeoxycholic acids via the\[44\] **bile-acid-inducible (*bai*) operon**, carried by\[45\] a narrow clostridial group (prototype ***Clostridium scindens***). The human liver cannot re-hydroxylate these, so DCA/LCA accumulate, generating **ROS and DNA damage**, perturbing **FXR and TGR5** signaling, and promoting proliferation — the heart of the **diet–microbiota–bile-acid axis** linking high-fat Western diets to CRC (DCA\[46\] is elevated in feces of CRC patients and high-risk individuals).
- **N-nitroso compounds (NOCs)** — formed endogenously from\[47\] **heme iron** (red/processed meat) and ingested in cured meats; alkylate DNA (O6-methylguanine), a mechanistic basis for the meat–CRC link.
- **Trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO)** — hepatic oxidation product of microbial trimethylamine from dietary **choline/L-carnitine** (red meat, eggs); pro-inflammatory, associated with CRC and cardiovascular risk.
- **Polyamines** (via SMO), **p-cresol/phenols** and **ammonia** from proteolytic fermentation of tyrosine/aromatic amino acids, **4-hydroxynonenal** (lipid-peroxidation product), and **acetaldehyde** from microbial ethanol metabolism (a Group 1 carcinogen pathway) — all contribute DNA-damaging or proliferative pressure in the distal gut.

Together with **biofilms** and **mucin degradation**, these metabolites explain how diet is transduced into colonic genotoxic/inflammatory exposure.

## 5. Dietary Translation — Optimizing Daily Choices

### 5.1 Fiber: quantity and diversity

- **Targets.** General guidelines recommend **25–38 g/day** (commonly ~25 g for women, ~38 g for men); most people eat far less. Evidence favors **30 g+/day**.
- **Dose–response and CRC.** A landmark systematic review/dose–response meta-analysis (Aune et al., *BMJ* 2011; 16 studies) found "the summary relative risk of developing colorectal cancer for 10 g daily of total dietary fibre was 0.90 (95% confidence interval 0.86 to 0.94, I²=0%)," with **cereal fiber and whole grains** showing the clearest benefit (~20% risk reduction per 90 g/day of whole grain). Later meta-analyses corroborate ~10% reduction per 10 g/day and stronger protection for proximal/distal colon.
- **Diversity.** The **American Gut Project** (McDonald et al., 2018) found that participants eating **>30 different plant types per week** (41 people) had more diverse microbiomes — and carried fewer antibiotic-resistance genes — than those eating **≤10** types (44 people). Prioritize whole grains, legumes/pulses, nuts/seeds, vegetables, fruit, and **resistant starch** (cooled cooked potato/rice/pasta, green banana, legumes).

### 5.2 Fermented foods

The **2021 Stanford trial** (Wastyk, Gardner, Sonnenburg et al., *Cell*) randomized 36 healthy adults (18/arm) for 10 weeks: a **high-fermented-food diet** (yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, vegetable brine drinks, kombucha)\[48\] **increased gut microbiome diversity and decreased 19 of 93 cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory serum proteins** — including IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12b — with effects scaling by servings; a **high-fiber diet**\[49\] maintained stable diversity and did not uniformly lower inflammation (none of the 19 proteins decreased in the high-fiber arm, whose benefits depended on baseline diversity, and it upregulated glycan-degrading CAZymes). **Caveat:** benefits require **live cultures** — many commercial sauerkraut/pickles are pasteurized or vinegar-brined (not fermented), and most kombucha/sourdough breads contribute little live-microbe dose. Natto, miso, tempeh, kefir, and live-culture yogurt/kimchi are reliable sources.

### 5.3 Polyphenols, healthy fats, and plant diversity

Polyphenol-rich foods (berries, green tea, cocoa, **extra-virgin olive oil**, nuts, coffee) act as **candidate prebiotics** and feed beneficial taxa (e.g., *Akkermansia*). **Omega-3** PUFAs (oily fish) are anti-inflammatory and favorably shift the microbiota.

### 5.4 Red and processed meat

- **IARC 2015:**\[50\] **processed meat = Group 1** ("carcinogenic to humans," principally CRC); **red meat = Group 2A** ("probably carcinogenic"). Each **50 g/day of processed meat** raises CRC risk ~**18%**; each **100 g/day of red meat** ~**17%**.
- **WCRF/AICR:**\[47\] eat **little, if any, processed meat**, and limit red meat to **≤3 portions/week (~350–500 g cooked)**. Mechanisms: **heme iron → N-nitroso compounds**, and **HCAs/PAHs** from high-temperature cooking (grilling, frying).

### 5.5 Western diet, emulsifiers, sweeteners, ultra-processed foods

- **Emulsifiers.** A randomized controlled-feeding trial (Chassaing et al., *Gastroenterology* 2022) showed dietary **carboxymethylcellulose (CMC, E466)** altered the gut microbiota and metabolome and reduced microbial diversity/encroached on the mucus layer in healthy humans; CMC and **polysorbate-80 (P80, E433)** promote inflammation in mechanistic/mouse models.\[51\] Prudent to limit ultra-processed foods containing these additives.
- **Non-nutritive sweeteners.** A randomized controlled trial (Suez, Segal, Elinav et al., *Cell* 2022) in\[52\] 120 NNS-naïve adults found\[53\] **saccharin and sucralose significantly impaired glucose tolerance** over 2 weeks, with\[54\] microbiome and plasma-metabolome shifts; fecal transplants into germ-free mice **transferred the glycemic effect**, establishing causality. Aspartame and stevia altered the microbiome/metabolome but did not significantly impair glycemia in this cohort.\[53\] Responses were highly personalized.
- **Ultra-processed foods (UPFs)** and **sugar-sweetened beverages** are associated in cohorts with higher CRC risk, and SSBs with rising **early-onset CRC**.

### 5.6 Alcohol, calcium/vitamin D, dairy, folate

- **Alcohol** is a CRC risk factor; microbial/host **acetaldehyde** (IARC Group 1 pathway) is genotoxic. Limit or avoid.
- **Calcium, vitamin D, dairy, magnesium** are associated with modestly lower CRC risk (calcium binds colonic secondary bile acids and free fatty acids).
- **Folate** has a **dual/timing effect**: adequate intake is protective, but very high supplemental doses in the presence of existing neoplasia may be unhelpful — favor food folate.

### 5.7 Dietary patterns with the best evidence

The **Mediterranean diet** (high in plants, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, fish; low in red/processed meat) has the strongest overall evidence for lower CRC risk and favorable microbiome/inflammatory profiles. **Plant-forward/flexitarian** and **DASH** patterns perform similarly. Evidence for **time-restricted eating/meal timing** on the human microbiome is preliminary.

### 5.8 Non-dietary microbiota modifiers

Practice **antibiotic stewardship** (avoid unnecessary courses; each course transiently reduces diversity); be aware **proton-pump inhibitors** alter the microbiome and raise enteric-infection risk; **regular exercise**, adequate **sleep**, and **stress** management favorably influence microbial diversity and barrier function.

### 5.9 What NOT to over-promise

- **Commercial probiotic supplements:** benefits are **strain- and indication-specific**; most marketed products lack evidence for general "gut health," and routine use in healthy people is not supported by the AGA. Choose products with defined strains and human trials for your specific indication.
- **Direct-to-consumer microbiome testing kits:** currently **not clinically validated** for diagnosis or personalized diet; results are unstable and poorly reproducible.
- **Individual variability/personalized nutrition:** the **PREDICT** studies and Zeevi/Segal (*Cell* 2015) work show glycemic and microbiome responses to identical foods differ markedly between people — a frontier, not yet a finished clinical tool.
- **When to see a clinician / screening:** Start **average-risk CRC screening at age 45** (**USPSTF 2021 B recommendation**; aligned with **ACS 2018**), continuing to 75 with individualized decisions to 85. Seek prompt evaluation for **alarm symptoms** — rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anemia, unexplained weight loss, a persistent change in bowel habit, or abdominal pain — **regardless of age**, given the documented **rise in early-onset CRC** (incidence has increased most sharply in adults 45–49, and early-onset cases now prompt the colibactin/early-life-exposure hypotheses discussed above).

---

## 6. Practical Summary — What to Eat / What to Limit

**Eat more (daily, for a healthy microbiota and lower CRC risk):**
- **30+ g of diverse fiber** from whole grains, legumes/pulses, vegetables, fruit, nuts/seeds; aim for **30+ different plants per week**.
- **Resistant starch**: cooked-and-cooled potatoes/rice/pasta, green banana, legumes, oats/barley (beta-glucans).
- **Live-culture fermented foods**: yogurt/kefir with live cultures, kimchi, unpasteurized sauerkraut, miso, natto, tempeh (several servings/day as tolerated).
- **Polyphenol/omega-3 sources**: extra-virgin olive oil, berries, green tea, coffee, cocoa, nuts, oily fish.
- Overall pattern: **Mediterranean / plant-forward**.

**Limit or avoid:**
- **Processed meat** (bacon, ham, sausages, deli meats) — avoid; **red meat** — ≤350–500 g cooked/week; minimize charring/high-temperature cooking.
- **Ultra-processed foods** and additives — especially emulsifiers **CMC** and **polysorbate-80**; **sugar-sweetened beverages**.
- **Non-nutritive sweeteners** — moderate, particularly **saccharin and sucralose**.
- **Alcohol** — limit or avoid.

**Also:** titrate fiber/prebiotics up slowly (especially with IBS); use antibiotics and PPIs only when indicated; exercise, sleep, and manage stress; **begin CRC screening at 45** and act on alarm symptoms at any age. Don't rely on probiotic supplements or microbiome test kits as substitutes for diet and screening.

---

### Key caveats and evidence grading (summary)

- **Established (human, high confidence):** fiber/whole-grain dose–response reduces CRC risk; processed/red meat increase CRC risk (IARC); probiotics help prevent AAD/*C. difficile* diarrhea, pouchitis, and NEC; colibactin leaves SBS88/ID18 signatures enriched in early-onset CRC.
- **Probable/mechanistically strong but not definitively causal in humans:** *Fusobacterium* Fna C2, ETBF/BFT, secondary bile acids, H₂S, and TMAO in CRC; fermented foods lowering inflammation (one RCT).
- **Preclinical or weak:** direct CRC prevention/treatment by probiotics; next-generation probiotics (*Akkermansia*, *F. prausnitzii*); most "candidate" prebiotic and microbiome-personalization claims.
- **Direction-of-causality** remains the central unresolved problem for most microbiome–CRC associations: tumors reshape their local microbial niche, so enrichment of a taxon in cancer tissue does not prove it initiated the cancer.
